🔍 Structured Paragraphs
2
Write a structured paragraph explaining how the promise to Abraham (Genesis 12) is finally fulfilled in the book of Joshua — a gap of many generations. Use specific evidence from both stories to show the connection clearly. 5 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 5 Bible — God's promise to Abraham of land, descendants, and blessing.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The book of Joshua represents the direct fulfilment of a promise God made to Abraham many centuries earlier. In Genesis 12:1-7, God promises Abraham that his descendants will become a great nation and will receive a specific land — but at the time of the promise, Abraham had no children and no ownership of the land at all, only a word from God. Generations later, after Abraham's descendants grew into the twelve tribes of Israel, endured slavery in Egypt, and were led out by Moses, Joshua leads them into the very land God first promised — the book of Joshua describes the actual, physical possession of Canaan, tribe by tribe (Joshua 13-21 divides the land among Israel's twelve tribes). This shows that God's promises operate across a timescale far longer than any individual human life — Abraham himself died without personally seeing this fulfilment, yet the promise was never abandoned or forgotten. The significance is profound: it demonstrates that biblical faith is not measured by seeing every promise fulfilled within one's own lifetime, but by trusting that God's word remains true across generations, even when fulfilment seems impossibly distant from the original promise.
3
Write a paragraph comparing the ROLE of obedience in Joshua's story with the role of obedience in Moses' story (Week 7). Is obedience presented the same way in both stories, or differently? Use the lesson's phrase "the pattern deepens" if it applies. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Obedience plays a central role in both Moses' and Joshua's stories, but the pattern deepens in an important way between the two. In Moses' story, obedience is largely about CONFRONTATION — Moses must obey God by repeatedly confronting Pharaoh, the most powerful ruler in the ancient world, demanding freedom for an enslaved people, despite Moses' own self-doubt about his ability to speak persuasively (Exodus 4:10). The obedience required is primarily about courage to SPEAK and CONFRONT authority. In Joshua's story, obedience shifts toward precise, sometimes strange ACTION — marching around Jericho in a specific pattern, stepping into a flooded river before it parts, following exact instructions with no room for improvisation. Where Moses' obedience is fundamentally about confrontation and advocacy, Joshua's obedience is about disciplined, exact execution of unusual instructions, even when the immediate logic is unclear. The pattern deepens because it moves from single dramatic acts of confrontation (Moses before Pharaoh) toward sustained, detailed, communal obedience across an entire military campaign (Joshua leading the whole nation through multiple precise rituals and battles) — showing that biblical obedience is not one uniform kind of action but takes different forms depending on what the specific moment and calling requires.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Joshua's story can be reasonably described as completing a multi-generational arc of faith that began with Abraham — and the claim that fulfilment requires just as much faith as waiting is strongly supported by the text. Three specific episodes demonstrate this. First, the repeated commissioning of Joshua (Joshua 1): God commands Joshua to be "strong and courageous" four times across a single chapter, revealing that even standing at the very threshold of a promise already clearly defined required significant courage to actually step forward and lead — the promise being close and specific did not make it easy, only clear. Second, the crossing of the Jordan (Joshua 3): the priests had to physically step into a flooded river, in faith, BEFORE the water stopped — this is not passive waiting for a miracle but active faith expressed through movement into apparent danger. Third, the fall of Jericho (Joshua 6): Israel had to obey a strange, seemingly illogical set of instructions with no natural connection between marching/shouting and walls collapsing — sustained obedience for seven full days without any visible progress until the final moment, testing endurance as much as initial courage. Compared with Abraham, whose faith was tested by uncertainty about an unknown destination over decades, and Moses, whose faith was tested by confrontation with Pharaoh, Joshua's faith was tested by the challenge of ACTIVELY RECEIVING what had already been promised and clearly identified — arguably a different, but not lesser, kind of faith. This suggests that faith is not simply about waiting patiently for an unclear promise; it is equally required in the concrete, risky, sustained obedience needed to actually walk into and take hold of a promise once it has become available. For this reason, the claim is well supported: Joshua's story shows that the final step toward a promise's fulfilment can require faith just as costly and just as tested as the faith that first waited for the promise to be given.

🏆 Multi-Step Real-World Reasoning
4
A medieval lord divides his land into plots for four vassals. The first vassal receives x hectares. The second vassal receives 3 hectares more than the first. The third vassal receives twice as much as the first. The fourth vassal receives 5 hectares less than the first. Combined, all four vassals receive 90 hectares.

(a) Write expressions for the second, third, and fourth vassals in terms of x.
(b) Write and simplify an equation for the total.
(c) Solve for x.
(d) Find how much land each vassal receives.
(e) Check your answer by adding all four amounts. 10 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) First = x. Second = x+3. Third = 2x. Fourth = x−5. (b) Equation: x + (x+3) + 2x + (x−5) = 90, which simplifies to 5x−2=90. (c) 5x=92. x=92÷5=18.4 hectares. (d) First = 18.4. Second = 18.4+3=21.4. Third = 2(18.4)=36.8. Fourth = 18.4−5=13.4. (e) Check: 18.4+21.4+36.8+13.4=90 ✓. (Note: this shows equations don't always give whole-number answers — a valuable lesson that real-world problems don't always produce neat numbers.)

Without notes: draw or describe the complete feudal pyramid with all four levels, state what EACH level gave and received, and explain the role of the Church as a separate power structure alongside it. 10 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Feudal pyramid: 1. Monarch — owned all land in theory; granted land to lords in exchange for loyalty and military support. 2. Lords/Nobles — received large estates from the monarch; gave loyalty to the king and granted land to knights in exchange for military service. 3. Knights — received land from lords in exchange for a set number of days of military service each year (often 40 days); provided protection over the serfs on their land. 4. The Church's role: The Church operated as a separate but parallel power structure — owning vast amounts of land independently of the king's feudal system, collecting tithes (a tenth of income) from the entire population, and holding significant political power through its ability to crown or excommunicate monarchs, combining religious authority with substantial economic and political influence that sometimes rivalled royal power.

Feudal Europe: Top position = the monarch, whose authority came from a combination of military power, inherited royal bloodline, and often claimed divine right or Church blessing. Similarity: Both systems were rigidly hierarchical, with clear obligations flowing between levels — those at lower levels provided labour/service, while those above provided protection, land, or organisation, and social mobility between levels was extremely limited in both societies. Difference: Egyptian authority was fundamentally RELIGIOUS at its core — the pharaoh was literally worshipped as divine — while feudal European authority, though connected to the Church, maintained a clearer separation between the monarch's temporal (worldly) power and the Church's spiritual power, creating two competing power centres rather than one unified religious-political authority as in Egypt.
3
Connect this week's History to this week's Science: the lesson notes that castles used natural GEOGRAPHY (hills, rivers) for defence. Using the idea of "elements combining to form compounds with new properties" (Science this week), can you draw an analogy between how INDIVIDUAL castle defensive features (walls, moat, keep) combine to create an overall defensive SYSTEM that is stronger than any single feature alone? 💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Just as sodium and chlorine combine to form a compound (table salt) with entirely NEW properties that neither element has alone, a castle's individual defensive features — walls, moat, keep, battlements — combine to create an overall defensive SYSTEM whose effectiveness is greater than the sum of its individual parts. A moat alone could be crossed with effort (boats, filling it in); thick walls alone could eventually be breached with enough time and siege equipment; a keep alone offers no outer protection. The "new property" that emerges from this combination is a defensive system capable of withstanding a prolonged siege — something no single feature could achieve alone, much as combining sodium and chlorine creates the entirely new property of edibility and stability that neither dangerous element possesses independently. This shows a similar underlying principle across totally different subjects: combining distinct components according to a deliberate structure can create emergent properties — new capabilities — that did not exist in any single component alone.
