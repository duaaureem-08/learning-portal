📖 Bible · Week 5 · Day 2
Abraham —
Faith Under Pressure
The promise tested · Hagar & Ishmael · God's patience · Abraham's failures
Day 23 Difficulty LevelsKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Recall and vocabulary. Use your notes if needed.
📚 Recall & Vocabulary
💡 ESL Tip: Intercede means to speak or plead on behalf of someone else. Abraham interceded for Sodom — he asked God to spare the city. This is similar to prayer, but specifically on behalf of other people.
1
What new names did God give Abram and Sarai, and what do the name changes signify? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Abram became Abraham — meaning "father of many nations." Sarai became Sarah — meaning "princess" or "mother of nations." The name changes are significant because in biblical culture, a name change signals a transformed identity and a new God-given purpose. God was not simply renaming them — he was declaring what they would become, even though they were old and childless at the time. The new names were acts of faith — God speaking the future into existence.
2
Who was Hagar and what happened to her? Give at least THREE key facts. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Hagar was Sarah's Egyptian servant, given to Abraham to bear a child on Sarah's behalf — a common cultural practice at the time. 2. She became pregnant with Ishmael, which caused conflict with Sarah; she was treated harshly and fled into the desert. 3. God appeared to Hagar in the wilderness and promised that Ishmael would also father a great nation — making Hagar one of the few people in the Bible who gave God a name: El Roi, "the God who sees me."
3
How many years passed between God's initial promise to Abraham and the birth of Isaac? Why is this significant? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Approximately 25 years passed between God's initial call and promise (when Abraham was 75) and the birth of Isaac (when Abraham was 100 and Sarah was 90). This is significant because it shows that God's promises are not always fulfilled quickly — they require patience and continued trust over a long period. Both Abraham and Sarah had long passed the normal age of childbearing, making the birth of Isaac genuinely miraculous and showing that the promise was fulfilled by God's power, not human ability.
Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Understanding and explanation questions.
🔍 Understanding & Explanation
4
Abraham laughed when God told him Sarah would have a son (Genesis 17:17), and Sarah also laughed (Genesis 18:12). What does this reveal about the nature of their faith? Does laughter necessarily mean unbelief? Use evidence from the lesson. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Their laughter reveals that faith is not always serene confidence — sometimes it includes genuine human shock and disbelief at how impossible a promise seems. Abraham was 100; Sarah was 90 — their reaction was natural and deeply human. However, laughter does not necessarily mean unbelief — Abraham is still described in Hebrews 11 as a hero of faith, and Genesis says "he did not waver through unbelief." Their laughter was a response to the impossibility of the situation, not a rejection of God. The name Isaac itself means "he laughs" — God chose to name the miracle-child after this moment of doubt, turning human weakness into part of the story rather than erasing it.
5
Abraham made serious moral failures during his journey of faith (e.g. twice pretending Sarah was his sister to protect himself). What does the fact that God continued working with Abraham despite these failures tell us about the nature of the covenant? 4 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 4 Bible — Noah's covenant was also one-sided — God made and kept the promise even though humanity was described as sinful.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Abraham's failures show that the covenant was not conditional on Abraham being perfect. God did not withdraw his promise when Abraham lied or when he fathered Ishmael with Hagar in an attempt to fulfil the promise himself rather than waiting for God. This tells us the covenant was God's initiative and God's commitment — it was not held together by Abraham's moral performance. This connects directly to Week 4: Noah's covenant was also made by God unilaterally and was maintained despite humanity's ongoing sinfulness. In both cases, the Bible presents a God whose faithfulness is not undermined by human failure — which is a central theme of both covenants.
6
When Abraham interceded for Sodom (Genesis 18), he negotiated with God — asking if God would spare the city for 50 righteous people, then 45, 40, 30, 20, 10. What does this scene reveal about the kind of relationship God had with Abraham? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
This scene is remarkable because it shows a relationship of genuine dialogue — Abraham was not simply receiving orders from a distant God, but engaging in a real conversation and even pressing God with further requests. God allowed — and even invited — this kind of bold engagement. It reveals that Abraham had grown into a relationship where God could be approached, questioned, and reasoned with. This is described later in the Bible as Abraham being called "God's friend" (James 2:23). The scene shows that biblical faith is not passive submission but active, trusting engagement.
Section C
🔴 HardSection C — Extended analysis and evaluation.
🏆 Analysis & Evaluation
7
The lesson presents Abraham as both a "great man of faith" AND a deeply flawed human being. Is this a contradiction? Write a sustained argument (at least 6 sentences) evaluating whether someone can be a person of great faith while also making serious moral mistakes. Use evidence from Abraham's story and from Week 4 (Noah) to support your case. 6 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 4 Bible — Noah also had a moral failure after the flood (Genesis 9:20-21).
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
This is not a contradiction — it is one of the most consistent patterns in the Bible. Abraham lied twice to protect himself, attempted to solve God's promise through his own means with Hagar, and showed moments of doubt and laughter. Yet Hebrews 11 holds him up as one of the greatest examples of faith in all of Scripture. This suggests that the Bible's definition of faith is not moral perfection but a sustained orientation of trust toward God across a lifetime, even when that trust is imperfect and interrupted by failure. Noah provides an identical pattern: he trusted God completely in building the ark, yet after the flood he became drunk and acted dishonourably. Neither man's failure erased their faith — and neither was excused from the consequences of their actions. The pattern suggests that faith and moral failure can coexist in the same person at different moments, and that the biblical narrative is honest about this rather than presenting sanitised heroes. What made Abraham great was not that he never failed, but that he kept returning to God, kept listening, and never permanently abandoned the relationship — and God kept working with him despite the failures.
8
Hagar is sometimes called "the forgotten figure" of the Abraham story. Based on what the lesson teaches about her, make a case for why her story matters and what it adds to our understanding of God's character in Genesis. Write at least 5 sentences. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Hagar's story adds an essential and often overlooked dimension to Genesis. She had no choice in any of this — she was a servant, given to Abraham by Sarah, with no power over her own situation. Yet when she fled into the desert, God found her and spoke to her — making her one of the first people in the Bible to receive a direct divine promise. She also gave God a name — El Roi, "the God who sees me" — which is theologically profound: God is not just the God of powerful patriarchs but sees and responds to the powerless and the forgotten. Later, when she and Ishmael were sent away and nearly died of thirst, God intervened again — providing water and repeating the promise that Ishmael would become a great nation. Her story shows that God's concern extends beyond the main covenant line to people who are on the margins. Far from being irrelevant, Hagar's story challenges any reading of Genesis that makes it purely about one family, reminding the reader that God's compassion is wider than any single covenant.

Day 23 Difficulty LevelsKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Vocabulary and identification of structural techniques.
📚 Structure & Voice Vocabulary
💡 ESL Tip: A hook is the very first sentence of a piece of writing — its job is to catch the reader's attention and make them want to read more. Think of it like a fishing hook: it grabs you and pulls you in. Good hooks are surprising, dramatic, sensory, or raise an immediate question.
1
Rank these five opening hooks from most effective (1) to least effective (5) for a descriptive piece. For each, give a ONE-sentence reason. 5 marks

a) "It was a dark and stormy night."
b) "Nobody told me the desert could be cold."
c) "This essay will describe a market."
d) "The smell hit her before the noise did."
e) "She ran. She had to."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Suggested ranking (accept well-reasoned alternatives): 1st — (d) "The smell hit her before the noise did" — immediately drops the reader into a sensory moment, creates intrigue, and uses an unexpected order (smell before noise). 2nd — (e) "She ran. She had to." — two short sentences create instant tension and urgency; the fragment "She had to" makes the reader want to know why. 3rd — (b) "Nobody told me the desert could be cold." — a surprising, specific detail that raises an immediate question; the personal voice draws the reader in. 4th — (a) "It was a dark and stormy night" — a cliché; overused to the point where it signals unoriginal writing rather than creating atmosphere. 5th — (c) "This essay will describe a market" — no hook at all; announces the topic rather than showing it; treats the reader as an examiner, not an audience.
2
Explain the effect of varying sentence length in descriptive writing. Then rewrite this passage improving it by deliberately varying the sentence lengths: 5 marks

"The market was busy. There were many people there. The stalls had lots of different foods. It smelled nice. She enjoyed walking around it."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Effect: Short sentences create pace, tension, and emphasis — they make the reader stop and notice. Long sentences slow the pace, allowing the reader to linger in a scene and absorb detail. Varying between the two creates rhythm that keeps the reader engaged and can mirror the experience being described (rushed moments → short sentences; peaceful detail → long flowing sentences). Rewritten example: "The market roared. Vendors shouted prices over each other's heads, and the air was thick with the smell of grilling meat, coriander, and diesel from the generators humming at the edge of the square. She stopped. Breathed in. Somewhere behind the spice stall, someone was frying something sweet — and she followed the smell before she had even decided to."

4
The lesson teaches that a strong descriptive paragraph has: (1) an opening that establishes the focus, (2) sensory and figurative detail, (3) a shift or turn, (4) a closing line that resonates. Write a full descriptive paragraph about an empty house using this four-part structure. Label each part in brackets. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
[Opening — establish focus] "The house had not been lived in for three years, and it showed." [Sensory/figurative detail] "The hallway smelled of damp paper and something else — something warmer and older, like a memory of cooking. 5
The lesson says "a writer's voice is the combination of word choices, sentence rhythm, and attitude that makes their writing sound like them." Read these two passages describing the same thing and explain what is different about the writers' voices. What specific word choices, sentence patterns, or attitudes create those differences? 4 marks

Passage A: "The train was late. Of course it was. She stood on the platform, bag at her feet, watching the board update, reset, update again. Fine. She could wait. She had been waiting for longer things."

Passage B: "She arrived at the platform at 14:07 to find the 14:12 service had been delayed. She waited for twenty-three minutes, during which time the departure board updated four times."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Passage A: The voice is dry, wry, and deeply personal — "Of course it was" signals world-weary resignation. The fragments ("Fine.") convey controlled frustration. The final line — "She had been waiting for longer things" — gives us a whole emotional backstory in one sentence. The rhythm mimics thought patterns; it feels lived-in and individual. Passage B: The voice is detached, clinical, and reportorial. Precise times (14:07, 14:12), the passive construction "had been delayed," and counting the board updates (four times) suggest someone observing rather than experiencing. There is no emotional register at all — we learn facts, not feelings. Key difference: Passage A uses selective, emotionally charged detail and fragmented rhythm to create a distinctive, intimate voice. Passage B presents information neutrally. Both describe the same situation — the voice is everything.

A tank is 3/5 full. 120 litres are added and it is now 9/10 full. What is the total capacity of the tank in litres? Show all working. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The increase in fraction = 9/10 − 3/5 = 9/10 − 6/10 = 3/10. So 3/10 of the total capacity = 120 litres. Total capacity = 120 ÷ 3 × 10 = 40 × 10 = 400 litres.

In a fraction sum, the numerator is 4 less than the denominator. The fraction is equivalent to 3/7. Find the fraction. Show your algebraic working. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Let denominator = n, numerator = n−4. Fraction = (n−4)/n = 3/7. Cross-multiply: 7(n−4) = 3n → 7n−28 = 3n → 4n = 28 → n = 7. So denominator = 7, numerator = 3. The fraction is 3/7. Check: numerator (3) is 4 less than denominator (7) ✓ and 3/7 = 3/7 ✓
8
Explain in words what it means to find "3/4 of 2/3." Draw a rectangle model if it helps, and write the answer. Then connect this to what you learned about percentage of amounts in earlier weeks — how is "3/4 of 2/3" similar to finding a percentage of a percentage? 3 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 3 Maths — percentages of amounts, percentage increase/decrease.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"3/4 of 2/3" means: take 2/3 of something, then take 3/4 of THAT result. 3/4 × 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2. Day 23 Difficulty LevelsKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Core recall about photosynthesis experiments.
📚 Experiments & Evidence
💡 ESL Tip: Iodine solution turns from brown/orange to blue-black in the presence of starch. This is called a positive result — the colour change shows starch IS present. If no starch is there, iodine stays brown (negative result). This test is how we know photosynthesis has produced glucose (which is then stored as starch).
1
Describe the steps to test a leaf for starch. Include the purpose of each step. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Step 1: Boil the leaf in water for 1–2 minutes — this kills the cells and breaks down the cell membranes so chemicals can enter. Step 2: Boil the leaf in ethanol (using a water bath, never directly on a flame) — this removes the green chlorophyll so the colour change from iodine can be seen clearly. Step 3: Rinse the leaf in cold water — removes the ethanol and softens the leaf. Step 4: Spread the leaf flat and add iodine solution — if starch is present, the leaf turns blue-black; if no starch, it remains brown/yellow.
2
What is a variegated leaf, and why is it useful for investigating photosynthesis? What result would you expect when testing a variegated leaf for starch, and why? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A variegated leaf has patches of green and white (or yellow) — the white/yellow areas lack chlorophyll. This is useful because it is a natural control experiment: the same leaf, under the same conditions, with and without chlorophyll. The expected result: after testing with iodine, the green areas (which have chlorophyll) will turn blue-black (starch present — photosynthesis occurred). The white/yellow areas will remain brown (no starch — no photosynthesis, because there is no chlorophyll to absorb light). This proves that chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis.
Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Water transport and transpiration.
🔍 Water Transport & Transpiration
3
Explain how water travels from the soil to the leaves of a plant. Name the tissue involved and describe the driving force that pulls water upwards. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Xylem is the tissue that carries water upwards through the plant. The route: water is absorbed by root hair cells by osmosis (from areas of high water concentration in the soil to areas of lower concentration in the root cells). It then enters the xylem vessels and is transported up through the stem to the leaves. The driving force is transpiration — water evaporates from the cells of the leaf and exits through the stomata. This evaporation creates a tension (like sucking on a straw) that pulls the water column upwards through the xylem. This is called the transpiration stream.
4
Name FOUR factors that increase the rate of transpiration and explain how each one works. 4 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 5 Science Day 1 — limiting factors. The factors affecting transpiration are similar in concept — each one affects the rate at which water leaves the leaf.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Higher temperature — water molecules have more kinetic energy and evaporate faster from leaf cells. 2. Higher light intensity — stomata open wider in light (to allow CO₂ in for photosynthesis), so more water vapour escapes. 3. Increased air movement/wind — removes humid air from around the leaf surface, maintaining a steep concentration gradient so water continues to evaporate rapidly. 4. Lower humidity — if the air around the leaf is dry, the concentration gradient for water vapour between the leaf interior (humid) and outside (dry) is steeper, so water exits more quickly.
5
Describe an experiment using a potometer to measure the rate of transpiration. What variable would you change, and what would you measure? What safety precautions are needed? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A potometer measures the rate of water uptake by a cut shoot (used as an indirect measure of transpiration rate). Method: Cut a leafy shoot underwater (to prevent air bubbles entering the xylem). Attach it to the potometer tube filled with water. Place an air bubble at the start of the scale. Measure how far the bubble moves per unit of time — the faster it moves, the faster water is being taken up. Independent variable (what you change): e.g. wind speed (use a fan), light intensity (use a lamp at different distances), or temperature. Keep all other variables constant. Dependent variable: distance the bubble moves per minute. Safety: Take care with scissors when cutting the shoot; handle the apparatus carefully as the tubes contain water; if using a lamp, ensure it does not overheat near the plant.
Section C
🔴 HardSection C — Experimental design, analysis, and evaluation.
🏆 Experimental Thinking
6
A student investigates whether light is needed for photosynthesis by covering part of a leaf with foil for 48 hours, then testing for starch. (a) Predict the result. (b) Explain WHY this result occurs at a cellular level. (c) Suggest ONE improvement to make this a more controlled experiment, and explain why it would improve validity. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Predicted result: The covered area (blocked from light) will remain brown/yellow when iodine is applied — no starch. The uncovered area will turn blue-black — starch present. b) Cellular explanation: The covered cells cannot absorb light — without light energy, chlorophyll in the chloroplasts cannot drive the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis. Without photosynthesis, no glucose is produced, and therefore no starch is formed or stored in those cells. The uncovered cells have access to light, so photosynthesis proceeds normally and starch accumulates. c) Improvement: "De-starch" the leaf first by keeping the plant in darkness for 24–48 hours before the experiment begins. This removes any starch already stored in the leaf, ensuring that any starch found at the end of the experiment was produced DURING the investigation, not before it. Without de-starching, a positive result in the uncovered area could be starch that was already there — it would not prove that light caused new photosynthesis.
7
"Plants and animals are more similar than different at the cellular level." Agree or disagree? Use specific scientific evidence about cell structures, processes (photosynthesis vs respiration), and the molecules involved. Write at least 6 sentences. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
There is strong evidence for both sides of this argument. Similarities: Both plant and animal cells have a nucleus (containing DNA), a cell membrane, mitochondria (for respiration), ribosomes (for protein synthesis), and cytoplasm. Both carry out aerobic respiration using the same equation: glucose + oxygen → CO₂ + water + energy — meaning at a molecular level, both are burning glucose for energy using the same process. Both also carry out DNA replication and cell division. Differences: Plant cells have three structures absent in animal cells — a rigid cell wall (cellulose), chloroplasts, and a permanent vacuole. The presence of chloroplasts gives plants a fundamentally different capability: photosynthesis — the ability to make their own food. This makes them autotrophs, while animals are heterotrophs. The molecules of life (glucose, DNA, proteins) are the same in both; the equipment used to manage and produce them differs. Overall, plants and animals share the same molecular foundation but have evolved significant structural differences that reflect their completely different relationships with energy in the ecosystem. The statement is partially true but the differences are profound.

🟢 EasySection A — Geographic reading skills. Use your lesson notes if needed.
📚 Reading Geographic Tools
💡 ESL Tip: Contour lines on a map connect points of equal height. Lines that are close together = steep slope (height changes quickly). Lines that are far apart = gentle slope (height changes slowly). The height number on the line tells you the altitude in metres.
1
What do contour lines show on a topographic map? Explain what it means when contour lines are: (a) very close together, (b) very far apart, (c) forming a closed circle. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Contour lines show lines of equal elevation (height above sea level). a) Close together = a steep slope — the height is changing rapidly over a short horizontal distance. b) Far apart = a gentle slope or flat land — the height is barely changing. c) Closed circle = a summit, hill, or peak — the innermost circle marks the highest point.
2
Name the SIX major climate zones of the world. For each, give ONE geographical fact: where it is typically found (latitude or region) and ONE characteristic of its climate. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Tropical — near the Equator (0°–15°N/S) — hot and wet year-round, high biodiversity. 2. Arid/Desert — ~15–30°N/S (e.g. Sahara) — very little rainfall, extreme temperature swings between day and night. 3. Mediterranean — ~30–45°N/S (e.g. southern Europe, California) — hot dry summers, warm wet winters. 4. Temperate — ~45–60°N/S (e.g. UK, northern Europe) — four distinct seasons, moderate rainfall year-round. 5. Continental — inland areas of large landmasses (e.g. central Russia, central USA) — very cold winters, warm summers, low humidity. 6. Polar/Tundra — near the poles (above ~65°N/S) — extremely cold, very little precipitation, permafrost.
Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Applying geographic tools to real contexts.
🔍 Applying Geographic Knowledge
3
A geographer is looking at a map of a river valley. She notices the contour lines form a V-shape pointing uphill. What does this tell her about the shape of the valley and the direction the river flows? Explain with as much geographical detail as possible. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A V-shape in contour lines that points uphill (toward higher ground) indicates a river valley. The river runs along the bottom of the V, and the direction the V-shape points tells you which direction is upstream — the point of the V always points toward higher ground, so the river flows away from the direction the V is pointing (i.e. downhill, toward lower contour numbers). The steep sides of the V indicate the valley walls are relatively steep compared to a wide, flat-bottomed valley. Young rivers (in mountainous areas) typically cut steep, narrow V-shaped valleys; older rivers in their lower course typically create wide U-shaped or flat-floored valleys.

Pakistan spans multiple climate zones. Using your knowledge of latitude, physical features, and climate zones, describe the different climate conditions you might find across Pakistan and explain why they differ. 5 marks

Pakistan sits between approximately 24°N and 37°N latitude, and spans a remarkable range of physical environments. The southern coastal areas around Karachi experience a hot, arid climate — influenced by the Thar Desert to the east and the Arabian Sea to the south; rainfall is low and temperatures high. Moving north, the Punjab and Sindh plains experience a semi-arid to continental climate with very hot summers and cooler winters, heavily influenced by the monsoon which brings significant rainfall between July and September. The northern highlands — including the Karakoram, Hindu Kush, and Himalayan ranges — experience a mountain/alpine climate: cold, snowy winters, cool summers, and in some areas some of the world's largest glaciers outside the poles. The Karakoram Highway passes through terrain that shifts from desert to high-altitude glacial zones within a few hundred kilometres. This diversity is explained by latitude (further north = cooler), altitude (higher = colder), distance from the sea (drier inland), and seasonal monsoon patterns that bring moisture from the Indian Ocean.

"Geography is destiny." This means that the physical geography of a region often determines what kind of civilisation or society develops there. Evaluate this claim using TWO specific historical or modern examples. For each, explain how geography either enabled or limited what humans could achieve. Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 4 History — Roman roads and expansion · Week 3 History — Athens and its port at Piraeus · Week 1–2 — Mesopotamia between the two rivers.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Example 1 — Mesopotamia (Weeks 1–2): "Mesopotamia" literally means "land between the rivers" — the Tigris and Euphrates. The geography was nearly perfectly suited for civilisation: fertile soil from annual flooding, water for irrigation, and the rivers themselves as transport routes for trade. This geography enabled the world's first cities, first writing systems, and first legal codes. Without those rivers in that specific location, the agricultural surplus that allowed specialist occupations (priests, scribes, soldiers) to exist would not have been possible. Geography enabled everything. Example 2 — Athens (Week 3): Athens had limited farmland (Attica was rocky and dry) but a superb natural harbour at Piraeus. This geography pushed Athens toward maritime trade rather than agricultural self-sufficiency — and that trade made Athens wealthy, cosmopolitan, and intellectually dynamic. The sea also provided defence. Athens became the cultural and intellectual centre of the ancient world partly because its geography forced it to engage with the wider world. Evaluation: Geography is a powerful shaping force but not an absolute destiny — Rome's geography gave it access to the Mediterranean but did not determine that Romans would build an empire. Human choices, leadership, and culture matter too. Geography sets the possibilities and constraints; humans decide what to do within them.
