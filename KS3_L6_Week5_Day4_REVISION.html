What does the phrase "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" establish about God's nature? Name TWO theological truths it implies. 2 marks

1. God is pre-existent — he existed before time and space and was not created. 2. God is Creator/sovereign — he is the source and owner of all that exists; nothing in creation is independent of him or rivals him.
2
Explain the three consequences of The Fall that affect all humans, according to the lesson. Then: which of these do you think has the most far-reaching impact and why? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Three consequences: 1. Broken relationship with God — Adam and Eve were expelled from Eden; direct, easy fellowship with God was disrupted. 2. Broken relationship with each other — blame, shame, and conflict entered human relationships (immediately visible in Cain and Abel). 3. Broken relationship with the earth — work became hard, the ground now produces "thorns and thistles," death enters. Most far-reaching: many would argue the broken relationship with God, because it is the root from which the other two flow — without the disruption of that primary relationship, the secondary and tertiary consequences would not have followed.
3
Why did God accept Abel's offering but reject Cain's? What did Cain do in response, and what does this reveal about the nature of sin? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The text doesn't give a fully explicit reason, but Hebrews 11:4 says Abel's offering was made "by faith" — suggesting the issue was the attitude and heart behind the offering, not merely its contents. Abel gave the "firstborn" and "fat portions" — the best he had; Cain simply gave "some of the fruits." Cain responded with anger and ultimately murdered his brother. This reveals that sin is not just isolated action but a growing pattern: resentment → anger → violence. God warned Cain beforehand that "sin is crouching at your door" — showing sin as an active force that gains power if not confronted at the attitude stage.

4
What made Noah "righteous" in God's sight? Does the Bible claim he was perfect? What does this tell us about what "righteousness" means in the Old Testament? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Genesis 6:9 describes Noah as "a righteous man, blameless among the people of his time" who "walked with God." However, after the flood Noah becomes drunk and dishonoured (Genesis 9:20–21) — showing he was not sinless. Righteousness here means a right orientation toward God — living in faithful relationship with God, choosing obedience in a culture that had abandoned him. Old Testament righteousness is about faithfulness and right relationship, not sinless perfection.
5
Describe Noah's covenant: who initiated it, what did God promise, what was the sign, and who was it for? How is this covenant different in scope from Abraham's (Week 5)? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Noah's covenant: God initiated it — it was not earned or negotiated. God promised never to destroy the earth by flood again. The sign was the rainbow. It was made with Noah, all his descendants, and every living creature — a universal, protective covenant for all life. Difference from Abraham's: Noah's covenant is protective and universal (God will not destroy). Abraham's covenant is purposeful and missional (God will build a nation and bless the world through it). Noah's looks backward (preventing destruction); Abraham's looks forward (building something).

What were the FOUR things God promised Abraham in Genesis 12:1–3? Write each promise precisely. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Land — "Go to the land I will show you." 2. A great nation — "I will make you into a great nation." 3. A great name/blessing — "I will bless you; I will make your name great." 4. Universal blessing — "All peoples on earth will be blessed through you." The four promises encompass land, people, reputation, and global impact.
7
The arc from Creation (Week 1) to Abraham (Week 5) shows a pattern: creation → fall → judgement → grace → new beginning. Map each of the five weeks' stories onto this pattern and explain how they connect. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
W1 Creation — the good beginning: God creates an ordered, beautiful world and places humanity in it as his image-bearers with a mandate to steward creation. W2 The Fall / Cain — the problem: humanity chooses independence from God; sin enters; consequences ripple outward into murder, broken relationships, and spreading corruption. W3 Noah called — judgement and preservation: God responds to cosmic corruption with the flood, but preserves a remnant through Noah's obedience. W4 Noah's Covenant — grace after judgement: God makes an unconditional promise never to destroy again; the rainbow seals it; a new start is given. W5 Abraham — purposeful new beginning: God does not just reset the story — he launches a rescue plan. By calling one man from one family, he begins the project of blessing the whole world. The entire arc shows a God who responds to humanity's failure not with abandonment but with increasingly specific, purposeful acts of grace — from universal preservation (Noah) to specific covenant-mission (Abraham).
8
BIG QUESTION: Looking across all five weeks, what do you think is the central message of Genesis 1–22? Write a sustained answer (at least 6 sentences) using evidence from at least THREE different weeks. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The central message of Genesis 1–22, read as a unified narrative, is that God's purpose for humanity and creation cannot be permanently derailed — and that his response to human failure is always a deeper act of grace. Creation (Week 1) establishes that God made humanity in his image for a relationship of love and stewardship — but The Fall (Week 2) shows this relationship was quickly broken through disobedience, and the consequences spread rapidly (Cain murdering Abel shows sin is not contained but grows). God's response to the flood (Week 3) might look like destruction, but it is actually preservation: Noah and his family are kept alive specifically so the story can continue. The rainbow covenant (Week 4) shows God's commitment to not give up — he makes an unconditional promise, binding himself to the restoration of creation. By Week 5, God's plan becomes more specific: through one man, Abraham, he begins to construct a path by which "all nations on earth will be blessed." The cumulative message is not about human achievement or moral progress — every major character fails at some point — but about a God whose faithfulness to his own purposes outlasts every human failure. Genesis 1–22 is not primarily a book about humanity; it is a book about the character of God.

📚Weeks 1–2: Reading & GrammarW1 + W2
1
Define: inference · explicit information · implicit meaning. Then explain: why is the ability to infer important in reading? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Inference: reading between the lines — working out meaning that is suggested but not directly stated. Explicit information: information that is directly stated in the text — the reader does not have to guess. Implicit meaning: meaning that is implied or hinted at through language choices, tone, or context — the reader must "read between the lines." Why inference matters: most skilled writing does not state everything directly. Authors use implicit meaning to create richer, more layered texts — if you can only read what is explicitly stated, you miss most of what the writer is actually doing.
2
Identify and correct the grammatical error in each sentence. Name the type of error. 4 marks

a) "The team are playing good today."
b) "She runned to the store quickly."
c) "Its the best book I have ever read."
d) "Me and him went to the market yesterday."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Adverb error — "good" is an adjective; should be "well" (adverb modifying "playing"). Corrected: "The team are playing well today." b) Irregular verb error — "run" is irregular; past tense is "ran" not "runned." Corrected: "She ran to the store." c) Apostrophe error — "Its" (possessive) should be "It's" (it is). Corrected: "It's the best book I have ever read." d) Pronoun case error — "Me and him" are object pronouns; as subjects they should be "He and I." Corrected: "He and I went to the market yesterday."

"Tariq had not spoken since Tuesday. His mother left food outside his door each morning — warm bread, a cup of sweet tea — and by evening it was always gone, but she never saw him take it. She had stopped knocking. The room behind his door was perfectly silent, the way a held breath is silent."

Created through: the mundane domestic detail (warm bread, sweet tea) contrasted with the invisible barrier; the repeated absence of Tariq despite evidence of his presence; the final simile that turns the silence into something physically tense and anticipatory.

6
CONNECTING QUESTION: Explain the key procedural difference between adding/subtracting fractions (Week 4) and multiplying/dividing fractions (Week 5). Why does multiplication NOT require a common denominator but addition DOES? Illustrate your explanation with a simple example of each. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Adding fractions combines parts of the same whole, so both fractions must refer to the same-sized pieces — that's why you need a common denominator first. Example: 1/2 + 1/3 — you cannot add them until they are in the same "language": 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6. Multiplying fractions is asking "what is a fraction OF a fraction" — a completely different operation. You are not combining pieces of the same whole; you are scaling one fraction by another. The denominator of the result tells you how many total pieces the new whole is divided into, and the numerator tells you how many you have. Example: 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6 — you have one-half of one-third, which is one-sixth of the whole. No common denominator is needed because you are not adding — you are multiplying. This is the single most important conceptual distinction in the fractions unit.

⚗️Weeks 3–4: Chemistry & Body BiologyW3 + W4
3
a) What is the difference between a physical change and a chemical change? Give ONE example of each. b) Name THREE signs that a chemical reaction has taken place. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Physical change: a change in the form or appearance of a substance that does NOT produce new substances — it is reversible. Example: ice melting to water (still H₂O). Chemical change: a change that produces one or more new substances with different properties — usually irreversible. Example: burning wood produces ash and CO₂ (cannot be un-burnt). b) Signs of chemical reaction: (1) colour change, (2) gas produced (bubbling/effervescence), (3) temperature change (exothermic = gets hot; endothermic = gets cold), (4) light produced, (5) a precipitate (solid) forming in a solution — accept any three.
4
Name the FOUR main types of digestive enzymes, what they digest, and where in the body they work. Then: what is the role of bile and where is it produced? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Amylase — digests starch → simple sugars. Works in: mouth (salivary amylase) and small intestine. 2. Protease — digests proteins → amino acids. Works in: stomach (pepsin), small intestine. 3. Lipase — digests fats/lipids → fatty acids + glycerol. Works in: small intestine. 4. Cellulase (or note this is not produced in humans — accept variation). Bile: produced in the liver, stored in the gall bladder. Role: emulsifies fats — breaks large fat droplets into smaller ones, increasing surface area for lipase to work on. Bile is NOT an enzyme (it does not speed up a reaction chemically — it is a physical emulsifier).
🌿Week 5: PhotosynthesisW5
5
Write the full balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis. Then: (a) identify what happens to each atom — where does the carbon go? Where does the oxygen come from? (b) explain the role of chlorophyll — is it a reactant or a catalyst, and why does the distinction matter? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (requires light energy). a) Carbon from CO₂ is fixed into the glucose molecule (C₆H₁₂O₆) — this is "carbon fixation." Oxygen: research has shown the oxygen released (O₂) comes from the splitting of water (H₂O) molecules, not from CO₂ — the water's oxygen atoms are released as O₂. b) Chlorophyll is a light-absorbing pigment, not consumed in the reaction — it absorbs light energy and transfers it to drive the reaction. It functions more like a catalyst (not consumed, not appearing in the equation). The distinction matters because: if it were a reactant it would be used up; because it is not used up, plants can continuously photosynthesize as long as light is available.
6
SYNTHESIS QUESTION: How are photosynthesis (Week 5) and respiration (a topic across multiple weeks) opposite but complementary processes? Write at least 5 sentences. Include equations, molecules, and the concept of energy. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Photosynthesis and respiration are, in many ways, reverse processes. Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (requires light energy, occurs only in plants with chlorophyll). Respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (occurs in ALL living cells, all the time). Photosynthesis stores energy — it takes light energy from the sun and locks it into the chemical bonds of glucose. Respiration releases energy — it breaks those same chemical bonds and releases the stored energy (as ATP) for the cell to use. The molecules involved are the same set but flowing in opposite directions: CO₂ and H₂O are the products of respiration and the reactants of photosynthesis; glucose and O₂ are the products of photosynthesis and the reactants of respiration. Together they form a cycle that keeps oxygen in the atmosphere and carbon cycling through living systems. Plants do both — photosynthesising during the day (net gain of glucose) and respiring all the time to fuel their own life processes.
7
BIG PICTURE: Looking across all five weeks of science, what is the ONE most important scientific principle you have learned? Write a paragraph (5–6 sentences) explaining it, using evidence from at least TWO different topics to show its importance. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any well-argued, evidence-based answer. Example: Structure determines function. This principle appears across every topic in five weeks of science. In cell biology (Week 2): the shape and structure of each organelle is precisely matched to its function — the mitochondria's folded inner membrane (cristae) maximises surface area for the reactions of respiration. The palisade mesophyll cell in a leaf (Week 5) is tall and column-shaped to pack in as many chloroplasts as possible — its shape directly serves its function of photosynthesis. In chemistry (Week 3): the structure of molecules determines how they react — different arrangements of the same atoms produce different substances with different properties. In digestion (Week 4): the villi and microvilli of the small intestine are folded to dramatically increase surface area — again, structure optimised for function (absorption). This principle is not just a biological observation but a deep design principle: understanding how something is built usually explains why it works the way it does.

What was the feudal system? Describe the hierarchy from king to serf. What held it together and what were its main weaknesses? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The feudal system was the social, political, and economic structure of medieval Europe — based on an exchange of land for loyalty and military service. Hierarchy: King → Nobles/Lords/Barons → Knights → Peasants/Serfs. Each level owed service and loyalty to the level above in exchange for land and protection. What held it together: the mutual obligation of land and service, reinforced by oath, religion, and the threat of military force. Main weaknesses: (1) power was decentralised — local lords often had more practical power than the king. (2) The Black Death (14th century) killed so many peasants that serfs gained bargaining power, breaking the feudal labour structure. (3) Rising merchant classes accumulated wealth outside the feudal land system.

What were THREE reasons for the fall of the Western Roman Empire? For each, explain it as a long-term cause rather than a single event. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Military overstretch and barbarian pressure — the empire had grown too large to defend effectively; the Rhine and Danube frontiers required enormous armies, and as Germanic and Hunnic peoples pushed against the borders over centuries, the military became increasingly unsustainable and dependent on non-Roman mercenaries who had less loyalty to Rome. 2. Economic decline and instability — centuries of military spending, currency debasement, heavy taxation, and the disruption of trade routes weakened the economy progressively; the wealth that funded the army, the roads, and the administration slowly dried up. 3. Political instability and weak leadership — in the 3rd century alone there were over 50 emperors, many assassinated; without stable central leadership, the empire fragmented in practice before it formally collapsed in 476 AD.
