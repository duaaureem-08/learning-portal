📖 Bible Studies · Week 6
Joseph —
Character Through Suffering
Genesis 37, 39–45 · Dreams, Betrayal & Forgiveness · God's Purposes in Difficult Circumstances
Genesis 50:20Genesis 37–45ForgivenessKS3 Level 6
⭐ Memory Verse
"You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good, to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives."
Genesis 50:20 — NIV
🧠
Word-by-word breakdown: "You intended to harm me" = Joseph names the wrong done to him honestly — he does not pretend his brothers' betrayal was not real or painful. "God intended it for good" = the SAME events can carry two intentions at once — human evil and divine purpose are not the same thing. "to accomplish... the saving of many lives" = the good that came was not small or private; it rescued an entire region from famine. This is the key verse of Joseph's whole story — said only at the very END, after twenty-two years and after he had already chosen to forgive.
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Learn these words before you continue
favouritism
Treating one person better or with more love than others, often unfairly. Jacob's open favouritism toward Joseph is the spark that lights this whole story.
betrayal
An act of deep disloyalty against someone who trusted you. Joseph's own brothers betray him by selling him into slavery.
interpret / interpretation
To explain the MEANING of something unclear, such as a dream. Joseph's gift for dream interpretation shapes his entire future.
integrity
Doing what is right even when no one is watching and it costs you something. Joseph shows integrity by refusing to do wrong even as a slave.
steward
A person trusted to manage someone else's household, property or affairs. Joseph becomes a steward first to Potiphar, later to all of Egypt.
famine
A severe shortage of food, often caused by drought or crop failure. (Remember this word from Week 5 — Abraham's famine. Egypt's famine is far more severe and lasts seven years.)
reconcile / reconciliation
To restore a broken relationship — making peace between people who were once divided. Joseph's reconciliation with his brothers is the emotional centre of the story.
providence
God's wise and unseen guidance over events, often only recognisable by looking BACKWARD over a long stretch of time — exactly as Joseph does in Genesis 50:20.
famine relief
Organised food storage and distribution to help people survive a famine. Joseph's 14-year grain plan (7 years storing, 7 years distributing) becomes Egypt's famine relief system.
🧥 A Family Torn by Favouritism — Genesis 37
📍
Joseph was the eleventh of Jacob's twelve sons, but the first son of his beloved wife Rachel. Jacob made no secret of his favourite — he gave Joseph an elaborate robe (often translated "a coat of many colours"), and his ten older half-brothers noticed and resented it bitterly.
😠 What Fuelled the Brothers' Hatred
Jacob's open, unequal favouritism toward Joseph
The expensive, symbolic robe — a visible sign of status
Joseph reporting his brothers' misdeeds to their father
Joseph's two dreams, both showing his family bowing to him
⚖️ How Far the Hatred Went
"They hated him and could not speak a kind word to him" (37:4)
Some brothers wanted to kill him outright (37:20)
Reuben persuades them to throw him in a pit instead (37:22)
Judah suggests selling him to traders for silver (37:26-27)
🔑
KS3 thinking point: Joseph was only about 17 years old (Genesis 37:2) when his brothers turned on him. Unlike Noah or Abraham, who were called by God directly into hardship, Joseph's suffering begins entirely through other PEOPLE'S choices — making this a story about how God works even through human wrongdoing.
💭 Joseph's Two Dreams — Genesis 37:5-11
📖
Joseph has TWO dreams, both pointing to the same future, told to his family at the worst possible moment.
Dream One
Sheaves of wheat in a field — Joseph's sheaf stands upright, and his brothers' sheaves bow down to it.
→
Dream Two
The sun, moon, and eleven stars bow down to Joseph — even his father Jacob is troubled by this one.
✅
KS3 key idea: Both dreams turn out to be literally true — but not for over twenty years, and not before unimaginable suffering. This is the first hint of a pattern that runs through the whole story: God's promises can be TRUE long before they look true.
⛓️ From the Pit to the Palace — Joseph's Long Road
Stage 1
Sold into Slavery (Genesis 37)
Joseph's brothers throw him into a pit, then sell him to Midianite/Ishmaelite traders for twenty pieces of silver. He is taken to Egypt and sold to Potiphar, an Egyptian official.
Stage 2
Falsely Accused & Imprisoned (Genesis 39)
Joseph serves Potiphar with such integrity that he is placed in charge of the whole household — until Potiphar's wife falsely accuses him, and he is thrown into prison despite doing nothing wrong.
Stage 3
Interpreting Dreams in Prison (Genesis 40-41)
In prison, Joseph correctly interprets the dreams of two royal officials. Two years later, when Pharaoh has two troubling dreams no one can explain, Joseph is remembered and brought before him.
Stage 4
Second-in-Command of Egypt (Genesis 41)
Joseph interprets Pharaoh's dreams as a warning of 7 years of plenty followed by 7 years of famine, and proposes a grain-storage plan. Pharaoh makes him second-in-command over all of Egypt — at around age 30.
Stage 5
Reunion & Forgiveness (Genesis 42-45)
When famine strikes Canaan too, Joseph's brothers travel to Egypt for grain and unknowingly bow before the brother they once sold. Joseph eventually reveals himself and forgives them completely.
💡
KS3 timeline link: Roughly 13 years pass between Joseph being sold (age 17) and standing before Pharaoh (age 30) — and another 9 years pass before his brothers arrive in Egypt. The dreams of Genesis 37 are not fulfilled for over two decades.
🤝 The Moment of Forgiveness — Genesis 45:4-8
🔑
The golden idea of this lesson: Joseph could easily have used his power to punish his brothers — instead, he weeps, reassures them, and reframes the whole story around God's purpose rather than his own pain.
What the Brothers ExpectedWhat Joseph Actually Said
Revenge, imprisonment, or execution"Come close to me... I am Joseph your brother, whom you sold into Egypt!" (45:4)
Permanent rejection and shame"Do not be distressed and do not be angry with yourselves" (45:5)
Blame placed entirely on them"It was not you who sent me here, but God" (45:8)
Being sent away in disgraceHe invites the whole family to live safely in Egypt's best land, Goshen
✅
ESL note: Forgiveness in this story does NOT mean pretending the wrong didn't happen (Joseph names it plainly: "whom you sold"). It means choosing not to let that wrong control how you treat the person now.

The pattern deepens: Joseph's story adds something new — it is the first major Bible story where the difficulty comes not from God's instruction or a natural disaster, but from the sin of OTHER PEOPLE. Yet God's purpose still moves forward through it.
🎯 What Trusting God Through Suffering Looks Like — Application
1
Integrity matters most when no one is checking
Joseph refused to do wrong even as an enslaved foreigner with no family to answer to — character is most visible under pressure, not comfort.
2
Unfair seasons do not always end quickly
Joseph spent roughly 13 years in slavery and prison before things changed. Faithfulness sometimes means continuing to do right with no end date in sight.
3
Looking back can reveal a purpose you couldn't see at the time
Joseph could only say "God intended it for good" at the END of the story, looking backward — not while he was still in the pit or the prison.
4
Forgiveness can exist alongside honesty about being wronged
Joseph names what his brothers did plainly, then chooses not to let it define how he treats them — the two are not contradictions.
✍️
Sentence starters for reflection: "Joseph's integrity is shown through his ___ because ___." / "Genesis 50:20 teaches that ___." / "A time I had to keep doing the right thing even when things felt unfair was ___."

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these — they unlock thousands of other words
root word
The CORE part of a word that carries its basic meaning, often borrowed from Latin or Greek. "Port" (to carry) is the root of transport, export, portable.
prefix
A group of letters added to the BEGINNING of a root word that changes its meaning. "Un-" + happy = unhappy (NOT happy).
suffix
A group of letters added to the END of a root word, often changing its word class (noun, verb, adjective). "-ful" + hope = hopeful (an adjective).
etymology
The study of where a word came from and how its meaning has changed over time.
syllable
A single unbroken sound in a spoken word. "Beautiful" has three syllables: beau-ti-ful.
synonym
A word with a SIMILAR meaning to another word. "Enormous" is a synonym of "huge."
antonym
A word with the OPPOSITE meaning to another word. "Ancient" is an antonym of "modern."
derivative
A new word formed by adding a prefix or suffix to a root word. "Disagreement" is a derivative of "agree."

🏗️ Building Academic Vocabulary — Step by Step
1
Break the word into its parts
Split an unfamiliar word into prefix + root + suffix wherever possible: "in-flex-ible" = not + bend + able to be.
2
Guess the meaning from the parts
"Inflexible" = "not able to be bent" — and by extension, a person or rule that won't change.
3
Check the word in context
Read the full sentence around the word to confirm your guess matches how it is actually being used.
4
Collect the word family
Note related forms: flex, flexible, inflexible, flexibility, flexibly — learning one root often gives you five words at once.
5
Use the new word actively
Write your own sentence with the word the same day you learn it — active use is far more effective than just reading a definition.
📝 Spelling Strategies That Actually Work
👀 Look, Say, Cover, Write, Check
Look closely at the word, say it aloud, cover it, write it from memory, then check — repeat until correct.
✂️ Break into Syllables
Split long words into sound chunks: "ne-ces-sa-ry" is far easier to spell than "necessary" attempted all at once.
🔗 Spot the Root
"Definitely" contains "finite" — remembering the root helps avoid the common misspelling "definately."
🎵 Use a Memory Phrase
"Necessary" = one Collar, two Sleeves (1 c, 2 s) — silly phrases for tricky letter patterns can stick for years.
💡
ESL tip: English spelling often does not match pronunciation exactly because words were borrowed from many different languages (Old English, French, Latin, Greek) at different points in history — this is exactly what etymology studies.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Builds on Week 5 — multiplying and dividing fractions
decimal
A number written using a DECIMAL POINT to show parts of a whole, e.g. 0.75. Each digit after the point has a place value (tenths, hundredths, thousandths).
percentage
A way of expressing a number as a fraction of 100. "Per cent" literally means "out of one hundred." 50% means 50 out of every 100.
equivalent
Having the same VALUE, even when written in a different form. 1/2, 0.5, and 50% are all equivalent.
place value
The value of a digit based on its POSITION in a number. In 0.75, the 7 is in the tenths place and the 5 is in the hundredths place.
recurring decimal
A decimal where one or more digits repeat forever, e.g. 1/3 = 0.333... Written with a dot above the repeating digit.
percentage increase
How much a value has GROWN, expressed as a percentage of the original amount.
percentage decrease
How much a value has SHRUNK, expressed as a percentage of the original amount.

➗ Converting Fractions to Decimals — Divide!
🔑
The golden rule: a fraction is really just a division. To convert any fraction to a decimal, divide the NUMERATOR by the DENOMINATOR.
Example: Convert 3/8 to a decimal

3/8 means 3 ÷ 8
3 ÷ 8 = 0.375

Final answer: 3/8 = 0.375
1
Identify numerator and denominator
For 3/8: numerator = 3, denominator = 8.
2
Divide numerator by denominator
Use short or long division: 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375.
3
Check for recurring decimals
Some fractions, like 1/3 or 1/7, never divide exactly and repeat forever — mark the repeating digit(s) with a dot.
💯 Converting Decimals to Percentages — ×100
⚠️
Common mistake: students often forget that multiplying by 100 simply MOVES the decimal point two places to the right — it does not add random zeros.
Decimal → Percentage: multiply by 100

0.375 × 100 = 37.5 → write as 37.5%
0.6 × 100 = 60 → write as 60%

Percentage → Decimal: divide by 100 (the reverse)
82% ÷ 100 = 0.82
🧮 Finding a Percentage of an Amount
💡
There are two reliable methods — choose whichever is faster for the numbers in front of you.
1
Method A — Decimal multiplier
Convert the percentage to a decimal, then multiply. To find 30% of 80: 0.30 × 80 = 24.
2
Method B — Find 1%, then scale up
To find 30% of 80: first find 1% (80 ÷ 100 = 0.8), then multiply by 30: 0.8 × 30 = 24.
3
Mental shortcut — use 10% and 50%
10% of 80 = 8 (divide by 10). 5% = half of that = 4. So 15% = 10% + 5% = 8 + 4 = 12 — fast, no calculator needed.
Worked example: Find 65% of 220

Decimal multiplier: 0.65 × 220 = 143
Check using 10% method: 10% = 22, so 60% = 132, and 5% = 11, giving 60%+5% = 143 ✓
📈 Percentage Increase & Decrease — Real-Life Maths
ScenarioWorkingAnswer
A £40 jacket has a 25% discount. New price?25% of 40 = 10. £40 − £10 =£30
A town's population of 5,000 grows by 12%.12% of 5,000 = 600. 5,000 + 600 =5,600
A £15 book is increased by 20% for a sale, then the sale gives 20% OFF.£15 → £18 → 20% of £18 = £3.60 → £14.40£14.40 — not £15!
⚠️
KS3 key idea: A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does NOT return you to the original amount — because the second 20% is calculated on a DIFFERENT (larger) number. This surprises many students and is a favourite exam trap.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Biology Guide
Builds on Week 5 — remember producer, photosynthesis and glucose
ecosystem
All the living organisms in an area, together with the non-living parts (water, soil, climate) they interact with.
habitat
The natural HOME or environment of a particular organism — where it lives and finds everything it needs to survive.
producer
An organism that makes its OWN food, usually through photosynthesis. Plants, algae and some bacteria are producers — the starting point of almost every food chain.
consumer
An organism that gets its energy by EATING other organisms, because it cannot make its own food.
decomposer
An organism (such as fungi or bacteria) that breaks down dead plants and animals, returning nutrients to the soil.
predator
An animal that hunts and eats other animals (its prey).
prey
An animal that is hunted and eaten by a predator.
food chain
A simple diagram showing the flow of energy from one organism to the next, in a single straight line.
food web
A network of MANY overlapping food chains, showing the real, more complicated feeding relationships within an ecosystem.
🍃 Producers, Consumers & Decomposers — The Three Roles
💡
Every organism in an ecosystem plays one of three energy roles. Together, these three roles keep nutrients and energy cycling endlessly through the system.
RoleHow It Gets EnergyExamples
ProducerMakes its own food via photosynthesis (Week 5 link!)Grass, trees, algae, seaweed
Consumer — PrimaryEats producers directly (a herbivore)Rabbit, grasshopper, deer
Consumer — SecondaryEats primary consumers (a carnivore or omnivore)Fox, frog, small fish
Consumer — TertiaryTop predator, eats secondary consumersEagle, lion, shark
DecomposerBreaks down dead organisms and wasteFungi, bacteria, earthworms
✅
KS3 key idea: Without decomposers, dead material would simply pile up forever, and the nutrients locked inside it could never be recycled back into the soil for new producers to use.

📊 Energy Pyramids — Why Food Chains Are Short
💡
Energy is LOST as heat at every stage of a food chain — only about 10% of the energy at one level passes on to the next. This is why food chains rarely have more than 4-5 links, and why top predators are always far less numerous than producers.
Producers (grass)

~1%
Tertiary consumers

✅
KS3 key idea: This is exactly why a single field can support thousands of blades of grass, hundreds of rabbits, but usually only a handful of foxes — energy shrinks dramatically at each level.
🌍 Habitats Around the World
🌳 Woodland
Trees provide shelter and food for foxes, owls, squirrels and countless insects and fungi.
🐠 Coral Reef
One of the most biodiverse habitats on Earth, home to thousands of interconnected marine species.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL History & Geography Guide
Builds on Week 5's map skills — latitude, longitude, scale
continent
One of the seven huge landmasses on Earth: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America, South America.
ocean
A vast body of salt water. There are five named oceans: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, and Arctic.
landform
A natural feature of the Earth's surface, such as a mountain, valley, plain, or plateau.
mountain range
A connected series of mountains formed by the same geological processes, often stretching for thousands of kilometres.
plateau
A large, raised area of fairly flat land — sometimes called a "tableland."
peninsula
An area of land almost completely surrounded by water, connected to the mainland by a narrow strip.
archipelago
A group or chain of many islands close together, such as Indonesia or the Philippines.
basin (river basin)
The entire area of land drained by a river and all its tributaries (smaller rivers that flow into it).

⛰️ Major Landforms — Reading the Physical World
⛰️ Mountain
Land that rises sharply, far higher than its surroundings — often formed where two of Earth's plates collide.
🏞️ Valley
Low land between hills or mountains, often carved out over millions of years by a flowing river.
🌊 River
A natural, flowing channel of fresh water that travels from a source (often mountains) toward the sea.
🏜️ Desert
A region with very low rainfall — not always hot; Antarctica is technically the largest desert on Earth.
🟫 Plain
A large area of flat or gently rolling land, often used for farming due to its fertile soil.
🗻 Plateau
A large flat-topped area raised well above the surrounding land — like a "tableland."

💧 How Rivers Shape the Land — From Source to Sea
1
Source
A river begins at its source — often a mountain spring, melting glacier, or a lake high above sea level.
2
Upper Course
Fast-flowing water cuts steep, narrow valleys through higher ground, eroding rock as it goes.
3
Middle & Lower Course
The river widens and slows on flatter land, often curving into large bends called meanders, and depositing fertile soil.
4
Mouth (Delta/Estuary)
The river finally reaches the sea, sometimes splitting into a fan-shaped delta as it deposits the last of its sediment.
✅
KS3 key idea: Many of the world's earliest civilisations grew up along major rivers (the Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus, Yellow River) because of the fertile farmland river deposits create.
⚖️ Comparing Two Contrasting Regions
🏜️ The Sahara Desert
Largest hot desert on Earth, spanning much of North Africa
Extremely low rainfall and very sparse vegetation
Daytime temperatures can exceed 45°C, but nights can be near freezing
🌳 The Amazon Rainforest
Largest tropical rainforest on Earth, spanning much of South America
Extremely high rainfall and the greatest biodiversity of any biome
Hot and humid year-round, with relatively stable temperatures
⚠️
KS3 key idea: "Desert" describes RAINFALL, not necessarily heat — both the Sahara and Antarctica are deserts by definition, despite being opposite in temperature. You'll explore climate zones and biomes further in Week 7.
