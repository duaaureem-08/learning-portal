📚 Full Technique Recall
1
Without notes, write a complete summary for each of the four separation techniques. For each: state what it separates, describe the process in 2-3 sentences, name the key equipment, and give ONE real-world use. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
FILTRATION: Separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel; the liquid (filtrate) passes through tiny holes; the solid (residue) is caught. Equipment: filter funnel, filter paper, conical flask. Real-world use: water treatment plants filter suspended particles from river water before purification. EVAPORATION: Separates a dissolved solid from a solution — used when you want the SOLID. The solution is heated in an evaporating dish; the liquid turns to steam and escapes; the solid remains as crystals. Equipment: evaporating dish, Bunsen burner. Real-world use: extracting salt from sea water commercially (salt pans). DISTILLATION: Separates a liquid from a solution, or two liquids with different boiling points — used when you want the LIQUID pure. The solution is heated; vapour rises and passes through a condenser (cooled tube), condensing back to a pure liquid collected in a flask. Equipment: round-bottom flask, condenser, thermometer, collection flask. Real-world use: producing distilled (pure) water; industrial production of alcohol; petroleum refinery (fractional distillation). CHROMATOGRAPHY: Separates mixtures of dissolved coloured substances. A spot of mixture is placed on paper above solvent; solvent travels up, carrying components at different speeds, producing separate coloured bands. Equipment: chromatography paper, solvent, beaker. Real-world use: forensic science (identifying unknown substances); testing food colourings; drug testing in sport.

A student is given a mystery liquid. She is told it is either pure water or a salt solution, but she cannot tell by looking. She has access to a Bunsen burner, an evaporating dish, a thermometer, a conductivity tester (which lights up when electricity passes through — salt water conducts, pure water does not), and some filter paper.

Design a TWO-step investigation to identify whether the liquid is pure water or a salt solution. For each step: state what you do, what you observe, and what the result tells you. Then explain which technique from this week is used at each step (or closest to it). 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Step 1 — Conductivity test: Dip the conductivity tester into the liquid. Observe: If the light comes on, the liquid conducts electricity — this indicates ions (like sodium and chloride from salt) are dissolved in it. If the light stays off, the liquid does not conduct electricity — suggesting it is pure water (no ions). What it tells you: A positive result strongly suggests salt solution; negative suggests pure water. Technique: This is a physical property test — similar in principle to identifying a pure substance by its properties. Step 2 — Evaporation test (confirmation): Pour a small amount of the liquid into the evaporating dish and heat gently until all liquid has evaporated. Observe: If white crystals (salt) remain in the dish, the liquid was a salt solution. If the dish is completely empty and clean, the liquid was pure water (nothing left to remain). What it tells you: This definitively confirms the presence or absence of a dissolved solid. Technique: Evaporation — the same technique used to recover salt from sea water. Note: A strong student might also suggest the boiling point test — pure water boils at exactly 100°C; salt water boils at slightly above 100°C. Using the thermometer during evaporation gives a third piece of evidence.

🏆 Extended Scientific Evaluation
4
"The four separation techniques — filtration, evaporation, distillation, and chromatography — share one principle: they exploit the DIFFERENT PROPERTIES of substances to pull them apart." Evaluate this statement. In your answer: explain what specific property each technique exploits; give an example showing how the same principle applies at different scales (lab and industrial); and discuss whether this principle has limits — can every mixture be separated by these techniques? Write at least 8 sentences. 10 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The statement is accurate and reveals the underlying unity of all four techniques. Filtration exploits the difference in PARTICLE SIZE — the liquid and any truly dissolved substances pass through the tiny holes in filter paper, while solid particles too large to pass are retained. At industrial scale, this is the principle behind municipal water filtration systems, where river water passes through sand and gravel beds of decreasing particle size. Evaporation exploits the difference in VOLATILITY — the liquid can turn to gas at temperatures where the dissolved solid cannot. At industrial scale, salt pans across the world use solar evaporation to harvest sea salt commercially. Distillation also exploits volatility, but more precisely exploits DIFFERENCES IN BOILING POINT between two liquids — the one with the lower boiling point vaporises first. Industrial fractional distillation of crude petroleum separates petrol, diesel, kerosene, and bitumen by their different boiling points at an enormous scale. Chromatography exploits differences in SOLUBILITY and AFFINITY — how strongly each substance "prefers" the solvent (mobile phase) versus the paper (stationary phase). At industrial and forensic scale, gas chromatography analyses tiny samples of complex mixtures with extraordinary precision. Limits of the principle: These techniques work well when the substances have significantly different properties. However, when substances have very similar properties (e.g. two proteins of nearly the same size), basic techniques may fail to separate them and more sophisticated methods are needed. Also, these techniques separate physical mixtures — they cannot separate substances that have been chemically combined (compounds), because the properties are no longer those of the original elements. A molecule of water cannot be separated into hydrogen and oxygen by filtration — that requires electrolysis, a chemical process. The principle therefore applies powerfully but only within the domain of physical mixtures.

a) Push factor: A reason that forces or encourages people to LEAVE their current home. Example: drought destroying crops. CAUSE of migration. b) Pull factor: A reason that attracts people TO a new place. Example: better job opportunities in a city. CAUSE of migration. c) Urbanisation: The process of more people moving from rural areas to cities, increasing the proportion living in urban areas. Example: Pakistan's urban population has grown from ~20% (1950) to ~40%+ (today). RESULT of migration (specifically rural-urban migration). d) Internal migration: Movement of people within the same country. Example: moving from a village in Punjab to Karachi. TYPE of migration. e) International migration: Movement of people between different countries. Example: migrating from Pakistan to the UK. TYPE of migration. f) Refugee: A person who has been forced to leave their home country due to war, persecution, or natural disaster and cannot safely return. Example: Syrian refugees who fled civil war from 2011. A refugee is a specific TYPE of international migrant — one driven primarily by push factors of danger, not economic choice.

Scenario 1: (a) Push factors: decreased rainfall/drought reducing crop viability; falling farm income making rural life economically unsustainable. Pull factors: much higher wages in Karachi (4× farm income); better transport access (motorway) making the move practical; family connections already in Karachi reducing risk. Remittances (money sent back by migrants) may be the main source of income for those who stay. Scenario 2: (d) International migration — crossing national borders from Ukraine into European countries. The Indo-Gangetic Plain (home to Delhi, Karachi, Dhaka, Kolkata) — one of the world's most densely populated regions — is a vast river-deposited lowland. The same fertile, flat, water-rich terrain that attracted the Indus Valley Civilisation 4,500 years ago still concentrates billions today. b) Week 7 (climate zones): The tropics and subtropics host the world's fastest-growing urban populations (Lagos, Dhaka, Kinshasa, Mumbai) — because warm climates allow year-round farming, supporting high densities. Conversely, polar and tundra climates have near-zero settlement because harsh climate limits food production. Science has made geography partially negotiable. d) Week 8 Bible (Commandments): High population density in cities means strangers living in extreme proximity. The commandments — especially do not murder (physical safety), do not steal (property security), do not lie (social trust) — describe exactly the social framework that makes dense co-living possible. Without these norms being widely practiced, cities would be dangerous and dysfunctional. Law and community norms are as essential as water and land to any sustainable settlement.

Option A guidance: Physical factors historically: water, fertile soil, mild climate, defence — all drove ancient settlement patterns.
