🔍 Structured Paragraphs
2
The lesson says Joseph's forgiveness "does not mean pretending the wrong didn't happen." Write a structured paragraph explaining the difference between genuine forgiveness and either (a) pretending the wrong never occurred, or (b) saying it doesn't matter. Use evidence from Joseph's words in Genesis 45 and 50. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Genuine forgiveness, as demonstrated by Joseph, is radically different from both denial and minimisation of wrongdoing. When Joseph reveals himself to his brothers, his first words are: "I am Joseph your brother, whom you sold into Egypt" (45:4) — he names the act directly and accurately. He does not say "it doesn't matter" or pretend the slavery and imprisonment did not happen. What changes is not his acknowledgement of the wrong, but his response to it: "Do not be distressed and do not be angry with yourselves" (45:5). Then in Genesis 50:20, he gives his full theological interpretation: the same act carried two different intentions simultaneously — his brothers' intention to harm, and God's intention for good. This is not cheap forgiveness that denies reality; it is a forgiveness that looks the reality of the wrong fully in the face and then chooses a different response than revenge. The significance for the reader is that forgiveness of this kind is only possible if the forgiver has found a larger framework — here, providence — that gives the suffering a meaning without minimising it. It is simultaneously the most honest and the most free response to injustice.
3
Write a paragraph explaining how Joseph's story develops the Bible's growing picture of faith from Genesis. Start with Noah, move to Abraham, arrive at Joseph — showing what NEW element each story adds. Use the lesson's phrase "the pattern deepens." 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The pattern of faith in Genesis deepens with each story, adding a new dimension to what trusting God requires. With Noah, the call is to trust a promise that seems absurd — build a boat because rain is coming when there has been no rain. Faith here means action before evidence. With Abraham, the challenge becomes trusting without knowing where you are going: "Go from your country... to the land I will show you" (Gen 12:1). Faith here means obedience without a map. But Joseph's story, the lesson notes, adds something neither Noah nor Abraham faced: his suffering was caused by other people's sin. He did not choose hardship; it was done to him. Yet God's purpose still moved forward through it. This is the new element: faith through injustice caused by others. The pattern deepens because each story asks more of the believer. Trusting before evidence; trusting without a destination; trusting when someone else's cruelty is the instrument — together, these three stories construct a theology of faith that covers almost every situation in which trust is tested.

🏆 Extended Essay
4
"Joseph's story is the most complete portrait of faith under pressure in the book of Genesis." Write a full essay response evaluating this statement. You must: refer to at least THREE specific episodes from Genesis 37–50; compare with Noah and/or Abraham; address the concepts of integrity, providence, and forgiveness; and write a conclusion that takes a clear position. Minimum 10 sentences. 14 marks
Suggested structure: Introduction (your position) → Evidence para 1 (integrity in adversity) → Evidence para 2 (providence across time) → Evidence para 3 (forgiveness and reconciliation) → Comparison with Noah/Abraham → Conclusion (why "most complete" may or may not be the right word).
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Joseph's story can be reasonably described as the most complete portrait of faith under pressure in Genesis, though not because it is the most dramatic — it is because it addresses the dimension of suffering that is most personally relevant: suffering caused by other people's wrong. Three specific episodes demonstrate this completeness. First, integrity in Potiphar's house (Gen 39): Joseph refuses the advances of Potiphar's wife not for fear of consequences but because to do so would be "a sin against God" — and his virtue is then immediately punished by false accusation and imprisonment. This tests faith at the level of daily moral choices in private. Second, the two years of being forgotten (Gen 40–41): Joseph correctly interprets the cupbearer's dream and makes a reasonable request to be remembered, and nothing happens for two years. This tests faith in God's timing when there is no visible progress. Third, the moment of forgiveness (Gen 45 and 50): Joseph has absolute power over the brothers who wronged him, and instead of revenge, offers reconciliation, reframing the entire story through the lens of divine providence. This tests faith at the level of the will — choosing a particular response to injustice. Compared with Noah and Abraham, Joseph's story adds the dimension of sustained injustice over years. Noah's trial was finite and physical; Abraham's was the long uncertainty of a nomadic life without a child. Joseph's is closer to what many ordinary people experience: being wronged by those you trusted, serving faithfully and being overlooked, waiting for a promise to come true in circumstances that seem to deny it. For this reason, his story may indeed be the most "complete" — not the most spectacular, but the most broadly applicable. The closing statement of Genesis 50:20 is the theological summary that makes the whole portrait complete: the same events can carry two intentions simultaneously, and what looked like destruction was actually rescue. That is as complete a statement about faith, suffering, and purpose as the book of Genesis contains.

3
Spot and correct the error in each student's working. For each one, explain what the student did wrong and give the correct answer: 6 marks

a) "35% of 80 = 35 ÷ 80 × 100 = 43.75"   b) "A price rises from £50 to £60. % increase = 10 ÷ 60 × 100 = 16.7%"   c) "After a 20% increase on £30, I have £36. Then a 20% decrease: £36 − £6 = £30."   d) "Original price after 25% discount = sale price + 25% = £45 + £11.25 = £56.25"
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Error: inverted the calculation — divided percentage by amount instead of multiplying. Correct: 35% of 80 = 0.35 × 80 = 28. b) Error: divided by the NEW price (60) instead of the ORIGINAL price (50). Correct: 10 ÷ 50 × 100 = 20% increase. c) Error: calculated 20% of £30 (= £6) for the decrease instead of 20% of £36 (the current value). Correct: 20% of £36 = £7.20. £36 − £7.20 = £28.80 (not £30). d) Error: added 25% of the sale price to find the original — but the 25% was taken off the ORIGINAL, not the sale price. 🏆 Multi-Step Real-World Reasoning
4
Egypt's population in 2000 was approximately 68 million. By 2024, it had grown to approximately 106 million. (a) Calculate the overall percentage increase from 2000 to 2024. (b) If the population grows at the same percentage rate again over the next 24 years, what would the population be in 2048? Give your answer to 3 significant figures. (c) What assumption are you making in part (b), and why might this not be accurate? 6 marks
🔗 Cross-subject link: History/Geography — population growth in the Nile Valley and its continuing importance today.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Change = 106 − 68 = 38 million. % increase = 38 ÷ 68 × 100 = 55.9% increase (to 3 sig figs: 55.9%). (b) Apply the same 55.9% increase to 106 million: 106 × 1.559 = approximately 165 million (3 sig figs). (c) Assumption: that the rate of population growth remains constant at ~55.9% per 24-year period. This is unlikely to hold because: birth rates typically fall as countries develop economically; healthcare, education, and urbanisation all tend to slow population growth; resource constraints (food, water — particularly relevant for the Nile valley where water is limited) may also slow growth. Population projections become increasingly unreliable over longer time periods because they cannot account for social, political, or environmental changes.
5
Three friends each invest money. Anya invests £500 at a 4% increase per year for 3 years. Ben invests £500 at a one-off 12% increase. Priya invests £500: 6% increase in year 1, 3% decrease in year 2, then 9% increase in year 3. (a) Calculate each person's final total to the nearest penny. (b) Who has the most money after 3 years? (c) Explain why Anya's result is NOT the same as a single 12% increase. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Anya: Year 1: £500 × 1.04 = £520. Year 2: £520 × 1.04 = £540.80. Year 3: £540.80 × 1.04 = £562.43. Ben: £500 × 1.12 = £560.00. Priya: Year 1: £500 × 1.06 = £530. Year 2: £530 × 0.97 = £514.10. Year 3: £514.10 × 1.09 = £560.37 (to nearest penny). (b) Anya has the most money (£562.43). (c) Anya's 4% per year for 3 years gives compound interest: each year's 4% is calculated on the new, larger total, not on the original £500. Ben's 12% is calculated on £500 only. The difference is small here (£562.43 vs £560.00) but it would grow significantly over longer periods — this is the fundamental principle of compound interest that makes long-term savings so powerful.

🟢 EasySection A — Quick consolidation of all key Science vocabulary from the week.
📚 Vocabulary Consolidation
1
Write a definition AND a one-sentence example for each term — without looking at your notes: 6 marks

ecosystem · food web · trophic level · decomposer · habitat · biodiversity
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
ecosystem: all the living organisms in an area plus the non-living environment they interact with. Example: a woodland ecosystem includes trees, insects, foxes, fungi, the soil, water, and sunlight. food web: many overlapping food chains in one ecosystem, showing the real complexity of feeding relationships. Example: a grassland food web shows that foxes eat both rabbits AND mice, and that both are prey for different predators. trophic level: a feeding level in a food chain or web. Example: grass = level 1 (producers); rabbits = level 2 (primary consumers); foxes = level 3. decomposer: an organism that breaks down dead material and returns nutrients to the soil. Example: fungi breaking down fallen leaves in woodland. habitat: the specific natural home or environment of an organism. Example: the ocean floor is the habitat of deep-sea fish. biodiversity: the variety of different species of living organisms in an area. Example: a coral reef has one of the highest biodiversities on Earth — thousands of different species in one habitat.
Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Scientific evaluation and calculating energy flow.
🔍 Energy Calculations & Scientific Reasoning
2
A coral reef food chain contains: Algae → Small fish → Large fish → Shark. The algae layer contains 500,000 kJ of energy. (a) Using the 10% rule, calculate the energy available at each trophic level. (b) How many sharks could theoretically be supported if each shark needs 100 kJ per year? (c) If ocean warming destroyed 60% of the algae, how would this affect shark numbers? Show your calculation. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Small fish: 10% of 500,000 = 50,000 kJ. Large fish: 10% of 50,000 = 5,000 kJ. Shark: 10% of 5,000 = 500 kJ. (b) 500 kJ ÷ 100 kJ per shark = 5 sharks. (c) Algae after 60% loss = 40% of 500,000 = 200,000 kJ. Small fish: 20,000 kJ. Large fish: 2,000 kJ. Shark: 200 kJ. New shark number: 200 ÷ 100 = 2 sharks. Conclusion: losing 60% of the algae (producer) base reduces shark numbers by 60% — from 5 to 2. The 10% rule means that a loss at the base is felt exactly proportionally all the way up the chain.
3
The lesson mentions that the Amazon Rainforest contains the greatest biodiversity on Earth. Give THREE specific reasons why cutting down large areas of the Amazon (deforestation) would have catastrophic effects not just locally but globally. Use science vocabulary from this week in your answer. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Reason 1 — Collapse of the local food web: Trees are the dominant producers in the Amazon food web. Removing them eliminates the energy source for millions of primary consumers (insects, birds, mammals). Without producers, the entire food web collapses from the base upward — a cascading extinction of consumers at every trophic level. Reason 2 — Destruction of biodiversity and carbon storage: The Amazon stores enormous quantities of carbon in its biomass. Deforestation releases this carbon as CO₂, accelerating climate change globally. Higher global temperatures affect ecosystems worldwide — including polar/tundra food chains (the lesson notes these are already short and fragile) and coral reefs (already stressed by warming oceans). Reason 3 — Loss of decomposer-nutrient cycles: The Amazon's nutrient cycle depends on rapid decomposition — decomposers return nutrients from dead matter to the soil quickly in the hot, wet conditions. Remove the trees and the nutrient cycle breaks down; the soil (which is actually quite thin and nutrient-poor without the tree canopy) becomes unusable for farming or regrowth within years. This connects to the lesson's point that decomposers are essential to the functioning of any ecosystem.

🔴 HardSection C — Extended scientific writing. Evaluate and synthesise across all this week's content.
🏆 Scientific Evaluation
4
Write a full scientific evaluation (minimum 10 sentences) responding to this claim: "Humans are just another organism in the food web — removing us would simply make other species flourish, with no more moral significance than removing any other predator."

Your response should: evaluate the scientific claim about food webs; address the uniqueness of human impact on ecosystems; connect to the Bible lesson on stewardship (Week 2 and/or Week 6); and reach a clear, reasoned conclusion. 14 marks
🔗 Cross-week connections: Week 2 Bible — stewardship and imago Dei. Week 6 Bible — Joseph as steward. Science — producers, food webs, energy flow, human impact.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The claim that humans are simply another organism in a food web is scientifically partially correct but deeply incomplete in its implications. It is true that humans occupy a position in food webs — primarily as omnivorous consumers, sometimes as top predators — and it is also true that the removal of any top predator tends to allow prey populations to increase initially. However, the scale and nature of human impact on ecosystems is categorically different from that of any other organism. No other species has transformed habitats on a global scale through agriculture, deforestation, urbanisation, pollution, and climate change. Other predators take what they need; humans have the capacity to alter the fundamental physical conditions that all ecosystems depend on — temperature, rainfall, atmospheric composition, and soil chemistry. The 10% energy rule shows us that the energy pyramid is extremely sensitive to changes at the base: destroying producers — as deforestation and ocean warming do — reduces energy at every subsequent trophic level, potentially collapsing entire food webs. Saying humans are "just another predator" ignores this unique capacity for systematic, large-scale alteration of the pyramid's base. The claim also misses a crucial dimension: moral significance. Unlike every other organism, humans are capable of understanding the consequences of their actions on ecosystems — and therefore of choosing differently. The Bible's concept of stewardship (Genesis 1:28, Week 2) argues that the dominion given to humans over creation carries responsibility, not license: a king's steward manages the kingdom on the king's behalf with care, not exploitation. Joseph's role as steward of Egypt's grain supply in this week's Bible lesson is a model: he managed an enormous food system — essentially an ecosystem resource — with wisdom, foresight, and restraint, ensuring it sustained life rather than depleting it. The scientific evaluation therefore leads to a conclusion that goes beyond science: humans are unique not just in their capacity to harm ecosystems but in their capacity to understand, protect, and restore them. "Removing humans" would not simply allow nature to flourish — it would leave ecosystems in states already profoundly shaped by human activity, requiring human effort to restore. The moral question is not whether to be in the food web, but how to occupy that position responsibly.

3
The lesson notes that Antarctica is the world's largest "desert" despite being the coldest place on Earth. (a) Explain how this is possible — what defines a desert? (b) How does the Antarctic "desert" differ from the Sahara in terms of: precipitation type, temperature range, and what organisms can survive there? (c) What does this comparison teach us about not relying on stereotypes when studying geography? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) A desert is defined by extremely low precipitation (rainfall or snowfall below approximately 250 mm per year) — NOT by heat. Antarctica receives almost no precipitation — the ice that covers it fell over millions of years and barely melts. This makes it the driest large landmass on Earth and therefore the world's largest desert by area. (b) Comparison: Sahara — precipitation as rain (rare but when it comes, liquid); temperatures ranging from 45°C+ in the day to near freezing at night; organisms include camels, snakes, scorpions, cacti. Antarctica — precipitation as snow (virtually none annually); temperatures averaging −40°C to −60°C inland; organisms include penguins, seals, krill (in the surrounding ocean), and specialised algae under ice. (c) The comparison teaches that geographical categories must be defined precisely, not by the most familiar stereotype. "Desert" immediately suggests heat and sand — but the definition is about water scarcity, not temperature. Similarly, "forest" doesn't always mean trees (kelp forests are underwater), and "plains" aren't always flat. Good geographical thinking requires precision in definition and willingness to challenge default mental images with actual evidence.

Science connection: This week's ecosystems lesson (food webs, habitats, energy pyramids) cannot be understood without geography: the lesson itself shows that polar, desert, woodland, coral reef, and grassland habitats all have radically different food webs precisely because of their different physical geographies. The Amazon's extraordinary biodiversity, the polar food chain's simplicity, and the Sahara's sparse web are all direct consequences of geographical conditions. History connection: The Week 2 lesson on Egypt and Mesopotamia — which provided the foundations of all subsequent history lessons — explained civilisational differences almost entirely through geography: the Nile's predictability vs the Tigris-Euphrates unpredictability produced different levels of political stability, different religions, different architectures. Geography was the first cause. Bible connection: Even Joseph's story (this week's Bible) played out in Egypt — a location chosen by God but also a location defined by its geography: only Egypt, with the Nile's agricultural surplus, could store grain for seven years to save a region from famine. Providence, in the biblical narrative, operated through geography. Maths connection: Percentage change calculations this week were applied to population growth — and population distribution on Earth is itself a product of physical geography: most of humanity lives near rivers, coasts, and fertile plains. Counter-argument: One could argue that the Bible's lesson on Joseph has the most lasting importance because it addresses human character, ethics, and the question of how to behave under suffering — which are arguably more universally and personally relevant than where mountains are. Conclusion: Geography provides the stage; the other subjects describe what happens on it. Understanding the physical world is the prerequisite to understanding everything else — which makes it the subject with the broadest and deepest connections to the world we actually inhabit.
