6
SYNOPTIC CHALLENGE — A swimming pool is 3/5 full. Every hour, Pipe A adds 1/8 of the pool and Pipe B drains 1/12 of the pool. After exactly 3 hours, is the pool more or less than 3/4 full? Show every calculation step AND explain in words what you are doing at each step. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Net per hour: LCM(8,12)=24 → 3/24−2/24=1/24. After 3 hours: 3×1/24=3/24=1/8. Final level: 3/5+1/8. LCM(5,8)=40 → 24/40+5/40=29/40. 7
MATHEMATICAL WRITING — Explain, in full sentences suitable for a student who has never learned about fractions, the complete journey from "what is a fraction" (Week 3) to "how to add fractions with unlike denominators" (Week 4). Your explanation must include: why the denominator matters, what equivalent fractions are, what the LCM is and why you need it, and the four steps of the full method. Aim for 6–8 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A fraction represents a part of a whole — the bottom number (denominator) tells you how many equal pieces the whole is divided into, and the top number (numerator) tells you how many of those pieces you have. This means 1/4 and 1/6 are different-sized pieces — you cannot add them directly any more than you can add one large slice of pizza and one small slice and call the result "two slices." To add fractions with different denominators, you need to convert them into the same-sized pieces first — this is what an equivalent fraction is: a fraction that has a different numerator and denominator but represents exactly the same amount. The most efficient way to find the right common denominator is to use the LCM — the Lowest Common Multiple — of the two denominators, because it is the smallest number both denominators divide into evenly, keeping your numbers manageable. The four steps are: (1) find the LCM of the denominators; (2) convert each fraction to an equivalent fraction with the LCM as its new denominator, by multiplying top and bottom by the same number; (3) add the numerators, keeping the denominator the same; (4) simplify the answer by dividing top and bottom by their HCF.

🔗 Three-Week Science Synthesis
4
Weeks 2, 3 & 4 — The complete journey of glucose: starting from a molecule of starch in food, trace the complete process until glucose provides energy inside a MUSCLE cell. You must mention: an enzyme (Week 4), an organ from the digestive system (Week 3), the villi (Week 4), the circulatory system (Week 3), and the mitochondria (Week 2). Write in connected sentences. 8 marks
🔗 Full synoptic: This question spans all three science topics. Cell biology (Week 2) + Organ systems (Week 3) + Digestion/Nutrition (Week 4).
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Starch in food enters the mouth where amylase (Week 4 — enzyme) begins breaking it into simple sugars. In the small intestine (Week 3 — digestive organ), pancreatic amylase completes this breakdown into glucose molecules small enough to be absorbed. Glucose passes through the walls of the villi (Week 4) — tiny finger-like projections that massively increase surface area — into the blood capillaries inside them. The circulatory system (Week 3) transports this glucose-rich blood from the intestine, through the liver, and via the heart to every tissue in the body, including leg muscles. At the muscle, glucose diffuses from the blood into individual muscle cells. Inside each cell, the mitochondria (Week 2 — organelle) carry out cellular respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + ATP. This ATP provides the energy for the muscle to contract. Every system — digestive, circulatory, and cellular — must work together for this to happen.
5
Weeks 2 & 3 — Compare a plant cell and an animal cell. Then explain how the differences connect to what you learned about organ systems in Week 3: why do plants NOT need a circulatory system or digestive system, while animals do? 6 marks Week 2 Week 3
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Three differences: plants have a cell wall (rigid support — no bones needed), chloroplasts (make their own food), and a large central vacuole (stores water, maintains turgidity). Plants do not need a circulatory system because they have no muscle cells that need rapid oxygen delivery — they produce oxygen in their own chloroplasts through photosynthesis, and distribution happens through diffusion and the vascular system (xylem/phloem) not a pumping heart. Plants do not need a digestive system because they make their own glucose through photosynthesis in chloroplasts — they are autotrophs (self-feeders) while animals are heterotrophs (must consume other organisms). The cell structure tells the whole story: the chloroplast in plant cells makes an entire organ system redundant — the factory is inside every plant cell already.

"Biology can be understood at every scale — from a single enzyme to an entire organism — and the key insight is that structure always determines function." 🔗 Full synoptic: Week 2 cell structure → function · Week 3 organ structure → function · Week 4 digestive structure (villi, enzyme active site) → function.

The claim is strongly supported at every level of biological organisation. At the cellular level (Week 2): the mitochondrion's folded inner membrane (cristae) massively increases the surface area available for the reactions of cellular respiration — structure (folding) directly enables function (maximum ATP production). Amylase can break starch but not protein because its active site is specifically shaped for starch molecules.
