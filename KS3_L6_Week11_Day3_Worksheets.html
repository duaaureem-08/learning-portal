🔍 Structured Paragraphs
2
Write a structured paragraph explaining why the prophets' stories (Elijah and Isaiah) add something NEW to the pattern of biblical calling studied this term — specifically regarding the relationship between PUBLIC ministry and PRIVATE struggle. Use specific evidence. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The stories of Elijah and Isaiah add a distinctive dimension to this term's pattern of biblical calling by showing, more explicitly than previous figures studied, that significant public ministry and genuine private struggle can occur in immediate succession within the same person, without one disqualifying or contradicting the other. Elijah's confrontation of Baal's prophets at Mount Carmel represents about as bold and successful a public stand as any biblical figure takes — yet the very next chapter shows him fleeing in fear and falling into despair severe enough to ask for death, with God's response being practical, gentle care rather than correction. Isaiah's calling similarly interweaves public purpose (being sent to speak God's truth) with intensely private reckoning (his own sense of unworthiness before God's holiness) as a NECESSARY PRECEDING STEP to his public ministry, not a separate or contradictory experience. This is significant because it demonstrates that biblical calling does not require or produce a person who is free from ongoing personal vulnerability, doubt, or struggle — the prophets' stories model faithful public ministry as something that coexists with, rather than eliminates, genuine private difficulty, adding realistic depth to what faithful service to God can look like.
3
Write a paragraph comparing how God's presence and communication is described differently in Elijah's story (the "gentle whisper" after dramatic events) versus Isaiah's story (an overwhelming vision of holiness). What pattern or principle might explain why God communicates differently with different people in different circumstances? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
God's communication with Elijah, particularly at Horeb (1 Kings 19), is notably restrained and gentle — deliberately NOT found in the dramatic wind, earthquake, or fire, but in "a gentle whisper," meeting Elijah in his state of genuine exhaustion and despair with quiet, personal comfort rather than more spectacle. This contrasts significantly with Isaiah's experience, where God's presence is communicated through an overwhelming, dramatic vision of holiness — angels, a shaking temple, and an unmistakably powerful sensory encounter (Isaiah 6:1-4). The apparent principle explaining this difference is that the FORM of divine communication seems suited to the specific person's circumstances and needs at that moment, rather than following one fixed pattern: Elijah, already having witnessed dramatic fire from heaven and now needing comfort and restoration in a state of collapse, receives something gentler and more intimate; Isaiah, who has not yet had a personal encounter of this magnitude and needs to grasp the full weight of God's holiness before being commissioned, receives something overwhelming and awe-inspiring. This suggests that divine communication is responsive to genuine human need and context, rather than being a uniform, one-size-fits-all experience — the same God can appropriately communicate very differently depending on what a person's specific situation actually calls for.

"The prophets' stories teach that speaking truth faithfully matters more than whether that truth is received well." Write a full essay response evaluating this statement. You must: refer to at least THREE specific episodes from this week's lesson; compare with at least one other figure studied this term; address the concepts of calling, faithfulness, and outcome; and write a conclusion that takes a clear position. Suggested structure: Introduction (your position) → Evidence para 1 (Mount Carmel's public success) → Evidence para 2 (Elijah's despair despite success) → Evidence para 3 (Isaiah's forewarned resistance) → Comparison with another figure → Conclusion.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The claim that faithful truth-telling matters more than whether that truth is well received is strongly supported by evidence across both Elijah's and Isaiah's stories. First, the Mount Carmel contest (1 Kings 18): while this episode DOES result in an immediate, positive public response ("The LORD — he is God!"), the lesson's broader context shows this positive reception was temporary and fragile — Jezebel's death threat and the nation's underlying instability continue almost immediately afterward, suggesting that even a dramatic, well-received truth does not guarantee lasting transformation. Second, Elijah's despair (1 Kings 19): despite achieving what looks like decisive public success, Elijah's own emotional and spiritual state afterward is one of genuine crisis, showing that even successful, well-received truth-telling carries a real personal cost that "good reception" does not eliminate. Third, and most directly relevant, Isaiah's calling (Isaiah 6:9-10) includes God's explicit forewarning that MUCH of Isaiah's prophetic message will NOT be received or understood by his intended audience — yet Isaiah still responds "Here am I. Send me!" knowing this in advance. This is the clearest evidence for the claim: Isaiah's willingness to serve is not contingent on any expectation of successful reception; faithfulness to the calling itself is what matters, regardless of predicted outcome. Compared with David (Week 10), whose story centres on the CONTENT of repentance and honest confession achieving restoration, the prophets' stories more explicitly separate the ACT of faithful truth-telling from its RECEPTION — David's honesty is received well by God, resulting in restoration, whereas Isaiah's honesty is explicitly forewarned to be received poorly by his human audience. This distinction supports the claim: across the prophets' stories, faithfulness in DELIVERING truth, not the audience's response to it, is consistently presented as the measure of a person's obedience and integrity before God.

3
Explain why pronoun clarity is PARTICULARLY important in historical and scientific writing (as opposed to casual conversation), where confusion about WHO or WHAT did something could seriously mislead a reader about facts. Give one specific example from this week's History or Science content. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Pronoun clarity is particularly important in historical and scientific writing because these subjects rely on the reader accurately understanding WHO performed which specific action, or WHICH substance/entity caused a particular effect — unlike casual conversation, where context and follow-up questions can quickly resolve ambiguity, written historical and scientific texts are often read without the ability to ask the author for clarification, meaning any vague pronoun reference risks permanently misleading the reader about actual facts. For example, in a sentence like "The acid reacted with the metal, and it produced a gas," if "it" is ambiguous between the acid, the metal, or the reaction itself, a science student could come away with a genuinely incorrect understanding of which substance is chemically responsible for producing the gas — a factual error with real consequences for understanding the underlying chemistry. Similarly, in history, a sentence like "When the explorers met the indigenous leaders, they made an agreement, but they broke it soon after" leaves entirely unclear WHICH group made the agreement and WHICH group broke it — a potentially serious historical misrepresentation if the pronoun reference is wrongly assumed by the reader.

Section B

🔍 Comparative Analysis
2
Compare the Silk Road and the Age of Exploration as two different periods/mechanisms of globalisation. What is SIMILAR about their overall effects, and what is DIFFERENT about how contact between civilisations happened in each case? 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Similarities: Both the Silk Road and the Age of Exploration resulted in significant transfer of goods, ideas, technology, and unfortunately disease between previously separate or less-connected regions of the world; both represent major historical examples of increasing global connectivity (globalisation) occurring centuries before the term was invented; both involved extended, sustained contact rather than a single brief event. Differences: The Silk Road primarily connected already-known, established civilisations (China, the Middle East, Europe) that had at least some awareness of each other's existence, through overland and established sea trade routes, over many centuries of relatively gradual, incremental contact. The Age of Exploration, by contrast, brought entirely NEW, previously unknown-to-each-other civilisations into sudden, often abrupt contact (most dramatically, Europeans and indigenous peoples of the Americas, who had no prior awareness of each other's existence), via ocean voyages specifically undertaken to find new routes and territories, producing a much more sudden, disruptive form of contact — leading to consequences like devastating disease impact precisely because there had been NO prior gradual exposure or immunity-building contact of the kind that had occurred along the more established Silk Road routes.
