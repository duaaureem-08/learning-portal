"The Lord will fight for you; you need only to be still."
Exodus 14:14 — NIV
1
Write out the memory verse in full. Then explain: (a) what was happening to the Israelites at the exact moment Moses said these words, and (b) why the phrase "you need only to be still" is unusual advice in a crisis. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"The Lord will fight for you; you need only to be still." (Exodus 14:14, NIV). (a) The Israelites were trapped between Pharaoh's pursuing army of 600 chariots behind them and the Red Sea in front of them — with no visible escape route. They were terrified and blamed Moses for leading them there. (b) Normal crisis advice would be "run," "fight," or "find a solution." God's instruction to "be still" is counter-intuitive — it requires trusting that God will act rather than using your own strength. The lesson notes this was said "at the most hopeless-looking moment in the whole story," which is exactly what makes it so powerful and memorable.
2
Match each vocabulary word to its correct meaning: oppression · Passover · covenant · intercession · calling · deliverance 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
oppression = treating a group of people in a cruel, unjust, and controlling way using power against them — the Egyptians' systematic cruelty toward the Israelites. Passover = the name of both the final plague and the special protective meal the Israelites ate the night before leaving Egypt; refers to God "passing over" and protecting marked homes from the final plague. covenant = a serious, binding agreement or promise — much stronger than a normal contract. God's covenant with Abraham is what He is honouring by rescuing the Israelites. intercession = praying or speaking on behalf of someone else. Moses intercedes between God and the Israelites many times. calling = a deep sense that God has chosen you for a specific purpose or task. Moses receives his calling through the burning bush. deliverance = rescue from danger, captivity, or suffering — God "delivers" His people from Egypt.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Water to blood — targets the Nile River and the god Hapi (god of the Nile floods), Egypt's most sacred resource. 2. Frogs — targets the frog goddess Heqet, a symbol of fertility. 3. 🔍 Understanding & Explanation
4
The lesson describes Moses giving FIVE excuses at the burning bush — and God answering each one. Fill in the table below, then answer the follow-up question. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Excuse 1: Moses felt insecurity — he was no longer a prince, just a shepherd. God answered: "I will be with you" — not arguing with Moses' self-assessment, just promising His presence is enough. Excuse 2: Moses felt uncertainty about how to explain who sent him. God answered: "I AM WHO I AM" — His eternal, self-existent name. Excuse 3: Moses felt fear of rejection. God answered by giving three miraculous signs: the staff becoming a snake, a hand made leprous then healed, and water turned to blood. Excuse 4: Moses felt inadequacy about his speech. God answered: "Who gave human beings their mouths? I will help you speak." Excuse 5: Moses showed pure reluctance — he simply didn't want to go. God was angry, but still provided Aaron as Moses' spokesman — showing God doesn't give up on people even when they resist Him.
5
The lesson says the ten plagues were NOT random — they were "a theological battle." Explain what this means. Why did God choose to target the things Egypt worshipped? What was He proving? Write at least 4 sentences. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A theological battle means the plagues were not just natural disasters or political pressure — they were a direct confrontation between the God of Israel and the gods of Egypt. Ancient Egypt was deeply religious, with gods connected to the Nile, the sun, fertility, and livestock. Each plague targeted something Egyptians worshipped or depended on — the Nile (god Hapi), frogs (goddess Heqet), cattle (god Apis), and finally Ra the sun god through the plague of darkness. By systematically overpowering each Egyptian god in turn, God was demonstrating that He is the only true God, and that no force in creation — however revered — can stand against Him. The lesson calls this "a power display put on the largest, most public stage yet." This explains why Pharaoh's own magicians said "this is the finger of God" by plague 3 — even Egypt's own religious specialists recognised they were outmatched.
6
Describe the specific instructions God gave the Israelites for the first Passover (Exodus 12). For EACH of the four elements below, explain what the instruction was AND what the lesson says it symbolises. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. A perfect lamb without defect — selected on the 10th of the month, kept until the 14th. Symbolises something of full, uncorrupted value being given in place of the sinner. The idea of a blameless sacrifice runs throughout the entire Old Testament. 2. Blood on the two doorposts and top of the door frame — forming a cross-like shape. When God saw the blood, He would "pass over" that house. The blood was the specific distinguishing mark between a protected house and an unprotected one. 3. Bitter herbs (maror) and unleavened bread (matzah) — the herbs to remember the bitterness of slavery; the bread without yeast because they were leaving in such a hurry there was no time for dough to rise. These physical, edible symbols turn the event into a re-lived experience. 4. Sandals on, staff in hand, eating in a hurry — they were to eat packed and ready to move immediately. The lesson says: "Faith, in this picture, looks like readiness. They did not eat and then start packing."

7
The lesson presents a comparison of four Old Testament figures: Noah, Abraham, Joseph, and Moses. Using the lesson's comparison table, explain what is UNIQUE about Moses' story compared to the other three — in terms of (a) the source of hardship, (b) what faith looked like, and (c) the scale of what God accomplished. Write at least 5 sentences. 5 marks
🔗 Cross-week link: Weeks 4–7 trace a pattern of faith across the Old Testament. What is the lesson building toward?
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Noah faced a natural disaster; Abraham faced God's own call to leave home; Joseph faced human cruelty and injustice. Moses faced something uniquely combined: both political oppression on a national scale (an enslaved people under the most powerful empire on earth) AND an impossible divine assignment. His hardship was not personal — it was carried on behalf of an entire nation. (b) Faith for Noah meant building a boat; for Abraham, leaving without a destination; for Joseph, maintaining integrity through years of slavery. For Moses, faith looked like going back to Pharaoh ten times after ten rejections, and standing at the Red Sea with an army behind him and a sea in front and declaring "The Lord will fight for you." The lesson says this is "Moses at his finest — terrified himself, but choosing to declare faith rather than panic." (c) Noah's story rescued one family; Abraham's launched one covenant; Joseph's saved one famine-era region. Moses' story delivered an entire nation of potentially over a million people from the greatest empire of the ancient world — and destroyed Pharaoh's entire army in a single night. It was God's power on the most public possible stage, and the lesson notes it is referenced over 100 times in the rest of the Bible. This is why the Exodus is called "the defining event of the entire Old Testament."
8
The lesson discusses "Pharaoh's hardened heart" — noting that the Bible says BOTH that God hardened Pharaoh's heart AND that Pharaoh hardened his own heart. The lesson uses the analogy of the sun and clay vs wax. (a) Explain the analogy and what it means. (b) Is Pharaoh responsible for his own choices? Explain using the lesson's reasoning. Write at least 4 sentences. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) The lesson explains that the same sun both melts wax and hardens clay — the RESULT depends on the nature of the material, not the sun. Applied to Pharaoh: God's power and the plagues are like the sun — the same divine pressure. Pharaoh's own proud, stubborn nature means that instead of "melting" (repenting and releasing the people), he "hardened" (became more resistant). The external force was the same; the internal response differed because of who Pharaoh already was. (b) Yes, Pharaoh is responsible — and the lesson makes this clear by noting that multiple times Pharaoh "hardened his own heart" (e.g. 8:15 — after the frogs, when the pressure lifted, he broke his promise himself). The scholars' understanding, as described in the lesson, is that "Pharaoh's own stubborn nature — choosing power and pride over truth — was the same spiritual condition that God used as the vehicle for His judgement." Pharaoh chose pride every time he had a chance to choose otherwise. He is not a passive victim of God's manipulation — he is a person whose character, consistently revealed under pressure, was a stubborn refusal to acknowledge a power greater than himself.

📚 Vocabulary & Identification
💡 ESL Tip: The lesson teaches that purpose is the REASON a text was written (what the writer wants to DO), while audience is WHO the text is written FOR. These two things work together — identifying both is the first step to reading any non-fiction text well.
1
Define each of the following vocabulary words from the lesson. Use your own words — do not copy the lesson directly. 6 marks

non-fiction · tone · bias · inference · source · headline
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
non-fiction = writing about real events, real people, and verifiable facts — not made-up stories. Includes news articles, reports, biographies, speeches, and textbooks. tone = the attitude or "feeling" in a piece of writing — how it sounds. Can be formal, informal, urgent, humorous, serious, or emotional; created through word choice, sentence length, and punctuation. bias = when a writer shows a one-sided viewpoint — either leaving out information that challenges their argument or using language that strongly favours one side. Bias is not always intentional. inference = a conclusion that is not directly stated in the text but that you can reasonably work out from the evidence given. It sits between fact and opinion. source = where information comes from; a reliable source has an author, date, verifiable organisation, and matches other reliable sources. headline = the title of a newspaper or online article, in large bold text; carefully chosen to attract readers, often using wordplay or strong emotion.
2
The lesson lists FIVE common purposes for non-fiction writing. Name all five and for each one, give a real-world example of a text type that mainly uses that purpose. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Purpose 1: To inform — a news article about a flood, a weather report, a Wikipedia entry. Purpose 2: To persuade — a charity appeal, an opinion column, an advertisement, a political speech. Purpose 3: To explain — an instruction manual, an explainer article about how vaccines work, a textbook chapter. Purpose 4: To entertain — a travel article, a witty personal essay, a nature documentary script (even non-fiction can aim to be enjoyable). Purpose 5: To instruct — a recipe, a user guide, a "how-to" article, safety instructions.
3
Decide whether each statement below is a FACT, OPINION, or INFERENCE. Give one reason for each decision. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) FACT — it contains a specific, measurable statistic that can be checked against research data. Numbers and measurements are hallmarks of factual claims. b) OPINION — "most serious" is a value judgement that cannot be objectively measured; different people might rank other problems as more serious. Contains no specific data. c) INFERENCE — the 40% rise is the fact; "public interest is growing" is a reasonable conclusion drawn from that fact, but not directly stated. d) FACT — a specific event with a specific location and date that can be verified. e) OPINION — "should not have been held" expresses a personal judgement about what ought to have happened; people can legitimately disagree. f) INFERENCE — winning three international awards is the fact; concluding the scientist is "highly respected" is a reasonable but unstated deduction.

Headline: OUR OCEANS ARE DROWNING IN PLASTIC — AND WE ARE TO BLAME

Every minute of every day, the equivalent of a rubbish truck full of plastic is dumped into our oceans. Scientists estimate that by 2050, there could be more plastic in the sea than fish. Surely this is a crisis we can no longer ignore?

What is the PRIMARY purpose of this article? What is a SECONDARY purpose? Provide specific evidence from the text to support BOTH of your answers. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Primary purpose: to persuade the reader to care about plastic pollution and change their behaviour. Evidence: emotional language throughout ("drowning," "devastating"), the direct address in the final paragraph ("You have the power to change this"), the call to action ("Our oceans cannot wait"), and the rhetorical question ("Surely this is a crisis we can no longer ignore?"). Secondary purpose: to inform — the article also provides facts and statistics to support its argument. Identify the intended AUDIENCE for this article. Give THREE specific pieces of evidence from the text that support your conclusion — referring to vocabulary level, tone, and the type of publication this article likely came from. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Intended audience: general adult readers of a national newspaper or news website. Evidence 1 (vocabulary): The vocabulary is accessible but not simplified — words like "microplastics" and "marine biologist" are used without detailed definition, suggesting a reader with general knowledge but not specialist expertise. This conversational directness suits a general newspaper audience rather than an academic or technical one. Evidence 3 (other clues): The inverted pyramid structure (most important facts first), the use of a named expert with institutional affiliation (Dr Cruz, University of Oxford), and the emotional headline all point to a mainstream quality newspaper format.
6
The lesson teaches you to identify language features AND explain their effect on the reader. For EACH language feature below, find an example from the article and explain the effect on the reader. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Rhetorical question: "Surely this is a crisis we can no longer ignore?" The word "surely" implies the answer is obvious — making the reader feel that disagreeing would be unreasonable or irresponsible. This draws the reader into agreeing with the writer's viewpoint without them realising they're being persuaded. b) Emotive language: "drowning" in the headline applies a word usually associated with death and panic to describe the oceans, creating a sense of life-threatening urgency. "Devastating" goes beyond "serious" or "significant" — it implies total destruction. The phrase "our own bodies" in the final paragraph makes the threat personal and physically immediate. Together, these words make the reader feel alarmed and involved rather than just informed. c) Expert quotation: "We are witnessing the most devastating pollution event in human history," said Dr Elena Cruz, marine biologist at the University of Oxford. The writer includes Dr Cruz's full title and institution to add credibility — the reader thinks: "this isn't just the journalist's opinion; an expert from Oxford agrees." Including specific institutional detail makes the quotation harder to dismiss.

⚖️ Critical Analysis & Extended Response
7
The lesson teaches five structural features of non-fiction texts. For the plastic article above, identify how EACH structural feature is used (or how the writer has chosen not to use it). Explain why each choice serves the article's purpose. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Headline: "OUR OCEANS ARE DROWNING IN PLASTIC — AND WE ARE TO BLAME" uses emotive language ("drowning"), a capital letters format for emphasis, and direct accusation ("WE ARE TO BLAME") to immediately engage the reader's emotions and sense of responsibility. 2. Opening paragraph: Uses the "inverted pyramid" — the most urgent fact (a rubbish truck of plastic every minute) comes first, followed immediately by a dramatic future projection (more plastic than fish by 2050), then a rhetorical question. This "hook" is designed to alarm the reader before they can disengage. 3. Body paragraphs: Each paragraph develops one idea — expert evidence (paragraph 2), countering the recycling argument with hard statistics (paragraph 3). The transition "But the uncomfortable truth is..." signals the writer is about to challenge an opposing view, giving the argument a debating structure that feels credible. 4. No images in this extract: This tells you the text is designed to stand alone as written argument — the emotive language is doing the work that an image of a polluted ocean might otherwise do. In a full article, you would expect images of plastic-covered beaches or seabirds, which would amplify the emotional impact. 5. Concluding paragraph: Ends with a direct call to action — "You have the power to change this" followed by three specific actions (rule of three) and a final short sentence: "Our oceans cannot wait." Short, urgent final sentences are a persuasive technique — they leave the reader with a sense of urgency, not a complex argument to mull over.
8
The lesson says: "Critical readers always ask: what is this writer NOT telling me?" Apply this to the plastic article. Identify at least TWO pieces of information the article does NOT include that would give a more balanced picture. Then explain: does the absence of this information make the article untrustworthy, or can a persuasive article still be reliable? Write at least 5 sentences. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Missing information 1: Who is Dr Elena Cruz and how recent is her research? The article quotes her but does not provide a date or the specific study. "We are witnessing the most devastating pollution event in human history" is a very strong claim — a critical reader would want to know when this study was conducted, whether it was peer-reviewed, and whether other scientists agree with this assessment. Missing information 2: No acknowledgement of progress or countervailing evidence. The article briefly mentions that "some argue recycling programmes will solve the problem" only to dismiss this. This selective presentation makes the situation seem entirely hopeless, which may not be accurate. Does absence make it untrustworthy? The statistics the article cites (9% recycling rate, 8 million tonnes) are verifiable facts that appear in scientific literature. The article is biased — it presents one side — but the facts it includes are real. A critical reader should verify the facts independently, seek out additional sources with different perspectives, and then form their own judgement. The lesson teaches us that bias is not the same as lying — but it is a reason to read carefully and widely.

3 Difficulty LevelsRatio & ProportionKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Vocabulary, definitions, and basic ratio problems. Show all working.
📚 Vocabulary & Basic Skills
💡 ESL Tip: The lesson teaches a crucial distinction: a ratio compares PARTS to PARTS (e.g. 3 boys to 5 girls = 3:5). A proportion compares a PART to the WHOLE (e.g. 3 boys out of 8 total = 3/8). Many students mix these up — always check which comparison you are being asked to make.
1
Define each term in your own words and give a numerical example DIFFERENT from the lesson examples. 7 marks

ratio · proportion · simplify a ratio · equivalent ratios · share in a ratio · unitary method · direct proportion
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
ratio = a comparison of two or more quantities showing how many times one is bigger than the other; written with a colon; no units. Example: In a bag with 5 apples and 3 oranges, the ratio of apples to oranges is 5:3. proportion = what fraction of a whole something is; compares a part to the total. Example: In the same bag, the proportion of apples is 5/8. simplify a ratio = dividing all parts by their HCF. Example: 10:15 → HCF=5 → 2:3. equivalent ratios = ratios expressing the same relationship with different numbers. Example: 2:3, 4:6, 10:15 are all equivalent. share in a ratio = dividing a total according to a given ratio using the 3-step method. Example: Share £20 in ratio 1:4 → 5 parts → £4 and £16. unitary method = finding the value of one unit first, then multiplying. Example: If 3 notebooks cost £4.50, one notebook = £1.50; six notebooks = £9.00. direct proportion = two quantities that increase or decrease by the same factor. Example: The more metres of fabric, the greater the cost — doubling metres doubles the cost.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) 14:21 → HCF=7 → 2:3. b) 30:45 → HCF=15 → 2:3. c) 16:24:40 → HCF=8 → 2:3:5. d) 2.4:3.6 → ×10 → 24:36 → HCF=12 → 2:3. e) 5cm:2m → convert 2m = 200cm → 5:200 → HCF=5 → 1:40. f) 750g:1.5kg → convert 1.5kg = 1500g → 750:1500 → HCF=750 → 1:2. 🔴 HardSection C — Multi-step problems and reasoning. Explain your thinking as well as calculating.
⚖️ Problem Solving & Reasoning
6
A student says: "To find the proportion of boys in a class with ratio boys:girls = 3:5, I just write 3/5." Explain the student's mistake. Write the correct proportion as a fraction and as a percentage. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The student's mistake: 3/5 would be the RATIO of boys to girls — not the proportion of boys. A proportion compares a part to the WHOLE. Total parts = 3+5 = 8. The proportion of boys = 3 out of 8 = 3/8. As a percentage: 3÷8 = 0.375 = 37.5%. Writing 3/5 would mean "3 boys for every 5 girls" (the ratio), not "boys make up 3/5 of the class" (which would be untrue — they make up 3/8).
7
A gold alloy is made of gold and silver in the ratio 18:6. (a) Simplify the ratio. (b) What percentage of the alloy is gold? (c) A jeweller has 360g of the alloy. How many grams of gold does it contain? (d) She wants to make a piece that is 75% gold. She has 270g of pure gold. How much silver does she need to add? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) 18:6 → HCF=6 → 3:1. (b) Total parts = 3+1 = 4. Gold = 3/4 = 75%. (c) Gold in 360g = 3/4 × 360 = 270g gold; 90g silver. (d) If she has 270g pure gold and wants 75% gold in the total: 270g = 75% of total → total = 270 ÷ 0.75 = 360g total. Silver needed = 360 − 270 = 90g. This confirms the ratio holds — 270g gold : 90g silver = 3:1 ✓.
8
Explain clearly — using an example — the difference between direct and inverse proportion. Then explain why the simple unitary method "divide, then multiply" works for direct proportion but NOT for inverse proportion. What must you do differently for inverse proportion? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Direct proportion: two quantities increase or decrease by the same factor — if you double one, you double the other. Example: buying more apples always costs more — 4 apples at 20p each cost 80p; 8 apples cost 160p. The ratio between cost and quantity stays constant. Inverse proportion: when one quantity increases, the other decreases proportionally. Example: more workers sharing a job means fewer days each — 5 painters take 6 days; 10 painters take only 3 days. As workers double, time halves. Why "divide then multiply" works for direct but not inverse: In direct proportion, you can find the "rate per one unit" because the relationship is multiplicative in one direction. For example, if 3 books cost £9, one book costs £3, so 7 books cost £21. The rate per unit is constant. In inverse proportion, dividing to find "one unit" and then multiplying gives you the wrong answer. You must instead calculate the TOTAL (e.g. total worker-days = workers × days), then divide that total by the new number of workers. This works because the product of the two quantities is always constant in inverse proportion — this is the invariant quantity.

3 Difficulty LevelsChemistryKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Vocabulary, definitions, and particle model basics. Use your lesson notes.
📚 Vocabulary & Recall
💡 ESL Tip: The lesson teaches the key principle: the state of matter depends on two things — how far apart particles are and how fast they move. Temperature controls both. When you heat a substance, you give energy to the particles, making them move faster and further apart.
1
Define each vocabulary term in your own words and give one everyday example for each. 6 marks

matter · particle · melting point · boiling point · evaporation · sublimation
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
matter = anything that has mass and takes up space. Example: air, water, rock — everything around you is matter. particle = an extremely tiny piece of matter (atom or molecule); too small to see. Example: the water molecules in a glass of water are particles. melting point = the specific temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. Example: ice melts at 0°C — this is water's melting point. boiling point = the temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas rapidly throughout the whole liquid. Example: water boils at 100°C at sea level. evaporation = when a liquid slowly turns into gas at the surface, at temperatures below boiling point. Example: puddles disappear after rain, even without boiling — the water evaporates slowly. sublimation = when a solid turns directly into a gas without going through the liquid stage. Example: dry ice (solid CO₂) sublimates — it goes straight from solid to gas, appearing to "smoke."
2
Complete this full comparison table of the three states of matter, including particle arrangement, movement, shape, volume, and compressibility. Give one example for each state. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
SOLID: Particles in tight, regular rows — very close together. Movement: vibrate on the spot only. Fixed shape: YES. Fixed volume: YES. Compressed: NO (particles already touching). Example: ice, rock, wood. LIQUID: Particles close together but randomly arranged — no fixed pattern. Movement: flow and slide past each other. Fixed shape: NO (takes container's shape). Fixed volume: YES. Compressed: barely (particles still close). Example: water, oil, mercury. GAS: Particles spread very far apart, random and widely spaced. Movement: move very fast in all directions, bouncing off each other and container walls. Fixed shape: NO (fills any container). Fixed volume: NO. Compressed: YES (lots of empty space). Example: steam, oxygen, carbon dioxide.
3
Name every change of state (there are 6 including sublimation). For each one: name it, state which change it involves (e.g. solid → liquid), and state whether heat is absorbed or released. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Melting: Solid → Liquid. Heat ABSORBED (particles gain energy to move freely). 2. Freezing: Liquid → Solid. Heat RELEASED (particles lose energy, slow down, lock together). 3. Evaporation: Liquid → Gas (slowly, at surface, below boiling point). Heat ABSORBED. 4. Boiling: Liquid → Gas (rapidly, throughout, at boiling point). Heat ABSORBED. 5. Condensation: Gas → Liquid. Heat RELEASED. 6. Sublimation: Solid → Gas directly (no liquid stage). Heat ABSORBED. Memory pattern: going from more ordered to less ordered (solid→liquid→gas) always absorbs heat. Going in reverse always releases heat.

📈 Heating Curves & Particle Explanations
4
The heating curve for water has FIVE sections (A–E). Describe what is happening in each section — in terms of both temperature AND particles. Then answer the follow-up question. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Section A: Temperature rises as ice warms up. Particles are solid, vibrating faster and faster but still locked in their positions. Section B: Temperature stays FLAT at 0°C even though heat continues to be added. The energy is being used to BREAK THE BONDS between particles so they can move freely as liquid — not to increase temperature. This is why the line is flat. Section C: Temperature rises again. Particles are now liquid and moving faster and faster, sliding past each other. Section D: Temperature stays FLAT at 100°C. All the energy is now being used to overcome the remaining forces between liquid particles so they can escape as gas — not to raise temperature. Section E: Temperature rises again as the gas (steam) gets hotter. Particles move very fast in all directions. FOLLOW-UP: The energy absorbed during a change of state is called latent heat — specifically, latent heat of fusion (at melting point) and latent heat of vaporisation (at boiling point).
5
Explain the difference between evaporation and boiling using your particle model knowledge. Include: (a) where in the liquid each process happens, (b) at what temperature each can occur, and (c) one everyday example of each. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Evaporation happens at the SURFACE only — particles at the top of the liquid with enough energy escape into the air as gas. Boiling happens throughout the ENTIRE liquid simultaneously — bubbles of gas form throughout, not just at the surface. (b) Evaporation can occur at ANY temperature below boiling point — it is a slow, continuous process even at room temperature. Boiling occurs only at the specific boiling point (100°C for water at sea level) — it requires enough energy to overcome all the forces between particles throughout the whole liquid. (c) Evaporation example: wet clothes drying on a washing line at room temperature — the water slowly escapes from the cloth surface without boiling. Boiling example: a kettle reaching 100°C with rapid bubbling throughout the water, turning it to steam quickly.

⚖️ Critical Thinking — The Particle Model
6
The lesson states: "The fact that a model has limitations does not mean it is wrong or useless." Using the lesson's map analogy, explain what a scientific model IS, why the particle model is useful, AND give THREE specific limitations of the particle model. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A scientific model is a simplified way of explaining how something works — it doesn't capture every detail but it's still extremely useful. The lesson's analogy: a school map doesn't show every brick, person, or pipe, but it's still very useful for finding your way around. Similarly, the particle model simplifies reality but explains most of what we observe. Why it's useful: it explains why solids keep their shape (particles locked in position), why liquids flow and take the container's shape (particles move freely but stay close), why gases fill any space (particles move fast and far apart), why gases can be compressed (lots of empty space between particles), and why heating raises temperature (particles move faster). Limitation 1: The model treats all particles as identical small spheres — but real atoms have complex shapes and electrical charges that affect how they interact. Limitation 2: The model doesn't show the forces between particles — it can't explain why particles attract or repel each other, which is essential for understanding why some substances dissolve in others and some don't. Limitation 3: The model doesn't explain plasma — a fourth state of matter found in stars and lightning — because plasma involves ionised particles with very different behaviour from the simple sphere model predicts.
7
The lesson says changes of state are REVERSIBLE PHYSICAL changes, not chemical changes. Explain clearly: (a) what makes a change PHYSICAL (not chemical), (b) why melting ice is physical but burning wood is chemical, and (c) what remains the same when water changes between solid, liquid, and gas. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) A physical change alters the form or appearance of a substance but does NOT create new substances — the atoms or molecules are rearranged but not permanently changed. Physical changes are usually reversible. (b) Melting ice is physical: the H₂O molecules are the same whether in ice, liquid water, or steam — only their arrangement and energy level change. You can freeze water back to ice endlessly. Burning wood is chemical: the wood reacts with oxygen to form completely new substances (carbon dioxide, water vapour, and ash). You cannot "un-burn" wood to get the original wood back — new substances have been formed. (c) When water changes state, the chemical identity remains the same — it is always H₂O. The molecules themselves are unchanged. Only their arrangement (how ordered they are) and movement (how fast and how far they move) changes with temperature.

🔍 Understanding & Explanation
3
The lesson lists FIVE factors that affect the climate of a place (beyond just latitude). For each factor, explain what it is AND give a specific example from the lesson to illustrate it. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Factor 1 (Latitude): Near the equator, the sun's rays hit at a steep angle — concentrated and warm. Near the poles, the same rays hit at a shallow angle — spread over a larger area, less concentrated, and much colder. Example: this is why the equator is always hot and the poles are always cold, regardless of season. Factor 2 (Altitude): Temperature drops approximately 6.5°C for every 1,000 metres of elevation. Example: Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania is near the equator (which is hot), yet its peak has permanent snow and ice because it is so high. Factor 3 (Distance from the sea / Maritime vs Continental): The sea warms and cools slowly, so coastal areas have milder, wetter weather year-round. Inland areas have extreme ranges. Example: London (maritime — mild and wet) vs Moscow (same latitude but continental — very cold winters, hot summers). Factor 4 (Ocean Currents): Warm currents carry heat from the tropics. Example: the Gulf Stream carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico to Western Europe — this is why the UK has a mild climate despite being at the same latitude as parts of Canada that are buried in snow. Factor 5 (Prevailing Winds): Winds that blow over oceans carry moisture; winds over deserts carry dry air. Example: the rain shadow effect — mountains force air up (causing rain on one side) and dry air descends on the other side, creating desert conditions.
4
The lesson teaches that "climate zone → vegetation → biome." Explain this chain of causes for the TROPICAL RAINFOREST biome, then for the HOT DESERT biome. For each: explain the climate conditions, what vegetation they allow, and what animals can survive as a result. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
TROPICAL RAINFOREST: Climate conditions — hot year-round (25–30°C), very high rainfall (2,000mm+), near the equator where sunlight is intense. Because conditions are warm, wet, and sunny year-round, photosynthesis can occur continuously. This allows dense, multi-layered vegetation: tall trees compete to reach sunlight in the canopy; below them are smaller trees, shrubs, and ground plants. This abundance of plant life (producers) supports an extraordinary diversity of animals — insects (primary consumers), birds, mammals, reptiles — and the high biodiversity supports many levels of predator. The rainforest has the widest base on any food pyramid. HOT DESERT: Climate conditions — extreme heat in the day (up to 50°C), cold at night, under 250mm of rainfall per year. Very few plants can survive — only those with specialist adaptations like cacti (thick waxy skin to prevent water loss, water stored in the stem, deep or wide roots to collect scarce rain). Because the producer base is so thin and scattered, very few primary consumers can be supported, which means very few secondary or tertiary consumers. Desert food chains are short and simple; populations at every level are small. The desert is the opposite of the rainforest in terms of biodiversity.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The friend is confusing weather (one April snow event — a short-term, specific event) with climate (long-term patterns over at least 30 years). The lesson specifically addresses this: "A single cold year does not disprove a long-term warming trend, just as a single hot day in winter does not prove anything about climate." Climate change refers to a long-term shift in these patterns, not the temperature on a single April day. The friend's error is treating a temporary weather event as evidence about a 100-year trend — these are simply different timescales and cannot be compared.
