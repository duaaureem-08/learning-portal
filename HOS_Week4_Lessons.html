📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Learn these words before you continue
ark
A huge boat built to survive a flood. The Hebrew word tebah means "box" or "chest" — a vessel built for protection and survival, not for sailing or exploring.
covenant
A solemn, binding PROMISE — often between God and people. Stronger than an ordinary promise. God's covenant with Noah was a promise He made to the whole earth.
righteous
Living in right relationship with God — doing what is right, even when others around you do not. Noah was described as righteous compared to his generation.
corrupt
Morally rotten or spoiled — full of violence, dishonesty and evil. Genesis describes the world before the flood as deeply corrupt.
faith
Trust in God shown through ACTION — especially when you cannot yet see the result. Faith is not just a feeling; it changes what you DO.
obedience
Doing what God (or a trusted authority) instructs — even when it is difficult, takes a long time, or does not make sense to others.
generation
All the people living during a particular period of time. "Noah's generation" means the people who were alive at the same time as Noah.
judgment
A decision or action that responds to right and wrong. In the Bible, God's judgment is His response to persistent evil — but it is never separated from mercy.
🌍 The World Before the Flood — Why Did God Judge It?
⚠️
Genesis 6:5 describes humanity's wickedness as having reached an extreme point — every inclination of people's hearts had become corrupt. But Genesis 6:9 describes Noah very differently: he was righteous and walked faithfully with God.
🌍 Noah's Generation
Violence and corruption had spread across the whole earth
People's thoughts and actions were consistently turned toward evil
No one else is described as living for God
God's judgment is described as a grieving response, not a casual one
🙏 Noah
Described as "righteous" and "blameless among the people of his time"
"Walked faithfully with God" — a close, ongoing relationship
Stood out as different from everyone around him
Chosen to carry forward God's purposes for humanity and creation
💡
KS3 thinking point: God's judgment in this story is not random or sudden — it is described as a response to a long pattern of corruption. At the same time, God always provides a way of escape. Noah and the ark show that judgment and mercy appear TOGETHER in this story, not as opposites.
🔨 Building the Ark — Faith in Action
🔑
The golden idea of this lesson: "Faith is acting on what God says BEFORE you see the result." Noah received instructions to build an enormous ark — with no rain yet falling, no flood yet visible, and (according to Jewish tradition) the construction took around 100 years. Imagine the questions from neighbours over generations: "Why are you building a giant boat on dry land?"
📐
According to Genesis 6:15, God gave Noah exact measurements: 300 cubits long, 50 cubits wide, 30 cubits high. A cubit is roughly the length of a forearm — about 45 cm. That makes the ark approximately 137 metres long, 23 metres wide, and 14 metres high.

🌧️ The Flood — Judgment and Mercy Together
Day 1
The Rain Begins
Genesis 7:11–12 — the rain falls for 40 days and 40 nights. Noah, his wife, his three sons and their wives (8 people total) and pairs of animals enter the ark, and God shuts the door.
Days 1–150
The Waters Rise and Prevail
Genesis 7:19–20 — the floodwaters rise until even the highest mountains are covered. The waters remain high for 150 days before beginning to recede.
~Month 7
The Ark Rests on Mount Ararat
Genesis 8:4 — as the waters recede, the ark comes to rest on the mountains of Ararat. The long wait for dry land continues for months afterward.

The Raven and the Dove
Genesis 8:6–12 — Noah sends out a raven (it does not return), then a dove (it returns with nothing, then later returns carrying an olive leaf — a sign that new life had begun on the earth).
✅
Both themes together: The flood is described as God's response to corruption (justice) — but the ark itself, given freely as a way of escape, shows mercy. Noah's family and the animals are not destroyed — they are SAVED. The story holds both ideas without one cancelling out the other.
🌈 The Covenant — God's Promise and Its Sign
📜
After the flood, Genesis 9:8–17 describes God making a covenant — not only with Noah, but with "every living creature" and with the earth itself. The promise: God will never again destroy the earth with a flood.

🌈
The sign of the covenant: Genesis 9:13 — God sets the rainbow in the sky as the sign of this covenant. Every time a rainbow appears — even thousands of years later — it points back to this promise. This is the FIRST of several major covenants in the Bible (later: Abraham, Moses, David, and the New Covenant through Jesus).
🎯 What Faith and Obedience Look Like — Application
1
Faith often means acting before you have proof
Noah built the ark without seeing rain first. Faith is forward-looking — it trusts a reliable source (God's word) ahead of visible evidence.
2
Obedience can take a long time and feel unrewarded at first
Building the ark may have taken decades. Real obedience is not always immediately validated — sometimes it requires patience across a long period.
3
Doing right when others do wrong takes courage
Noah's righteousness stood out precisely because it was uncommon. Standing apart from a crowd — for the right reasons — is one of the themes this story explores.
4
God's promises are meant to be remembered
The rainbow was given as a lasting, visible reminder of God's covenant. Symbols and signs help people remember important truths across generations.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these — they are the foundation of correct sentence writing
clause
A group of words containing a SUBJECT and a VERB. "The dog barked" is a clause — subject = "the dog," verb = "barked."
independent clause
A clause that can stand ALONE as a complete sentence. "The dog barked." makes complete sense by itself — also called a MAIN clause.
dependent clause
A clause that CANNOT stand alone — it depends on a main clause for its meaning. "...because it was hungry" has a subject and verb but does not make sense by itself — also called a SUBORDINATE clause.
conjunction
A word that JOINS clauses or words together. Two types matter here: coordinating (joins equals) and subordinating (introduces a dependent clause).
coordinating conjunction
Joins two INDEPENDENT clauses of equal importance. Remember with FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So.
subordinating conjunction
Begins a DEPENDENT clause and shows its relationship to the main clause. Examples: because, although, when, if, since, while, after, before, unless.
simple sentence
ONE independent clause. Can be short OR long — length does not determine the type, STRUCTURE does.
compound sentence
TWO OR MORE independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (or a semicolon). Both halves could stand alone.
complex sentence
An independent clause PLUS one or more dependent clauses, joined by a subordinating conjunction.
🧩 What Is a Clause? — The Building Block of Every Sentence
🔍
The test: Does the group of words have a SUBJECT (who or what) and a VERB (what they do)? If yes, it's a clause. Now ask: does it make COMPLETE sense by itself? If yes → independent clause. If no → dependent clause.
Example A
"The dog barked."
Subject: the dog. Verb: barked. Makes sense alone → independent clause.
vs
Example B
"...because it was hungry."
Subject: it. Verb: was. Does NOT make sense alone → dependent clause.
💡
Combine them: "The dog barked because it was hungry." One independent clause + one dependent clause, joined by the subordinating conjunction "because" = a COMPLEX sentence.

🔗 FANBOYS — Joining Independent Clauses (Compound Sentences)
⚠️
The rule: independent clause + comma + FANBOYS word + independent clause. Each FANBOYS word has its own meaning — choosing the right one changes the relationship between the two ideas.
FANBOYS WordMeaning / RelationshipExample
ForGives a reason (formal — like "because")She studied hard, for the exam was important.
AndAdds an equal ideaMaya finished her homework, and she practised the piano.
NorAdds a second negative ideaHe did not complain, nor did he give up.
ButShows contrastI wanted to play outside, but it started raining.
OrShows a choice/alternativeWe can revise today, or we can revise tomorrow.
YetShows surprising contrastThe test was difficult, yet she passed easily.
SoShows result/consequenceIt was raining heavily, so the match was cancelled.
🧷 Subordinating Conjunctions — Building Complex Sentences
📌
A complex sentence can be written in TWO orders. The comma rule depends on which order you choose:
Dependent clause FIRST → use a comma
[Dependent clause], [Independent clause].
"Although she was tired, she finished her homework."
"Because it was raining, the match was postponed."
"When the bell rang, the students lined up quietly."
Independent clause FIRST → usually no comma
[Independent clause] [dependent clause].
"She finished her homework although she was tired."
"The match was postponed because it was raining."
"The students lined up quietly when the bell rang."
✅
Common subordinating conjunctions: because, although, when, if, since, while, after, before, unless, until, even though, as soon as. Each one signals a different relationship — try swapping "because" for "although" in a sentence and notice how the meaning changes completely.
🖊️ Punctuation Rules — Commas, Full Stops & Question Marks
1
Full stop ( . ) — ends a statement
Used at the end of every simple, compound, and complex sentence that makes a statement. "The library closes at 5pm."
2
Question mark ( ? ) — ends a direct question
Only for DIRECT questions. "What time does the library close?" — NOT for reported/indirect questions: "She asked what time the library closes." (this needs a full stop, not a question mark).
3
Comma — separating items in a list
"I packed a pencil, a ruler, a calculator and a book." Note: in UK English, a comma before "and" in a list (the "Oxford comma") is optional but should be used consistently.
4
Comma — after an introductory word or phrase
"However, the results were surprising." / "After the lesson ended, the students went to lunch." The comma signals a brief pause before the main clause begins.
5
Comma — joining two independent clauses (compound sentences)
Comma + FANBOYS word. "I finished my work, so I went outside." Without the comma AND the conjunction together, this becomes an error.
6
Comma — after a dependent clause that comes first
"Although it was late, she kept working." If the dependent clause comes second, the comma usually disappears: "She kept working although it was late."
🚫 Common Mistakes — Comma Splices & Run-On Sentences
⚠️
Comma splice: joining TWO independent clauses with ONLY a comma — no conjunction. This is one of the most common errors at KS3.
❌ Incorrect✅ Correct OptionsWhat Changed?
I went to the shop, I bought some bread.I went to the shop, and I bought some bread.Added a FANBOYS conjunction → compound sentence
I went to the shop, I bought some bread.I went to the shop. I bought some bread.Split into two simple sentences with a full stop
I went to the shop, I bought some bread.I went to the shop; I bought some bread.Used a semicolon to join two related independent clauses
I went to the shop because I bought some bread.I went to the shop because I needed bread."Because" must introduce a REASON — the original sentence doesn't make logical sense
✅
Quick self-check: Read each half of your sentence separately. If BOTH halves make complete sense alone, you need a conjunction, a semicolon, or a full stop between them — never just a comma on its own.

📚 Key Vocabulary — Defined Simply
Builds on Week 3 — make sure you remember numerator, denominator, simplify and HCF
like denominators
Fractions that have the SAME bottom number. Example: 2/9 and 4/9 have like denominators (both 9).
unlike denominators
Fractions with DIFFERENT bottom numbers. Example: 1/4 and 1/6 have unlike denominators (4 and 6).
common denominator
A denominator that BOTH fractions can be converted to so they can be added or subtracted. Usually the LCM of the two denominators.
LCM
Lowest Common Multiple — the SMALLEST number that both denominators divide into exactly. LCM of 4 and 6 = 12.
mixed number
A whole number AND a fraction together. 2¾ = "two and three quarters." Revision from Week 3.
borrowing
When subtracting mixed numbers, if the fraction you are subtracting is BIGGER than the fraction you start with, you "borrow" 1 whole from the whole number and convert it into a fraction.
simplify
Reduce a fraction to its lowest terms by dividing top and bottom by their HCF (Highest Common Factor) — always do this as your LAST step. Revision from Week 3.

🔄 Adding Fractions with UNLIKE Denominators — Find the LCM First
⚠️
You CANNOT add fractions with different denominators directly. 1/4 + 1/6 is NOT 2/10! You must first convert both fractions to have the SAME denominator.

➖ Subtracting Fractions — Same Method, Different Operation
💡
Subtraction follows exactly the same process as addition: like denominators → subtract straight away; unlike denominators → find the LCM, convert, THEN subtract.
Like denominators:
7/9 − 4/9 = (7−4)/9 = 3/9 = 1/3 (simplify! HCF(3,9)=3)

Unlike denominators:
5/6 − 1/4 → LCM(6,4)=12 → 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12
(5/6 → ×2 → 10/12) (1/4 → ×3 → 3/12)
⚠️
Order matters in subtraction! 5/6 − 1/4 is NOT the same as 1/4 − 5/6. Always subtract in the order written — the first fraction is the starting amount, the second is what is taken away.
🔢 Mixed Number Calculations — Adding
✅
Method: Add the whole numbers together. Add the fraction parts together (finding a common denominator if needed). If the fraction part is improper (≥1), convert and carry the extra whole number across.
Example: 2¾ + 1½

Whole numbers: 2 + 1 = 3
Fractions: 3/4 + 1/2 → LCM(4,2)=4 → 3/4 + 2/4 = 5/4
5/4 is improper → 5/4 = 1¼
Combine: 3 + 1¼ = 4¼

Final answer: 2¾ + 1½ = 4¼
🔁 Mixed Number Calculations — Subtracting with Borrowing
⚠️
When do you need to borrow? When the fraction you are subtracting is BIGGER than the fraction you are starting with. You cannot have a "negative" fraction part, so you borrow 1 whole from the whole-number part and add it to the fraction.
1
Compare the fraction parts first
Example: 4⅕ − 1⅗. Fraction parts: 1/5 vs 3/5. Since 1/5 < 3/5, we cannot subtract directly — we need to borrow.
2
Borrow 1 from the whole number
4⅕ = 3 + 1 + 1/5 = 3 + 5/5 + 1/5 = 3 + 6/5 = 3 6/5. We've turned 4⅕ into "3 and six-fifths" — same value, different form.
3
Subtract whole numbers and fractions separately
Whole numbers: 3 − 1 = 2. Fractions: 6/5 − 3/5 = 3/5.
4
Combine and simplify
2 + 3/5 = 2⅗. Check: HCF(3,5)=1, already simplified. Final answer: 4⅕ − 1⅗ = 2⅗
💡
Alternative method: Convert BOTH mixed numbers to improper fractions first, then subtract, then convert back. 4⅕ = 21/5. 1⅗ = 8/5. 21/5 − 8/5 = 13/5 = 2⅗ ✓ Same answer — choose whichever method you find clearer.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Biology Guide
Builds on Week 3 — remember organ, tissue and function
nutrition
The process of taking in food and using it for energy, growth, and health. "Good nutrition" means eating the right foods in the right amounts.
nutrient
A substance in food that the body needs: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water are all nutrients.
balanced diet
Eating the right VARIETY and PROPORTION of foods for good health — not equal amounts of everything, but the correct proportions of each group.
carbohydrate
The body's MAIN source of energy. Found in rice, bread, pasta, potatoes, and other starchy foods.
protein
Needed for GROWTH and REPAIR of body tissues. Found in chicken, fish, lentils, beans, chickpeas, eggs and dairy.
fibre
Helps digestion run smoothly and keeps the gut healthy. Found in fruits, vegetables, whole grains and pulses (lentils, beans).
digestion
The process of BREAKING DOWN food into nutrients small enough for the body to absorb into the blood.
enzyme
A biological molecule that SPEEDS UP digestion — it breaks large food molecules into smaller ones. Different enzymes break down different nutrients.
peristalsis
Wave-like muscle squeezing movements that push food along the digestive tract — from the oesophagus all the way through the intestines.
absorption
Nutrients passing from the gut into the BLOODSTREAM — mainly happens in the small intestine, through the villi (Week 3 link).
🍛 What Is a Balanced Diet? — The Five Food Groups
💡
A balanced diet does NOT mean equal amounts of everything. It means eating the RIGHT PROPORTIONS — much more of some groups (carbohydrates, fruit and vegetables) and much less of others (fats and sugary foods).

✅
KS3 key idea: An unbalanced diet — even if it contains "healthy" foods — can cause problems. Too much carbohydrate and fat with too little fruit, vegetables and protein can lead to poor energy levels, weak immunity, and long-term health issues such as obesity.
🔬 Macronutrients vs Micronutrients
Macronutrients — needed in LARGE amounts
Carbohydrates: primary energy source — broken down into glucose
Proteins: broken down into amino acids — used to build and repair tissue
Fats: broken down into fatty acids and glycerol — long-term energy storage, cell membranes, insulation
"Macro" = LARGE — these make up most of your daily food intake
Micronutrients — needed in SMALL amounts
Vitamin C: immune system, healing wounds — found in citrus fruit, peppers
Vitamin D: helps the body absorb calcium for bone health — sunlight + eggs, fortified milk
Calcium: strong bones and teeth — found in dairy, leafy greens
Iron: needed to make red blood cells — found in red meat, lentils, spinach. Deficiency causes anaemia (tiredness)

🧪 Enzymes — Biological Catalysts
⚡
An enzyme is a biological molecule that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up itself. In digestion, enzymes break large food molecules (which are too big to absorb) into small molecules (which CAN be absorbed into the blood).
EnzymeWhere ProducedBreaks Down...Products

💡
Bile — not an enzyme, but essential: Bile is produced by the liver and stored in the gallbladder. It is not an enzyme — it does not break molecules apart chemically. Instead, it EMULSIFIES fats — breaking large fat globules into tiny droplets, which gives lipase a much larger surface area to work on.
🏃 Why Does This Matter? — Health and Growth
✅
KS3 application: During your teenage years, your body is going through rapid growth — you need MORE protein (for muscle and tissue growth), MORE calcium and vitamin D (for bone development), and MORE iron (especially important to prevent anaemia). A diet that lacks variety — even if it "feels" healthy — can leave gaps that affect energy, concentration, and growth.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL History Guide
Builds on Week 3 — compare these terms to Ancient Greece as you go
empire
A large territory containing many different lands and peoples, ruled by ONE supreme authority. At its height, the Roman Empire stretched from Britain to Egypt to Iraq.
republic
A system of government where REPRESENTATIVES are elected to govern, rather than a king ruling alone. Rome was a republic for almost 500 years before becoming an empire.
emperor
The supreme ruler of the Roman Empire — often holding power for life, unlike the elected leaders of the Republic.
senate
A council of senior, experienced politicians who advised and governed Rome. Under the Republic the Senate held real power; under emperors it became mostly advisory.
legion
A large unit of the Roman army — around 5,000 soldiers. The Roman army's organisation, training and engineering made it one of the most effective military forces in history.
citizen
A legal member of Rome with rights (such as voting and legal protection) and responsibilities. Compare to Athenian citizenship from Week 3 — both systems excluded large groups of people.
province
A region or territory controlled by Rome OUTSIDE Italy — such as Britannia (Britain), Gaul (France), or Egypt.
aqueduct
A structure (often a bridge-like channel) built to carry water over long distances using gravity — a major feat of Roman engineering.
Latin
The language of the Romans. Latin is the root of Italian, Spanish, French, Portuguese and Romanian, and forms the basis of a huge amount of English vocabulary.
🏛️ From Republic to Empire — How Rome Grew
📍
Founding legend: According to Roman legend, the city of Rome was founded in 753 BC by twin brothers Romulus and Remus, who were said to have been raised by a wolf. Historians treat this as legend — but Rome's growth from a small city to the largest empire the ancient world had ever seen is very real history.
753 BC (legend)
Founding of Rome
A small city-state begins on seven hills beside the river Tiber in central Italy.
509–27 BC
The Roman Republic
Rome is governed by elected officials (consuls) and the Senate — not by kings. The motto "SPQR" (Senatus Populusque Romanus — "The Senate and People of Rome") appears on Roman buildings, coins and military standards.
49–44 BC
Julius Caesar's Rise and Assassination
Caesar becomes the most powerful man in Rome, declared dictator. He is assassinated by senators in 44 BC who feared he wanted to become king — a fear that ironically helped bring about the end of the Republic.
27 BC
Augustus Becomes First Emperor
Octavian (Caesar's adopted heir) takes the title "Augustus" and becomes Rome's first emperor — beginning the Roman Empire and a long period of relative stability known as the Pax Romana ("Roman Peace").
43 AD
Roman Conquest of Britain
Emperor Claudius leads the full conquest of Britain (after Julius Caesar's earlier limited expeditions in 55–54 BC). Britain becomes the province of Britannia.
122 AD
Hadrian's Wall Built
Emperor Hadrian orders a great wall built across northern Britain to mark and defend the empire's frontier against the Picts and other northern tribes.
410 AD
Roman Withdrawal from Britain
As pressures grow elsewhere in the empire, Roman forces withdraw from Britain — ending nearly 400 years of Roman rule there.
476 AD
Fall of the Western Roman Empire
The last Western Roman emperor is removed. The Eastern half of the empire (the Byzantine Empire, centred on Constantinople) continues for almost another 1,000 years.

⚔️ The Roman Army — Legions and Engineering
1
The Legion — Rome's Core Military Unit
A legion contained around 5,000 soldiers, divided into smaller groups called centuries (originally ~100 men, later ~80), each led by a centurion. This structure allowed huge armies to be organised, commanded and moved efficiently.
2
Discipline and Training
Roman soldiers trained constantly — marching, building camps, formation drills. This level of discipline echoes the Spartan military culture from Week 3, though for different underlying reasons (Rome's was about conquest and control of a vast empire; Sparta's was about survival against the helots).
3
Roman Roads — Built for the Army, Used by Everyone
Roman roads were built primarily so legions could move quickly across the empire. Many were so well-built that parts of their routes are still used by modern roads today — including in Britain.
4
At Its Height
The Roman Empire stretched from Britain in the northwest to Egypt in the southeast and Iraq in the east — covering roughly 5 million km² and governing tens of millions of people across dozens of different cultures and languages.

🛡️ The Conquest (43 AD)
Emperor Claudius led the full Roman conquest of Britain in 43 AD, following Julius Caesar's earlier, more limited expeditions a century before. Britain became the Roman province of Britannia.
🧱 Hadrian's Wall
Built from 122 AD across northern Britain — roughly 117 km long. Marked the northern frontier of the empire and was defended by forts and garrisons along its length.
🛣️ Roman Roads
A network of straight, well-engineered roads connected towns and forts across Britain. Several modern roads still follow these ancient routes.
🏙️ Roman Towns
Londinium (London) grew into a major trading centre. Aquae Sulis (Bath) became famous for its Roman baths, parts of which survive today. Eboracum (York) and Deva (Chester) were important military centres.
🏺 Roman Baths & Villas
Wealthy Romans and Romanised Britons built villas with underfloor heating (hypocausts) and bathhouses — technology that disappeared from Britain for centuries after Rome's withdrawal.
📜 Departure (410 AD)
As the empire faced growing pressure elsewhere, Roman forces withdrew from Britain around 410 AD, leaving local rulers to manage their own defence — a major turning point in British history.
📉 Why Did Rome Fall? — Multiple Causes
🔑
KS3 key concept: The fall of the Western Roman Empire was NOT a single event — it was a slow process over roughly 200 years, caused by MULTIPLE factors working together. Historians still debate which causes mattered most.
CauseExplanation
Economic troublesHeavy taxation to fund the army and government, combined with inflation, weakened the economy and made it harder to pay soldiers and maintain infrastructure.
Military pressureGroups such as the Goths, Vandals and Huns moved into or attacked Roman territory, putting enormous strain on Rome's borders and armies.
Political instabilityFrequent changes of emperor — sometimes through assassination or civil war — made long-term planning and stable government very difficult.
Size of the empireThe empire became so large that it was split into Western and Eastern halves to make it easier to govern — but this also weakened unity and shared resources.
✅
The Eastern Empire continued: While the WESTERN Roman Empire fell in 476 AD, the EASTERN Roman Empire (known as the Byzantine Empire, centred on Constantinople) continued for almost another 1,000 years, until 1453 AD. "The fall of Rome" really means the fall of the WESTERN half.
🌍 Roman Legacy — Impact on the Modern World
🗣️ Language — Latin's Influence
Italian, Spanish, French, Portuguese, Romanian — and English vocabulary
▼
Latin is the direct ancestor of Italian, Spanish, French, Portuguese and Romanian — known as the "Romance languages" (from "Roman"). English is a Germanic language, but huge numbers of English words come from Latin, especially academic and formal vocabulary. Examples: video (I see), audio (I hear), et cetera (and the rest), via (by way of). Even the alphabet you are reading right now — the Latin alphabet — is a Roman invention.
⚖️ Law — Foundations of Modern Legal Systems
Many legal principles used today have Roman roots
▼
Roman law developed detailed written codes covering property, contracts, family and criminal matters. Many legal systems around the world — including in Europe and countries influenced by European law — are based partly on Roman legal principles, such as the idea that laws should be written down, publicly known, and applied consistently. The Latin phrase "innocent until proven guilty" reflects a principle with roots in Roman legal thinking.
🏗️ Engineering & Architecture
Roads, aqueducts, the arch, and concrete
▼
The Romans perfected the arch — allowing them to build aqueducts that carried water across valleys using gravity alone, and structures like the Colosseum that could hold tens of thousands of people. They also developed a form of concrete, used in structures like the Pantheon in Rome, which still stands today after roughly 1,900 years. Modern bridges, domes and tunnels owe a debt to Roman engineering principles.
📅 The Calendar
Why is it called "July" and "August"?
▼
The calendar we use today is based on the Roman Julian calendar (later adjusted into the Gregorian calendar). The month July is named after Julius Caesar, and August after the emperor Augustus. Several other month names come from Roman gods and numbers (e.g. "December" comes from the Latin word for "ten," decem — because it was originally the tenth month in the old Roman calendar).
🏛️ Government — Ideas That Inspired Later Democracies
The word "republic" itself comes from Rome
▼
The word "republic" comes from the Latin phrase res publica — "public affair" or "the people's matter." When later thinkers and revolutionaries designed new systems of government (for example, in the late 1700s), many looked back to the Roman Republic — alongside Greek democracy — as a model for representative government, a senate, and checks on the power of any single ruler.
