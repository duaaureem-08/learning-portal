📚 Week 5 — Abraham
Week 5
1
Write the memory verse Genesis 12:2 from memory, and explain what God asks Abraham to do, and what he promises in return. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you; I will make your name great, and you will be a blessing." (Genesis 12:2, NIV). God asks Abraham to leave his home country, his people, and his father's household, and go to a land God will show him — without specifying the destination in advance. In return, God promises to make Abraham into a great nation, to bless him, and — crucially — that Abraham himself will become a source of blessing to others, ultimately "all peoples on earth" (12:3).
2
Explain why Abraham's faith is often described as trusting "without knowing the destination." What made this different from Noah's earlier test of faith? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Abraham is told to leave his home for "the land I will show you" — he sets out without knowing exactly where he is going, trusting God to reveal the destination along the way. This differs from Noah, whose challenge was to trust a specific, clearly described warning (a coming flood) and act accordingly by building the ark — Noah knew WHAT was coming, even though he could not yet see it. Abraham, by contrast, does not even know the specific destination of his journey — his faith requires an ongoing trust in guidance, not just a one-time act based on a clear instruction.
Week 6 — Joseph
🧥 Week 6 — Joseph: Character Through Suffering
Week 6
3
Briefly describe the FIVE key stages of Joseph's journey, from the pit to becoming second-in-command of Egypt. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Sold into slavery (Gen 37) — brothers throw him into a pit, sell him to traders. 2. Falsely accused and imprisoned (Gen 39) — despite serving Potiphar with integrity. 3. Interpreting dreams in prison (Gen 40-41) — correctly interprets two officials' dreams. 4. Second-in-command of Egypt (Gen 41) — interprets Pharaoh's dreams, made ruler at around age 30. 5. Reunion and forgiveness (Gen 42-45) — brothers arrive for grain, Joseph forgives them.
4
Write the memory verse Genesis 50:20 from memory, and explain the key theological idea it teaches about God's purposes. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good, to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives." (Genesis 50:20, NIV). This verse teaches that the SAME events can carry two different intentions simultaneously — Joseph's brothers intended harm, while God intended good — without denying that real harm was done. It shows that God's purposes can move forward even through other people's wrongdoing, and that this is often only recognisable by looking backward, once the full story has unfolded.
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Compare Abraham's faith (trusting without knowing the destination) with Joseph's faith (trusting through suffering caused by others). What NEW dimension does Joseph's story add to the pattern of faith traced across the term so far? Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Abraham's faith centres on trusting God's guidance despite not knowing where that guidance will lead — he must act without a map, trusting that the destination will become clear along the way. Joseph's story adds a significantly different dimension: his suffering is not the result of his own choice to obey a difficult calling, but is inflicted on him entirely by OTHER PEOPLE'S sin and cruelty — his brothers' betrayal, Potiphar's wife's false accusation. This means Joseph's faith is tested not by uncertainty about direction, but by enduring genuine injustice with no apparent purpose visible at the time. The pattern deepens across these two stories: Noah trusted a promise before any evidence existed; Abraham trusted without knowing the destination; Joseph trusted God's purpose even while suffering wrongs done to him by others, only recognising that purpose decades later. Together, these stories build an increasingly complete picture of what biblical faith requires — action before evidence, obedience without full information, and endurance through undeserved suffering — covering a very wide range of situations in which trust is genuinely tested.

📚 Week 5 — Descriptive Writing
Week 5
1
Define "simile" and "metaphor," and explain the KEY difference between them. Give one original example of each. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Simile: a comparison between two things using "like" or "as." Example: "Her smile was as bright as sunshine." Metaphor: a comparison that describes one thing AS BEING another, without using "like" or "as." Example: "Her smile was sunshine." Key difference: similes explicitly signal a comparison using "like"/"as," while metaphors state the comparison directly as if it were literally true, often creating a stronger or more immediate image.
2
Write a short descriptive paragraph (at least 4 sentences) about a stormy sea, using at least ONE simile AND one metaphor, labelled. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Example: "The sea was a raging monster tonight, [metaphor] hurling itself against the rocks without mercy. Waves rose like grey mountains [simile] before collapsing into churning foam. The wind screamed through the rigging, and the boat groaned under the weight of the storm." Award marks for genuine, original imagery and correctly labelled devices.

Explain the spelling rule for why "in-" changes to "im-" or "ir-" in certain words, giving TWO examples. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼

📚 Week 5 — Photosynthesis & Plant Biology
Week 5
1
Write the word equation for photosynthesis, and state where in the plant cell this process occurs. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Word equation: Carbon dioxide + water → (using light energy) → glucose + oxygen. This process occurs in the chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll that absorbs light energy.
2
Describe TWO features of a leaf's structure that help it carry out photosynthesis efficiently. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any two of: Broad, flat shape — maximises surface area exposed to sunlight. Thin structure — allows gases (CO₂, O₂) to diffuse in and out quickly, and light to penetrate to inner cells. Stomata (small pores, usually on the underside) — allow carbon dioxide in and oxygen/water vapour out. Many chloroplasts in palisade cells — maximise light absorption near the leaf's upper surface.
Week 6 — Ecosystems & Food Webs
🕸️ Week 6 — Ecosystems & Food Webs
Week 6
3
Define "producer," "primary consumer," and "decomposer," giving one example of each. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Producer: an organism that makes its own food, usually via photosynthesis. Example: grass, trees. Primary consumer: an organism that eats producers directly (a herbivore). Example: rabbit, grasshopper. Decomposer: an organism that breaks down dead organisms, returning nutrients to the soil. Example: fungi, bacteria.
4
Explain why a food WEB is considered more realistic than a single food CHAIN. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A food chain shows energy flowing in a single straight line, implying each organism has only one food source and one predator. A food web is more realistic because it shows the overlapping relationships that actually exist in nature — most animals eat MORE THAN ONE type of food and are eaten by MORE THAN ONE predator, so a web better represents the true complexity of feeding relationships in an ecosystem.
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Explain the connection between photosynthesis (Week 5) and food webs (Week 6) — specifically, why producers are described as "the base of every food chain." Use the word equation for photosynthesis in your explanation. Write at least 5 sentences. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Photosynthesis is the process by which producers convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, using light energy — this is the ONLY way that energy from the sun enters a food web in a usable, storable form (as glucose, a source of chemical energy). Because producers are the sole organisms capable of capturing this solar energy directly, every other organism in a food web ultimately depends on them: primary consumers eat producers to access this stored energy, secondary consumers eat primary consumers, and so on up the chain. This is why producers are described as "the base" — without photosynthesis happening somewhere at the start of the chain, there would be no energy available for any consumer at any level. If a producer population were damaged or reduced, this would directly limit the energy available to every level above it in the food web, showing just how fundamental the process studied in Week 5 is to the entire ecosystem studied in Week 6.

📚 Week 5 — Maps, Globes & Geographic Tools
Week 5
1
Explain what latitude and longitude are, and how coordinates are written (which comes first). 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Latitude lines run horizontally around the Earth, measuring distance north or south of the equator (0°). Longitude lines run vertically (pole to pole), measuring distance east or west of the Prime Meridian (0°, passing through Greenwich, London). Coordinates are conventionally written with latitude first, then longitude (e.g. 27.99°N, 86.93°E).
2
Explain what "map scale" means, and why it is important when reading a map. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Map scale shows the relationship between distance on the map and actual distance in the real world (e.g. 1:100,000 means 1cm on the map represents 100,000cm, or 1km, in reality). It matters because without scale, a map cannot be used to calculate real distances, plan routes, or understand the true size of the area shown.

Explain why Antarctica is technically classified as a "desert," despite being extremely cold. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"Desert" is defined by very LOW RAINFALL/precipitation, not necessarily heat. Antarctica receives virtually no annual precipitation, making it technically the world's largest desert by area, despite being the coldest place on Earth — the ice that covers it accumulated over millions of years and barely melts.
