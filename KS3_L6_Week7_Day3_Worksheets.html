intercession · covenant · deliverance · calling · plague
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
intercession: praying or speaking on behalf of someone else before God or another authority. Example: Moses cried out to God on behalf of the Israelites multiple times — including asking God to remove the frogs and other plagues after Pharaoh promised to let the people go. covenant: a serious, binding promise stronger than a contract. Example: Moses received his calling at the burning bush on Mount Horeb — God specifically named Moses and gave him the task of returning to Egypt to confront Pharaoh. plague: a terrible disaster or judgment. Example: God sent ten plagues against Egypt — from water turning to blood to the death of every firstborn — each targeting something Egypt worshipped or relied on.

🔍 Explanation
3
The lesson says the ten plagues were "a theological battle, not just a political one." Choose FOUR of the ten plagues and for each one: name the Egyptian god it targeted, explain what that god was supposed to control, and explain what the plague demonstrated about that god's power (or lack of it). 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any four of: Plague 1 (water to blood): targeted the god Hapi (deity of the Nile floods) and the Nile itself — Egypt's most sacred and vital resource. Demonstrated that the God of Israel controlled what Egypt depended on most. Plague 2 (frogs): targeted the frog goddess Heqet, a symbol of fertility and new life. Demonstrated that Egypt's symbol of birth and abundance could become an instrument of horror. Plague 5 (livestock disease): targeted the sacred bull god Apis. The death of Egypt's cattle — economically devastating — showed Apis had no power to protect his own domain. Plague 6 (boils): targeted the Egyptian priests and magicians directly — they couldn't even stand before Moses. Egypt's own religious intermediaries were publicly incapacitated, unable to counter or replicate the plague. Plague 9 (darkness): targeted Ra, the supreme sun god whom Pharaoh himself was supposed to represent as his earthly embodiment. Three days of total, tangible darkness — while Israelites had light — completely humiliated the most powerful deity in the Egyptian pantheon. Plague 10 (death of firstborn): targeted Pharaoh himself (his son died) — the man who claimed to be divine. Demonstrated that even the "living god" of Egypt could not protect his own household.
4
The lesson makes a distinction between what God said to Moses at the burning bush ("I will be with you") and what Moses might have expected God to say ("I will make you qualified"). Why does this distinction matter? What does "I will be with you" communicate about the basis of Moses' calling — and what does it demand from Moses in response? Write at least 3 sentences. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The distinction matters because it locates the source of the calling entirely in God rather than in Moses. If God had said "I will make you qualified," the relationship would be transactional — Moses would gain personal capabilities and then go do the job. Instead, "I will be with you" means Moses remains exactly who he is — an unqualified, reluctant shepherd — but God's presence is what makes the mission possible. Every step of the Exodus journey would require Moses to trust in a presence rather than rely on personal ability. What it communicates: the basis of Moses' calling is not his CV or character — it is God's faithfulness to His own purposes. Moses was not chosen because he was the best available option; he was chosen because God decided to act through him. What it demands: it demands ongoing trust rather than a one-time act of courage. Moses could not spend forty years in the wilderness building qualifications and then launch the Exodus. He had to go now, unqualified, with only a promise. That is the call — and the lesson's golden idea: God does not call the equipped; He equips the called.

5
The lesson says the Passover "is not just a historical meal — it is a theological prototype." Using the lesson's specific connections between the Passover lamb and Jesus (called "the Lamb of God" in John 1:29), write an extended response explaining: (a) what specific features of the Passover lamb point forward to Jesus in Christian understanding, (b) how the Last Supper connects to the Passover, and (c) why the lesson says understanding the Passover makes the New Testament "significantly richer" to read. Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) The Passover lamb had to be perfect and without defect — selected four days before it was killed. In the New Testament, Jesus is repeatedly described as sinless and "without blemish." The blood on the doorposts was what protected the household — it was not the family's goodness but the blood that distinguished a protected house. Christians see this as a picture of the blood of Jesus providing protection: "the blood of the Lamb" is a repeated phrase in Revelation. The lamb was substitutionary — an innocent animal died in place of the firstborn. This is the same logic Christians see in Jesus' death: an innocent person dying in place of those who deserved judgment. (b) Jesus held his final meal with his disciples specifically during the Passover (Luke 22:7-20). When he took the unleavened bread and wine of the Passover meal, he gave them new meaning: "This is my body broken for you... this is my blood of the new covenant." He was not abolishing the Passover but deliberately reinterpreting it — placing himself as the new Passover lamb. The phrase "new covenant" echoes Exodus 24 (the Sinai covenant) and Jeremiah 31 (the promised new covenant). (c) Without knowing the Passover, a reader of the New Testament misses layer upon layer of meaning. When John the Baptist calls Jesus "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29), a reader who knows the Passover understands immediately: this is the ultimate Passover sacrifice. When Paul writes "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7), the entire theological logic depends on knowing what a Passover lamb was and what it did. The Exodus is not background history — it is the conceptual vocabulary of the New Testament.
6
Write a structured argument (for or against) responding to this statement: "The story of Moses teaches that great things are accomplished by exceptional people with exceptional abilities." Use at least FOUR specific pieces of evidence from the Exodus story to support your position. Write at least 5 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Model position: Disagree — the story of Moses actively contradicts this statement. Evidence 1: Moses gave five separate excuses at the burning bush explaining why he was the wrong person — including "I am not a good speaker" and "please send someone else." The text does not present him as exceptionally confident or gifted. Evidence 2: Moses was 80 years old, had been living as a shepherd for 40 years, and had no political standing in Egypt. His credentials were the opposite of impressive. Evidence 3: When the Israelites panicked at the Red Sea, Moses' first response was to cry out to God — not to devise a military strategy. The sea did not part because Moses had exceptional engineering skills; it parted because God acted. Evidence 4: Moses' greatest moments of failure (Exodus 32 — rage at the golden calf; Numbers 20 — striking the rock in anger) came even at the height of his leadership. He was not a perfected person. Conclusion: The lesson's golden idea directly inverts the statement: "God does not call the equipped — He equips the called." Moses' story is specifically constructed to show that what matters is not the person's abilities but the presence and faithfulness of God working through them. If the story were about exceptional ability, it would have begun with an extraordinary person. Instead, it begins with a reluctant shepherd arguing himself out of a divine assignment five times. The whole point is precisely the contrast between Moses' ordinariness and what God accomplished through him.

Headline: THE SCHOOL HOLIDAY IS KILLING YOUR CHILD'S FUTURE

This summer, millions of children across the country will fall behind. Not because they are not clever. Not because they lack potential. But because six weeks without structured learning is enough — according to educational researchers — to undo two months of progress made in school.

This "summer slide" is not a myth invented by overanxious parents. "The long summer holiday is a relic of Victorian agricultural Britain," said Professor James Adeyemi of Oxford University's Department of Education. "It was designed for a farming calendar, not a modern knowledge economy. We are paying for it with our children's futures."

There is a simple solution, and other countries have already found it. Finland, South Korea, and Singapore all have shorter summer breaks and longer, distributed holiday periods across the year. Their educational outcomes consistently rank among the highest in the world.

It is time to stop pretending that six weeks of unstructured time is somehow good for children. For the children of the wealthy, it might be. For everyone else, it is six weeks of lost ground that may never be fully recovered. We cannot keep accepting a system that advantages the already-advantaged. Reform the school calendar — now.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Technique 1: Expert quotation: Professor James Adeyemi, Oxford. A paint company makes a colour called "Ocean Blue" by mixing blue, white, and grey paint in the ratio 5:3:2.
(a) How much of each colour is needed for 4 litres of Ocean Blue?
(b) The company has 6 litres of blue, 4 litres of white, and 3 litres of grey. What is the maximum total volume of Ocean Blue they can make? (Hint: check which colour runs out first.)

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Total parts=10. One part=4÷10=0.4L. Blue=5×0.4=2L, White=3×0.4=1.2L, Grey=2×0.4=0.8L. (b) Check each colour as the limiting factor: Blue (6L): 6÷5=1.2L per part → total=1.2×10=12L. 5
Two taps fill a tank. Tap A fills it in 6 hours alone. Tap B fills it in 4 hours alone. (a) What fraction of the tank does each tap fill per hour? (b) If both taps are open, what fraction of the tank is filled per hour? (c) How long does it take to fill the whole tank with both taps open? (d) This is a proportion problem — is it direct or inverse, and why? 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Tap A: 1/6 of tank per hour. Tap B: 1/4 of tank per hour. (b) Together: 1/6 + 1/4 = 2/12 + 3/12 = 5/12 per hour. (c) Time to fill whole tank = 1 ÷ 5/12 = 12/5 = 2.4 hours (2 hours 24 minutes). (d) This is inverse proportion — more taps (or more filling capacity) means less time to fill the tank. If you double the combined rate, you halve the time. Rate × time = 1 tank (a constant product), which is the defining property of inverse proportion.
6
Prove algebraically that if two quantities x and y are in direct proportion (y = kx for some constant k), then: (a) doubling x doubles y, (b) the ratio y₁:y₂ always equals x₁:x₂ for any two values. Then give a real-world ratio/proportion example that demonstrates both of these properties. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) If y = kx, and we double x to 2x: new y = k(2x) = 2(kx) = 2y. So doubling x gives 2y — i.e. y also doubles. ✓ (b) Let x₁ and x₂ be any two values of x. Then y₁ = kx₁ and y₂ = kx₂. The ratio y₁:y₂ = kx₁ : kx₂. Dividing both parts by k: = x₁ : x₂. So y₁:y₂ = x₁:x₂ for any values. ✓ Real-world example: Cost of apples at £2 per kg. If x = mass (kg) and y = cost (£), then y = 2x. (a) 3kg costs £6; 6kg costs £12 — doubling mass doubles cost. ✓ (b) The ratio of costs for 3kg and 5kg = £6:£10 = 3:5, which is exactly the ratio of masses (3:5). ✓ This is why the unitary method always works for direct proportion: the ratio of outputs equals the ratio of inputs.
7
A nutritionist says a healthy meal should have protein, carbohydrates, and fat in the ratio 2:5:1 by mass. (a) A meal weighs 400g total. How much of each macronutrient should it contain? (b) Protein provides 4 calories per gram, carbohydrates provide 4 cal/g, and fat provides 9 cal/g. How many calories come from each macronutrient? (c) What is the total calorie count? (d) What percentage of the total calories comes from fat? 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Total parts=2+5+1=8. One part=400÷8=50g. Protein=2×50=100g, Carbs=5×50=250g, Fat=1×50=50g. Check: 100+250+50=400 ✓. (b) Protein: 100×4=400 cal. Carbs: 250×4=1,000 cal. Fat: 50×9=450 cal. (c) Total calories=400+1,000+450=1,850 calories. (d) % from fat=450÷1,850×100=24.3% (1 d.p.). Note: although fat is only 1/8 of the mass, it provides 24.3% of the calories — because fat has more than twice the calories per gram as protein or carbohydrates. This is a real-world example of why mass ratio alone doesn't tell the whole nutritional story.

2
A nurse notices that when she applies alcohol (ethanol) to a patient's skin before an injection, the skin quickly feels cold. Use the particle model to explain this observation fully. Include: what state change is occurring, whether heat is absorbed or released, where the heat comes from, and why the skin feels cold. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
State change: The alcohol is evaporating — changing from liquid to gas. This is the liquid → gas state change (evaporation, not boiling, since it happens below the boiling point of alcohol at the surface). Heat absorbed or released: Evaporation absorbs heat — this is always the case when a liquid turns to gas. Where the heat comes from: The alcohol absorbs heat energy from the patient's skin — the skin is providing the thermal energy needed for the alcohol particles to escape as gas. Why the skin feels cold: Because the alcohol is removing thermal energy (heat) from the skin surface, the skin temperature drops — this is detected by the patient as a cold sensation. The faster the evaporation, the more heat is removed and the colder it feels. Bonus: Alcohol has weaker forces between its molecules than water does, meaning its particles need less energy to escape as gas — so it evaporates faster at the same temperature.
3
A student heats 200g of ice at −10°C in a beaker using a steady heat source. Describe what happens to the temperature and the particles during each of these five stages, and explain why stage B and stage D look different from the others on a graph. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Stage A (−10°C to 0°C): Temperature rises steadily. Particles are solid (ice) — they vibrate faster and faster as energy is added, but remain locked in fixed positions. Stage B (stays at 0°C): Temperature stays constant even though heat continues to be added. Particles are breaking free from their fixed lattice structure as melting occurs — energy goes entirely into breaking the bonds between particles, not into raising temperature. This is why stage B is flat on the graph — all the latent heat of fusion is being absorbed. Stage C (0°C to 100°C): Temperature rises steadily again. Particles are now liquid — they slide past each other and move faster and faster as energy increases. Stage D (stays at 100°C): Temperature stays constant again. Particles are escaping from the liquid surface and throughout the liquid as gas — all energy goes into overcoming the remaining forces between liquid particles (latent heat of vaporisation). Flat on graph for the same reason as B, but the flat is longer because more energy is needed to vaporise than to melt. Stage E (above 100°C): Temperature rises steadily. Particles are gas (steam) — they move very fast in all directions, speeding up further as more energy is added.

A science magazine claims: "Ice at −1°C is colder than water at 1°C — and ice at −1°C contains more energy than water at 1°C." Evaluate this claim carefully. (a) Is the first part correct? (b) Is the second part correct? Explain using what you know about latent heat. (c) What does this tell us about the relationship between temperature and energy? 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Yes, the first part is correct. Ice at −1°C has a lower temperature than water at 1°C. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles — at −1°C, particles have less kinetic energy than at 1°C. (b) No, the second part is incorrect. Water at 1°C contains MORE energy than ice at −1°C, not less. Here is why: to convert ice at 0°C into water at 0°C, you must add the latent heat of fusion — a significant amount of energy that goes entirely into breaking the bonds between ice particles, without raising the temperature. Water at 1°C (which has already absorbed this latent heat during melting) contains more total energy than ice at −1°C, even though the temperature difference is only 2°C. The latent heat absorbed during melting is far more energy than the small difference in kinetic energy between −1°C and 1°C. (c) This reveals the crucial distinction the lesson makes: temperature and total energy content are not the same thing. Temperature measures the kinetic energy of particles (how fast they move). Total energy includes latent heat — energy stored in the breaking of intermolecular bonds during state changes. Two samples of the same substance at almost the same temperature can have very different total energy contents if one has undergone a phase change. This is why it takes so much energy to boil water even after it reaches 100°C.

The lesson says: "The fact that a model has limitations does not mean it is wrong or useless." Using the particle model as your example, write a structured scientific argument (minimum 5 sentences) that defends this principle. Your argument should: acknowledge a real limitation, explain why the model is still useful despite that limitation, and compare it to another area of life where an imperfect model or simplified framework is still valuable. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Sample answer: The particle model of matter has genuine limitations: it represents all particles as identical small spheres with no electrical charge, no shape, and no inter-particle forces — none of which are true for real atoms. A real atom has a complex electron structure, positive-negative charge interactions, and a specific geometric shape that determines how it bonds with other atoms. Yet despite these inaccuracies, the particle model correctly predicts and explains the most important observable properties of matter: why solids keep their shape (particles locked), why liquids flow and take the container's shape (particles mobile but close), why gases expand to fill any volume (particles fast and far apart), why heating raises temperature (particles move faster), and why changes of state happen at specific temperatures (energy used to break bonds). These are the phenomena students encounter in the real world, and the particle model handles all of them elegantly. The model should be judged not by whether it captures every quantum-mechanical detail, but by whether it provides reliable, testable predictions at the scale we are working at — and it does. A useful parallel: a map of a city does not show every person, every pipe, or every brick, but it reliably tells you how to get from one place to another. We do not discard the map because it omits detail; we understand its purpose and use it accordingly. Scientific models are purpose-built simplifications, and their value lies in what they get right, not in their incompleteness.

Design a simple experiment to test whether sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water. Write up your experiment using the following headings: Hypothesis, Variables (independent, dependent, control), Method (in steps), Expected results, and How you would present your data. 6 marks

3
Use the lesson's chain (climate → vegetation → biome) to explain why the SAVANNAH biome has a distinctive two-season pattern — wet and dry — rather than the continuous rainfall of the rainforest. Explain: what causes the wet and dry seasons, what vegetation has adapted to this pattern, and what animals have adapted to survive both seasons. Write at least 4 sentences. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The savannah's two-season pattern is caused by the movement of the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) — a belt of low pressure near the equator where trade winds meet, causing heavy rainfall. As the Earth orbits the sun, the ITCZ shifts north and south — bringing the wet season when it moves into a region and leaving the dry season when it retreats. Savannah regions are close enough to the equator to receive high rainfall when the ITCZ passes, but not directly on the equator (where rainfall is year-round, creating rainforest). The wet season lasts roughly 6–8 months; the dry season 4–6 months. Vegetation has adapted with: deep roots that access underground water during the dry season; trees with thick fire-resistant bark (fires occur in the dry season); grasses that can grow back rapidly after fire, exploiting the nutrients released by burning; and deciduous trees that lose leaves in the dry season to reduce water loss. Animals have adapted with: migration — many large herbivores (wildebeest in the Serengeti) follow the rains, covering thousands of kilometres annually to access fresh grass; burrowing — some small animals estivate (a summer equivalent of hibernation) during the driest months; opportunistic predation — predators like lions concentrate near waterholes in the dry season when prey animals must come to drink. The savannah food web is seasonal in a way the rainforest's is not: during the wet season, grass production is enormous and herbivore populations boom; during the dry season, prey concentrates and predators benefit — creating a cycle that structures the entire ecosystem.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Sample example: In 2021, a record-breaking cold spell hit Texas, USA, with temperatures far below what the region normally experiences — infrastructure froze, power grids failed, and some pointed to this as evidence against global warming. This is a classic example of confusing weather (one extreme cold event in one location in one winter) with climate (the long-term average global temperature trend measured over decades). A single cold event in one place at one time cannot tell us anything about the 30-year average global temperature — especially since climate change is predicted to make extreme weather events (including unusual cold snaps in some regions) more frequent and severe, not just warmer on average everywhere. One flood, one drought, or one cold week is weather. An increasing frequency of floods, droughts, and temperature anomalies over 30 years is a climate trend.
