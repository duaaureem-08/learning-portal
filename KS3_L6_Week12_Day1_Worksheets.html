Week 2 — Creation
🌍 Week 2 — Creation, Genesis 1–2
Week 2
4
Summarise, in order, what God creates across the days described in Genesis 1. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Day 1: light and darkness. Day 2: sky/atmosphere. Day 3: land, sea, and plants. Day 4: sun, moon, and stars. Day 5: sea creatures and birds. Day 6: land animals and humans. Day 7: God rests. (Accept any accurate summary showing the general progression from basic conditions to living creatures.)
5
Explain what it means that humans are made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27), and what responsibility this gives humans toward the rest of creation. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Being made "in the image of God" (Latin: imago Dei) means humans uniquely reflect God's character and are given a special role and dignity above the rest of creation — not that humans physically resemble God, but that they share qualities like the capacity for relationship, creativity, and moral responsibility. This gives humans a responsibility of stewardship: to care for and manage creation wisely on God's behalf (Genesis 1:28, 2:15), rather than exploit it — a position of authority that comes WITH responsibility, not just privilege.
6
Genesis repeatedly says God saw that what he made "was good." Why might this detail matter for how Christians view the physical world? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The repeated declaration that creation is "good" suggests that the physical world is not something to be viewed negatively or as unimportant — it reflects God's intentional, careful design and is valued by God himself. This shapes a view of the world as something with inherent worth (not just a barrier to spiritual things), and it strengthens the case for treating creation with care rather than carelessness, since it is something God himself calls good.
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
7
Explain why understanding the GENRE of a Bible passage (Week 1) is especially important when reading Genesis 1–2 (Week 2), which has been read by different Christians as history, poetry, or a mixture of both. Write at least 5 sentences. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Understanding genre matters enormously for Genesis 1–2 because how a reader classifies the text shapes what kind of claims they think it is making. If read primarily as historical narrative, its details (six days, specific order) may be understood as describing a sequence of literal events. If read with attention to its structured, repetitive, almost poetic patterns (the refrain "and God saw that it was good," the ordered pairing of days 1-3 with days 4-6), some readers emphasise its theological message — that God is the intentional, orderly source of all creation — over strict chronological reporting. Because Genesis does not explicitly announce its own genre the way a Psalm heading might, careful readers pay attention to its literary features to decide how literally to press its details. This is exactly why Week 1's lesson on recognising genre is foundational: without that skill, a reader might either dismiss Genesis 1-2 for not matching modern scientific description, or misread its poetic structuring as a strict scientific timeline — both of which risk missing what the text itself is actually claiming.

📚 Week 1 — Narrative Writing Structure
Week 1
1
Define "exposition," "rising action," and "climax," and explain what each contributes to a story. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Exposition: the opening part of a story that introduces the characters, setting, and initial situation — it gives the reader the background needed to understand what follows. Rising action: the series of events that build tension and complicate the situation, developing conflict and raising stakes toward the story's turning point. Climax: the turning point or moment of greatest tension in the story, where the central conflict reaches its peak and is often decided.
2
Write a short exposition (3-4 sentences) for an original story of your choice, introducing a character, setting, and hint of a coming conflict. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Award marks for: a clearly introduced character (name, brief detail); an established setting (time/place); and a hint of conflict or tension to come, without necessarily resolving it. Example: "Maya had lived on the narrowboat for as long as she could remember, gliding along canals most people never noticed. But this morning, a folded note was wedged beneath the door — one she hadn't left, and one nobody else should have been able to reach."

🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Two buses leave a station at the same time. Bus A returns to the station every 18 minutes; Bus B returns every 24 minutes. (a) After how many minutes will they next be at the station together? (b) Round your answer to the nearest 10 minutes. (c) Explain which topic from this week's review (HCF or LCM) you needed to solve part (a), and why. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) The buses will next be together at the LCM of 18 and 24. 18 = 2×3², 24 = 2³×3. LCM = 2³×3² = 8×9 = 72 minutes. (b) Rounded to the nearest 10 minutes = 70 minutes. (c) This requires LCM, not HCF, because we need the smallest time that is a multiple of BOTH cycles — the point where both repeating patterns coincide again. HCF would instead answer a different kind of question, such as finding the largest equal-sized group into which two quantities could be split.

📚 Week 1 — Scientific Enquiry & Lab Skills
Week 1
1
Define "independent variable," "dependent variable," and "control variable," using an example investigation of your choice. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Example: investigating how temperature affects how quickly sugar dissolves in water. Independent variable: the one factor the experimenter deliberately changes — here, temperature. Dependent variable: the factor being measured as a result — here, time taken to dissolve. Control variable(s): factors kept the same to ensure a fair test — here, the amount of sugar, amount of water, and stirring method.
2
Explain what makes a test "fair," and describe TWO safety rules that should be followed in a school science lab. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A test is fair when only the independent variable is changed, while all other variables are kept constant (controlled) — this ensures any difference in the result can be confidently attributed to the one variable being tested, not some other uncontrolled factor. Safety rules (any two): wear safety goggles when handling chemicals; tie back long hair and secure loose clothing near flames or moving equipment; never taste substances in the lab; report spills or breakages immediately; follow teacher instructions for handling equipment.

Week 2
3
Name THREE structures found in plant cells but NOT in animal cells, and state the function of each. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Cell wall: provides rigid structural support, allowing the plant to stand upright. Chloroplasts: contain chlorophyll and carry out photosynthesis, allowing the plant to make its own food. Large permanent vacuole: stores water and helps maintain the cell's rigidity (turgidity).

State the function of the nucleus, the cell membrane, and the cytoplasm — structures found in BOTH plant and animal cells. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Nucleus: controls the cell's activities and contains the genetic material (DNA). Cell membrane: controls what substances enter and leave the cell. Cytoplasm: a jelly-like substance where many chemical reactions take place within the cell.

🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Design a fair test to investigate whether plant cells from a sunlit leaf contain more visible chloroplasts than plant cells from a shaded leaf, viewed under a microscope. Identify the independent variable, dependent variable, and at least TWO control variables. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Independent variable: light exposure of the leaf (sunlit vs shaded). Dependent variable: number of visible chloroplasts per cell (or per field of view) under the microscope. Control variables: same plant species; same leaf age; same microscope magnification; same staining/preparation method; same area of leaf sampled (e.g. always from the leaf's edge). Brief method: take a thin sample from a sunlit leaf and a shaded leaf of the same species, prepare slides identically, view both under the same microscope magnification, and count visible chloroplasts in a fixed field of view for each, repeating several times for reliability.

📚 Week 1 — Sources & Evidence
Week 1
1
Define "primary source" and "secondary source," and give ONE example of each. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Primary source: evidence created at the time of the event being studied, by someone directly involved or present. Example: an ancient Egyptian tomb inscription, or a diary written during a historical event. Secondary source: evidence created afterward, by someone analysing or describing the event without having directly experienced it. Example: a modern history textbook or documentary about Ancient Egypt.
2
Explain what a "timeline" is used for in history, and why understanding chronology (the order events happened) matters for studying the past. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A timeline is a visual tool showing events in the order they occurred, often across a specific span of time, helping historians and students see relationships and patterns between events. Understanding chronology matters because history is fundamentally about CAUSE and EFFECT — knowing which event came first is often essential to understanding why something else happened afterward; getting the order wrong can lead to false conclusions about what caused what.
Week 2 — Early Civilisations
🏺 Week 2 — Mesopotamia & Egypt
Week 2
3
Explain why the Nile Valley was essential to the development of Ancient Egyptian civilisation. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The Nile flooded predictably every year, depositing fertile black silt along its banks, which allowed reliable farming and food surpluses in an otherwise desert region. This agricultural stability supported a growing population, freed some people to specialise in non-farming roles (building, writing, governing), and — combined with desert borders providing natural defence — gave Ancient Egypt the stability needed to develop as a lasting civilisation.
4
Name TWO inventions or achievements from Ancient Mesopotamia or Egypt, and briefly explain the significance of each. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any two of: Cuneiform writing (Mesopotamia) — one of the earliest writing systems, allowing records, laws, and trade to be documented. Hieroglyphics (Egypt) — Egypt's writing system, used for religious and administrative records. The wheel (Mesopotamia) — revolutionised transport and technology. The pyramids (Egypt) — massive stone monuments demonstrating advanced engineering and organisation. Irrigation systems (both) — allowed farming to extend beyond naturally flooded land.
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Using what you know about primary and secondary sources (Week 1), evaluate how reliable you think our knowledge of Ancient Egyptian pharaohs and social structure really is. What kinds of primary sources have survived, and what limitations might they have? Write at least 5 sentences. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Our knowledge of Ancient Egyptian pharaohs and society relies substantially on primary sources such as tomb inscriptions, hieroglyphic records, statues, and preserved papyrus documents, which have survived remarkably well due to Egypt's dry climate. However, these sources have significant limitations: most surviving records were created or commissioned by the pharaohs and priestly classes themselves, meaning they often present an idealised, self-promoting version of events (a pharaoh's own monument is unlikely to record his failures). Ordinary people's daily lives are far less well documented, since they left fewer durable written records, meaning our picture of Egyptian society may be skewed toward the perspective and priorities of the powerful rather than a balanced view of the whole population. Secondary sources — modern historians' interpretations — help fill some gaps by comparing multiple types of evidence (archaeology, art, administrative records), but ultimately our knowledge remains shaped by which sources happened to survive, and whose perspective those sources represent. This is a good example of why historians must always consider WHO created a source and WHY, not simply treat ancient evidence as an objective record of fact.
