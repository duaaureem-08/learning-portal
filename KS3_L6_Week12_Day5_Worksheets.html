Week 10

Describe David's journey from shepherd to king, naming at least THREE key stages. 5 marks

2
Write the memory verse Psalm 23:1 from memory, and explain what the image of God as a "shepherd" communicates. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"The LORD is my shepherd, I lack nothing." (Psalm 23:1, NIV). The image of God as a shepherd communicates close, personal care — a shepherd guides, protects, and provides for the needs of the sheep in his care. Written by David, who was himself literally a shepherd before becoming king, this image draws on his own experience to describe an intimate, trusting relationship with God, rather than a distant or purely formal one.

Explain what a "prophet" is in the Bible, and describe ONE example of a prophet confronting injustice or wrongdoing (e.g. Elijah). 5 marks

5
Compare David's role (a king ruling God's people) with the prophets' role (speaking God's truth, sometimes to kings themselves). Why might a nation need BOTH kinds of figures? Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
David's role as king involved governing, leading in battle, and establishing a lasting dynasty — practical, political leadership over God's people. The prophets, by contrast, were not primarily rulers but truth-tellers, often called specifically to confront kings (including David himself, when the prophet Nathan confronted him over his sin with Bathsheba) and hold power accountable to God's standards. A nation arguably needs both kinds of figures because political power alone, without any check or challenge, risks becoming corrupt or self-serving — kings could misuse their authority without an external voice willing to name wrongdoing plainly. The prophets provided exactly this kind of accountability, standing somewhat outside the political structure and therefore able to challenge even the most powerful figures, including kings as significant as David. This pattern — a ruling structure paired with a prophetic check on that structure — reflects a recurring biblical concern that power must remain accountable to a moral and spiritual standard beyond itself, rather than becoming an unquestioned authority.

📚 Week 10 — Poetry: Forms & Devices
Week 10
1
Define "alliteration" and "onomatopoeia," giving one original example of each. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Alliteration: the repetition of the same initial consonant sound in nearby words. Example: "The silent snake slithered silently." Onomatopoeia: a word that imitates or suggests the sound it describes. Example: "The kettle hissed and sizzled on the stove."
2
Explain what a "stanza" is, and how a haiku's structure (in terms of syllables per line) differs from free verse. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A stanza is a grouped set of lines within a poem, similar to a paragraph in prose writing. A haiku follows a strict structure of three lines with 5, 7, and 5 syllables respectively — a fixed pattern the poet must follow. Free verse, by contrast, has no fixed rhyme scheme, meter, or syllable count, giving the poet much greater flexibility in structure.

Week 10
1
Explain the correct order for writing coordinates (x, y), and name the FOUR quadrants of the coordinate plane, describing the sign of x and y in each. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Coordinates are always written as (x, y) — horizontal position first, then vertical. Quadrant 1 (top right): x positive, y positive. Quadrant 2 (top left): x negative, y positive. Quadrant 3 (bottom left): x negative, y negative. Quadrant 4 (bottom right): x positive, y negative.

A point starts at coordinates (2, 3). It moves 5 units left and 7 units down. (a) Using negative number rules (Week 11), calculate its new coordinates. (b) State which quadrant the new point is in (Week 10). (c) Explain, using negative numbers, how "moving left" and "moving down" are represented mathematically. 8 marks

📚 Week 10 — Physical vs Chemical Changes
Week 10
1
Explain the difference between a physical change and a chemical change, and classify each of the following: melting ice, burning wood, tearing paper, rusting iron. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A physical change alters the form or appearance of a substance WITHOUT creating a new substance — it is usually reversible. A chemical change creates one or more entirely NEW substances with different properties, and is usually difficult or impossible to reverse. Melting ice: physical change (still H₂O, just a different state). Burning wood: chemical change (produces ash, smoke, gases — new substances). Tearing paper: physical change (still paper, just smaller pieces). Rusting iron: chemical change (iron reacts with oxygen to form a new substance, iron oxide).
Week 11 — Acids & Alkalis
🧪 Week 11 — Acids & Alkalis
Week 11
2
Explain what the pH scale measures, and state whether a pH of 3, 7, and 11 would be classified as acidic, neutral, or alkaline. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline a substance is, typically ranging from 0 to 14. pH 3 = acidic (below 7). pH 7 = neutral (e.g. pure water). pH 11 = alkaline (above 7).
3
What happens when an acid reacts with an alkali? Name the type of reaction and its products. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
When an acid reacts with an alkali, this is called a neutralisation reaction. The products are a salt and water — the acidic and alkaline properties cancel each other out, moving the resulting mixture's pH closer to neutral (pH 7).
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
4
Neutralisation (Week 11) is an example of a chemical change (Week 10). Explain why neutralisation counts as a CHEMICAL change rather than a physical one, referring to what happens to the original substances and what new substance(s) form. Write at least 5 sentences. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Neutralisation counts as a chemical change, not a physical one, because when an acid reacts with an alkali, the original substances are chemically transformed into entirely NEW substances — a salt and water — with different chemical properties from either of the original reactants. This matches the definition of a chemical change established in Week 10: a process that produces new substances, generally not easily reversible by simple physical means (such as evaporating or reheating). By contrast, if this were merely a physical change, the acid and alkali would still be present in some altered physical form (like a solid becoming a liquid), retaining their original chemical identity. Instead, the acidic and alkaline properties are genuinely cancelled out in the reaction, and testing the resulting mixture with a pH indicator would show a completely different pH result — direct evidence that a chemical, not physical, transformation has taken place. This connection shows how the general categories learned in Week 10 (physical vs chemical change) provide a useful framework for classifying more specific reactions studied afterward, like the acid-alkali reactions of Week 11.

📚 Week 10 — The Crusades & Cultural Exchange
Week 10
1
Briefly explain what the Crusades were, and name ONE example of cultural or technological exchange that resulted from contact between European and Middle Eastern societies during this period. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The Crusades were a series of religious military campaigns, primarily between the 11th and 13th centuries, in which European Christian forces sought to capture and control the Holy Land (including Jerusalem) from Muslim control. Despite the conflict, extended contact between European and Middle Eastern societies led to significant cultural exchange, including: the spread of new foods, spices, and textiles to Europe; the transmission of advances in mathematics, medicine, and science (much of it preserved and developed in the Islamic world); and increased trade connections across the Mediterranean.
Week 11 — Trade, Exploration & Early Globalisation
🧭 Week 11 — Trade, Exploration & Early Globalisation
Week 11
2
Name ONE major European explorer from this period of early exploration, and briefly describe the significance of their voyage(s). 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any reasonable example, such as: Christopher Columbus — his 1492 voyage across the Atlantic, seeking a westward route to Asia, instead led to European contact with the Americas, beginning a new era of transatlantic trade, colonisation, and exchange. Vasco da Gama — opened a sea route from Europe to India around the southern tip of Africa, transforming trade between Europe and Asia. Ferdinand Magellan's expedition — completed the first circumnavigation of the globe, proving definitively that the Earth could be sailed around entirely.
3
Explain what "early globalisation" means in this historical context, and give ONE example of goods, ideas, or people moving between previously separate regions of the world. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"Early globalisation" refers to the increasing connection between previously separate and distant regions of the world through trade, exploration, and the resulting exchange of goods, people, and ideas, especially from the 15th century onward. Example: new crops such as potatoes and tomatoes travelled from the Americas to Europe, while horses and wheat travelled from Europe to the Americas — part of what historians call the "Columbian Exchange."
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
4
Compare the Crusades (Week 10) and the Age of Exploration (Week 11) as two different examples of long-distance contact between distant societies. What did each have in common in terms of BOTH conflict/tension AND cultural exchange? Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Both the Crusades and the Age of Exploration involved significant contact between previously separate or distant societies, and in both cases this contact combined serious conflict with substantial, if often unintentional, cultural exchange. The Crusades were fundamentally military and religious conflicts, yet the sustained contact they created led to the transmission of scientific knowledge, foods, and trade connections between Europe and the Middle East. Similarly, European exploration and subsequent colonisation frequently involved conflict, exploitation, and violence toward indigenous peoples in the Americas and elsewhere, yet this same contact resulted in the large-scale exchange of crops, animals, and ideas known as the Columbian Exchange. In both cases, the historical pattern is similar: extended contact between different societies — even when driven primarily by conflict, conquest, or religious motivation — tends to produce lasting cultural and economic exchange as an almost unavoidable side effect, showing that the movement of ideas, goods, and technology across cultural boundaries often occurs even in contexts explicitly defined by hostility rather than cooperation.
