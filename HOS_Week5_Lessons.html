⭐ Memory Verse
"I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you; I will make your name great, and you will be a blessing."
Genesis 12:2 — NIV
🧠
Word-by-word breakdown: "great nation" = an enormous number of descendants — astonishing for a man with no children yet. "I will bless you" = God's favour and goodness resting on Abram's life. "make your name great" = lasting honour and reputation, not fame for its own sake. "you will be a blessing" = the promise was never meant to stop with Abram — it flows OUTWARD to other people. This is the key difference from Noah's covenant: Noah's covenant protected the earth; Abraham's covenant sends blessing out into the world through one family.
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Learn these words before you continue
call / calling
An invitation from God to a person to do something specific — often something that requires leaving the familiar behind.
homeland
The country or place where a person was born and grew up. Abram's homeland was Ur of the Chaldeans, in Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq).
covenant
A solemn, binding PROMISE between God and people. (Remember this word from Week 4 — Noah's covenant. Abraham receives the SECOND major covenant in the Bible.)
blessing
God's favour and goodness — often goodness that is meant to flow outward and benefit OTHER people too, not just the person who receives it.
descendant
A person who comes from a particular ancestor. Abraham's descendants include the nation of Israel and, in the New Testament's view, all who share his faith.
patriarch
A founding "father" figure of a family or nation. Abraham is often called the first patriarch of the Bible — followed by his son Isaac and grandson Jacob.
altar
A structure built for worship or sacrifice. Abram builds altars at several points in his journey to mark significant places where God spoke to him.
nomad
A person who moves from place to place rather than settling permanently in one location — Abram lived as a nomad for the rest of his life after leaving Ur.
famine
A severe shortage of food, often caused by drought or crop failure, that can force people to migrate in search of survival.
🏺 Where Did Abraham Come From? — Leaving Everything Familiar
📍
Abram (his name before God renamed him "Abraham" in Genesis 17) lived in Ur of the Chaldeans — a wealthy, advanced city in Mesopotamia, in what is now southern Iraq. Ur was full of temples to many different gods. Abram's own family, according to Jewish tradition, worshipped idols before God called him.
🏙️ What Abram Left Behind
A settled home in an established, wealthy city
His wider family and extended household
A familiar culture, language area, and way of life
Security and certainty about the future
🌅 What He Was Promised Instead
A land he had not yet seen — "the land I will show you"
Descendants forming a "great nation," though he had none yet
God's blessing and protection on the journey
A role in blessing "all peoples on earth"
🔑
KS3 thinking point: Abram was 75 years old (Genesis 12:4) when he left — not a young adventurer, but a man giving up a settled life late in life, based on a promise with no visible proof yet. This connects directly to Noah's faith from Week 4: trusting God's word before seeing the result.
📜 The Call of Abraham — Genesis 12:1–3
📖
God's call has TWO parts: a command to leave, and a promise of what will follow. Look closely at the structure below.
The Command
"Go from your country, your people and your father's household to the land I will show you."
→
The Promise
"I will make you into a great nation... I will bless you... you will be a blessing... all peoples on earth will be blessed through you."
✅
Notice the order: God asks Abram to obey FIRST — leave everything — and only afterward explains the destination ("the land I will show you," not "the land of Canaan"). This is the same pattern of faith-before-evidence seen with Noah: trust comes before full information.
🗺️ Abram's Journey — From Ur to Canaan
Stage 1
Ur of the Chaldeans
Abram's birthplace and homeland in Mesopotamia. His father Terah first leads the family out of Ur, intending to go to Canaan.
Stage 2
Harran
The family settles in Harran for a time, and Terah dies there. It is in Harran that God repeats and clarifies His call directly to Abram — now 75 years old.
Stage 3
Canaan — The Promised Land
Abram, his wife Sarai, his nephew Lot, and their household arrive in Canaan (Genesis 12:5–6). God appears to Abram at Shechem and promises this very land to his descendants. Abram builds an altar there.
Stage 4
A Detour to Egypt
A severe famine forces Abram to travel temporarily to Egypt for food (Genesis 12:10) — even the man of faith faces real, practical hardship. He later returns to Canaan once the famine ends.
💡
KS3 map skills link: This journey — Ur to Harran to Canaan — covers roughly 1,000 miles (about 1,600 km) across the Fertile Crescent, almost entirely on foot or by donkey/camel caravan. Keep this journey in mind for this week's History lesson on maps and geographic tools!

🎯 What Trusting God Looks Like — Application
1
Trust sometimes means stepping into the unknown
Abram left without knowing his final destination. Trusting God does not always come with a full explanation in advance.
2
God's promises are often bigger than the evidence available at the time
Abram was promised a "great nation" while childless. Real trust holds onto a promise even when current circumstances seem to contradict it.
3
Faith does not remove every hardship
Even after answering God's call, Abram faced a famine and had to travel to Egypt. Trusting God is not a guarantee of an easy path — but of God's continued presence on it.
4
Blessing is meant to be shared outward
"You will be a blessing" — Abram's calling was never just personal gain. Genuine blessing flows out to benefit other people too.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these — they turn flat writing into vivid writing
descriptive writing
Writing that creates a vivid, detailed picture in the reader's mind — usually using sensory detail and figurative language.
imagery
Language that appeals to the FIVE SENSES — sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch — to help the reader imagine an experience.
figurative language
Language that means something BEYOND its literal, word-for-word meaning — used to create a stronger or more vivid effect.
literal language
Language that means EXACTLY what it says, with no comparison or exaggeration. "The fire was hot" is literal.
simile
A comparison between two different things using the words "like" or "as." "The fire roared like a hungry lion."
metaphor
A comparison that states one thing IS another — without using "like" or "as." "The fire was a hungry lion."
personification
Giving human qualities, feelings, or actions to something that is NOT human. "The wind whispered through the trees."
hyperbole
Extreme exaggeration used for effect, not meant to be taken literally. "I've told you a million times."
sensory detail
Specific description that draws on one or more of the five senses, making writing feel real and immediate.
🆚 Literal vs Descriptive — Why Detail Matters
🔍
The test: Could a reader picture, hear, smell, taste, or feel what you are describing? If a sentence only states a fact with no sensory detail, it is LITERAL. If it helps the reader experience the scene, it is DESCRIPTIVE.
❌ Literal (Flat)✅ Descriptive (Vivid)What Changed?
The room was messy.Crumpled papers spilled across the desk, and the bin overflowed with crushed cans.Specific, visual detail replaces a vague adjective
The food smelled good.The smell of cinnamon and fresh bread drifted from the kitchen.Names the EXACT smell rather than a generic judgement
It was cold outside.An icy wind bit at her fingers and stung her cheeks.Adds physical sensation (touch) instead of a plain statement

👀 Sight

📝 Show, Don't Tell — Strengthening Adjectives and Adverbs
🔑
The golden idea of this lesson: "Show, don't tell" means replacing a vague label (an adjective that simply states an emotion or quality) with concrete sensory detail or action that lets the READER work out the feeling for themselves.
❌ Telling✅ Showing
She was nervous.Her hands trembled, and she kept glancing at the door.
The dog was angry.The dog's lips curled back, and a low growl rumbled in its throat.
The forest was scary.Twisted branches clawed at the darkening sky, and every snapped twig echoed like a gunshot.
💡
Choosing precise adjectives: Instead of "big," consider enormous, towering, vast, immense. Instead of "good," consider delicious, magnificent, soothing, triumphant. Precise word choice is more powerful than piling up multiple weak adjectives.
🏗️ Building a Descriptive Paragraph — Step by Step
1
Choose a clear focus
Pick ONE scene, object, or moment — not an entire holiday or a whole day. A tightly focused description is far more powerful than a broad summary.
2
Select two or three senses to focus on
Decide which senses suit the scene best (a bakery might use smell and taste; a thunderstorm might use sound and sight).
3
Add ONE simile or metaphor
Do not overload a paragraph with figurative language — one well-chosen comparison is more effective than five crowded together.
4
Replace vague adjectives with precise ones
Read back through and swap any "nice," "big," "good," or "bad" for something more specific and vivid.
5
Read it aloud and check the rhythm
Descriptive writing should flow naturally when spoken. Vary your sentence lengths — a short sentence after several long ones can create real impact.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Builds on Week 4 — adding and subtracting fractions
numerator
The TOP number of a fraction — shows how many parts you have. In 3/4, the numerator is 3.
denominator
The BOTTOM number of a fraction — shows how many equal parts the whole is divided into. In 3/4, the denominator is 4.
reciprocal
A fraction FLIPPED upside down — swap the numerator and denominator. The reciprocal of 2/3 is 3/2. The reciprocal of a whole number like 5 is 1/5.
cross-cancel
Simplifying common factors between a numerator and a denominator BEFORE multiplying — makes the final answer easier to simplify.
improper fraction
A fraction where the numerator is LARGER than the denominator, e.g. 7/4. Always convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying or dividing.
mixed number
A whole number combined with a fraction, e.g. 2¾. Must be converted to an improper fraction before you can multiply or divide it.
unit fraction
A fraction with a numerator of 1, e.g. 1/2, 1/3, 1/8. Useful for visualising multiplication "of" a quantity.
✖️ Multiplying Fractions — Straight Across
✅
The rule: multiply the numerators together, then multiply the denominators together. Unlike adding and subtracting, you do NOT need a common denominator to multiply.
a/b × c/d = (a×c) / (b×d) ← top times top, bottom times bottom

2/3 × 1/4 = (2×1)/(3×4) = 2/12 = 1/6 ← simplify the final answer
Step 1 — Take 1/4 of the whole
1/4
Step 2 — Now take 2/3 of THAT 1/4 piece
2/3 of it
= 2/12 of the whole = 1/6
💡
Why "of" means multiply: "2/3 of 1/4" and "2/3 × 1/4" mean exactly the same thing. Whenever a word problem uses "of" between two fractions, you are being asked to MULTIPLY them.
✂️ Simplify BEFORE You Multiply — Cross-Cancelling
⚡
Cross-cancelling makes your numbers smaller and easier to work with — and means you often won't need to simplify at the end at all.
1
Look diagonally across the two fractions
For 4/9 × 3/8: check if any numerator shares a common factor with either denominator (not just its own).
2
Divide matching factors by their HCF
4 (numerator) and 8 (denominator) share a factor of 4: 4÷4=1, 8÷4=2. Also 3 (numerator) and 9 (denominator) share a factor of 3: 3÷3=1, 9÷3=3.
3
Multiply the simplified fractions
Now you have 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6 — much simpler than working with 4/9 × 3/8 = 12/72 and simplifying afterward.
4
Check: both methods give the same answer
12/72 simplified (HCF=12) also equals 1/6. Cross-cancelling just gets you there with smaller numbers.
🔢 Multiplying Mixed Numbers — Convert First!
⚠️
The most common mistake: you CANNOT multiply the whole numbers and fraction parts separately. 2½ × 1½ is NOT (2×1) + (½×½). You must convert both to improper fractions FIRST.
Example: 2½ × 1⅓

Convert to improper fractions: 2½ = 5/2    1⅓ = 4/3
Multiply straight across: 5/2 × 4/3 = (5×4)/(2×3) = 20/6
Simplify: HCF(20,6)=2 → 20/6 = 10/3
Convert back to a mixed number: 10/3 = 3⅓

Final answer: 2½ × 1⅓ = 3⅓
➗ Dividing Fractions — Keep, Change, Flip
🔑
The golden rule: to divide by a fraction, KEEP the first fraction the same, CHANGE the ÷ sign to ×, and FLIP the second fraction (find its reciprocal). Then multiply as normal.
1
KEEP the first fraction
For 3/4 ÷ 2/5: keep 3/4 exactly as it is.
2
CHANGE ÷ to ×
3/4 ÷ 2/5 becomes 3/4 × ...
3
FLIP the second fraction (find its reciprocal)
2/5 flips to become 5/2. Now we have: 3/4 × 5/2.
4
Multiply straight across
3/4 × 5/2 = (3×5)/(4×2) = 15/8 = 1⅞. Final answer: 3/4 ÷ 2/5 = 1⅞
🍕 Why Does "Keep, Change, Flip" Actually Work?
🤔
Dividing by a fraction answers the question: "How many of that fraction fit inside the first number?"
Real-life example: 4 ÷ 1/2 = ?

Question: how many halves fit into 4 whole pizzas?
Each pizza splits into 2 halves, so 4 pizzas = 4 × 2 = 8 halves

Using Keep, Change, Flip: 4/1 × 2/1 = 8/1 = 8 ✓ Same answer!
✅
KS3 key idea: Dividing by a fraction SMALLER than 1 always makes the answer BIGGER than the number you started with — because you are asking how many small pieces fit into a bigger amount. This often surprises students, since dividing usually feels like it should make numbers smaller!
🔁 Dividing Mixed Numbers
⚠️
Exactly like multiplying mixed numbers — convert to improper fractions FIRST, then apply Keep, Change, Flip.
Example: 3¼ ÷ 1½

Convert: 3¼ = 13/4    1½ = 3/2
Keep, Change, Flip: 13/4 × 2/3
Cross-cancel: 4 and 2 share a factor of 2 → 13/2 × 1/3
Multiply: 13/2 × 1/3 = 13/6 = 2⅙

Final answer: 3¼ ÷ 1½ = 2⅙

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Biology Guide
Builds on Week 4 — remember nutrient, absorption and enzyme
photosynthesis
The process by which plants make their OWN food (glucose) using light energy, carbon dioxide and water. The word literally means "putting together using light."
chlorophyll
The GREEN pigment found in chloroplasts that absorbs light energy — this is why most leaves are green.
chloroplast
The organelle (small structure) inside plant cells where photosynthesis actually takes place. Contains chlorophyll.
glucose
A simple sugar — the food/energy product made by photosynthesis. Used by the plant for energy, growth and storage.
stomata
Tiny pores, mostly on the underside of a leaf, that allow gases (carbon dioxide in, oxygen and water vapour out) to pass in and out.
guard cells
Pairs of cells that open and close each stoma, controlling gas exchange and water loss.
xylem
Tube-like tissue that transports WATER and minerals upward from the roots to the rest of the plant.
phloem
Tube-like tissue that transports the GLUCOSE made during photosynthesis to all parts of the plant — this process is called translocation.
transpiration
The loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly through the stomata in its leaves.
⚗️ The Photosynthesis Equation
💡
Photosynthesis is a chemical reaction. Like any equation, it has REACTANTS (what goes in) and PRODUCTS (what comes out) — and it needs an energy source to happen.
Word equation:
carbon dioxide + water  --(light energy)-->  glucose + oxygen

Symbol equation:
6CO2 + 6H2O  --(light, chlorophyll)-->  C6H12O6 + 6O2
TermRoleWhere It Comes From
Carbon dioxideReactant — raw materialAbsorbed from the air through the stomata
WaterReactant — raw materialAbsorbed from the soil by the roots, carried up by the xylem
Light energyPowers the reaction (not a substance)Sunlight (or artificial light), absorbed by chlorophyll
GlucoseProduct — the plant's foodUsed for energy, growth, or stored as starch
OxygenProduct — released as a "waste" gasReleased back into the air through the stomata
☀️ Why Plants Need Light — Where Does the Energy Come From?
🔑
KS3 key concept: Plants are called "producers" because, unlike animals, they do not need to eat other organisms — they PRODUCE their own food using light energy. This is the very first step of almost every food chain on Earth (you will study this further in Week 6: Ecosystems & Food Webs).
✅
Without light, photosynthesis stops almost immediately — this is why plants kept in darkness eventually turn pale (lose their green colour) and die. Chlorophyll cannot function without a light source to absorb.

⚖️ Limiting Factors — What Can Slow Photosynthesis Down?
⚠️
A limiting factor is anything in short supply that slows down the rate of a process. If ANY of the three factors below is too low, photosynthesis slows down — even if the other two are plentiful.
Limiting FactorEffect If Too LowReal-World Example

🔬 Plant Cells vs Animal Cells — Review & Extension
🌱 Plant Cells — Extra Structures
Cell wall: rigid outer layer made of cellulose — gives shape and support
Chloroplasts: contain chlorophyll — site of photosynthesis
Large permanent vacuole: stores water and helps keep the cell rigid
These three structures are NEVER found in animal cells
🐾 Shared by Both Cell Types
Nucleus: controls the cell's activities, contains genetic material
Cytoplasm: jelly-like substance where chemical reactions occur
Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell
Mitochondria: site of respiration — releases energy in BOTH cell types
🔗
Connecting to Week 2: Remember plant vs animal cells from earlier this term? This week adds the DETAIL of what actually happens inside the chloroplast — turning that basic structure into a working understanding of plant biology.
🌍 Why Photosynthesis Matters — For Plants and For Us
1
It produces the oxygen we breathe
Most of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere is a "waste product" of photosynthesis — produced by plants, algae and some bacteria over billions of years.
2
It is the foundation of almost every food chain
Plants are "producers" — nearly all energy in an ecosystem traces back to photosynthesis, even in animals that never eat plants directly (they eat something that ate a plant).
3
It created fossil fuels
Coal, oil and gas formed from the remains of ancient plants (and other organisms) that captured solar energy through photosynthesis millions of years ago.
4
It removes carbon dioxide from the air
Photosynthesis absorbs CO₂, which is why deforestation (cutting down large numbers of trees) is a major concern for climate change.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL History & Geography Guide
Builds on Week 1's timelines — geographic tools use similar systems of measurement
latitude
Imaginary horizontal lines circling the Earth, measuring distance NORTH or SOUTH of the Equator (0°).
longitude
Imaginary vertical lines running pole to pole, measuring distance EAST or WEST of the Prime Meridian (0°).
equator
The imaginary line at 0° latitude, circling the Earth exactly halfway between the North and South Poles.
Prime Meridian
The imaginary line at 0° longitude, passing through Greenwich, London — the reference point for all longitude measurements.
coordinates
A pair of numbers (a latitude and a longitude) that together pinpoint one EXACT location on Earth.
hemisphere
Half of the Earth. The Equator divides North/South hemispheres; the Prime Meridian (with its opposite line) divides East/West hemispheres.
compass rose
A diagram on a map showing the cardinal directions (N, S, E, W) and intercardinal directions (NE, NW, SE, SW).
scale
A ratio showing the relationship between distance ON a map and the real distance ON THE GROUND.
key / legend
A box on a map that explains what each symbol, colour or line pattern represents.
🌐 Latitude & Longitude — Earth's Grid System
📍
Together, latitude and longitude form a GRID over the entire planet — similar to the rows and columns on the coordinates grid you used in Week 4's Maths lesson, but wrapped around a sphere.
FeatureLatitudeLongitude

KS3 memory tip: Latitude lines are "flat" like the rungs of a LADDER (LATitude = LADDER — both start with similar sounds). Longitude lines are "LONG," stretching from the top of the globe to the bottom.
📌 Reading Coordinates — Finding an Exact Location
1
Coordinates are always written latitude FIRST, then longitude
A common memory trick: "Latitude before Longitude," alphabetical order, just like reading along the floor (Lat) before going up a ladder (Long).
2
Each number includes a direction letter
London's coordinates are approximately 51.5°N, 0.1°W — meaning 51.5 degrees north of the Equator, and 0.1 degrees west of the Prime Meridian.
3
Find the latitude line first
Trace along the correct line north or south of the Equator to the stated number of degrees.
4
Then find the longitude line
From that latitude line, move to the correct number of degrees east or west of the Prime Meridian. Where the two lines cross is your exact location.
🐫
Connecting to this week's Bible lesson: Ur of the Chaldeans (Abram's homeland) sits at approximately 30.9°N, 46.1°E — while Canaan (his destination) lies around 31.5°N, 35°E. Plotting Abram's journey on coordinates shows just how far he travelled into the unknown.

📏 Map Scale — Real World vs Paper
💡
A map of the whole UK obviously cannot be the same size as the UK itself — scale tells you exactly how much smaller the map is, so you can calculate real-world distances.
Scale shown as a ratio: 1 : 50,000

Meaning: 1 cm on the map = 50,000 cm in real life
Convert to metres: 50,000 cm ÷ 100 = 500 m

So measuring 6 cm between two towns on this map:
6 × 500 m = 3,000 m = 3 km in real life
📐
Other ways scale is shown: as a scale BAR (a small ruler-like line drawn directly on the map), or in WORDS ("1 centimetre represents 1 kilometre"). All three methods describe the exact same relationship.
🗺️ Types of Maps — Choosing the Right Tool
🏔️ Physical Map
Shows natural features: mountains, rivers, deserts, forests — usually using colour and shading to show landscape.
🏳️ Political Map
Shows human-made borders: countries, states, cities and capitals — focused on how land is governed and divided.
⛰️ Topographic Map
Shows elevation (height) using contour lines — lines close together mean steep terrain; lines far apart mean gentle slopes.
🌦️ Climate Map
Shows weather patterns and climate zones across a region — useful for understanding biomes (Week 7 topic).
🛣️ Road Map
Shows transport routes — roads, railways, airports — designed to help travellers navigate between places.
🌍 Globe vs Flat Map — Why Are They Different?
🌐 Globe
A 3D model — the most ACCURATE way to represent the Earth's true shape, size and distances
Impossible to see the whole world at once
Not practical to carry around or fold into a book
🗺️ Flat Map
Practical, portable, and shows the whole world at once
Always involves some DISTORTION — stretching a sphere flat always changes size, shape, distance, or direction somewhere
Different "projections" (methods of flattening) distort different things
⚠️
Famous example — the Mercator projection: on this commonly used flat map, Greenland appears roughly the same size as Africa. In reality, Africa is about 14 times larger than Greenland! This happens because the Mercator projection stretches land near the poles to keep direction lines straight — useful for sailors, but misleading about true size.
