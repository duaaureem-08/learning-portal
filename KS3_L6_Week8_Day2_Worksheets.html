🟢 EasySection A — Recall and identification. Focus on the two-tablet structure.
📚 Two Tablets, Two Categories
💡 ESL Tip: The Ten Commandments were written on TWO stone tablets. This was not just practical — it showed two categories. Tablet 1 (commands 1-4) = our VERTICAL relationship with God. Tablet 2 (commands 5-10) = our HORIZONTAL relationships with people. Think of a cross: one line going up (God), one line going across (people). Both lines are essential.
1
Sort the ten commandments into the correct tablet. Then for each tablet, write what theme connects all the commands on it. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
TABLET 1: 1. No other gods. 2. No idols. 3. Do not misuse God's name. 4. Keep the Sabbath. Theme: Exclusive worship and reverence for the one true God — protecting the vertical relationship between humans and their Creator. TABLET 2: 5. Honour parents. 6. Do not murder. 7. Do not commit adultery. 8. Do not steal. 9. Do not give false testimony. 10. Do not covet. Theme: Protection of human dignity, family, property, truth, and inner life — maintaining right relationships within a community.
2
Which commandment is described as "the first commandment with a promise"? What is the promise and where is it found? Why do you think God attaches a promise to this specific command? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Commandment 5 — Honour your father and mother. The promise: "so that you may live long in the land the LORD your God is giving you" (Exodus 20:12). Ephesians 6:2-3 (Paul, writing in the New Testament) calls it "the first commandment with a promise." Why a promise is attached: Commandment 5 is the bridge between the two tablets — it connects the God-focused commands (1-4) to the people-focused commands (6-10). Family is the first and most fundamental human community. God attaches a promise because honouring parents is the foundation of social stability — children who respect and care for parents build societies where the elderly are protected, wisdom is passed down, and family loyalty is strong. The promise is both personal (long life) and national (long in the land) — stable families lead to stable nations.
3
The Sabbath commandment (4th) says people should rest one day in seven. The lesson says it protects people "from becoming slaves to work." Explain this in your own words. Why might someone NEED a law to make them rest? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"Slaves to work" means that if people never stop working, work becomes the thing that controls their life — they have no freedom from it. Without rest, people can be exploited by employers who demand endless hours, or they might feel they can never stop without something going wrong. Why a law is needed: In a competitive world, an individual might feel they cannot afford to take a day off — especially if their employer demands 7 days, or if others around them keep working. A law makes it a universal right and expectation — everyone rests, so no one is at a disadvantage for resting. It also establishes that human beings are more than their productivity — they have dignity regardless of how much they produce. The Sabbath teaches that God provides; people do not have to earn everything by ceaseless effort.

4
The lesson says Commandment 6 (do not murder) is rooted in the idea that human beings are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27 — "imago Dei" from Week 2). Explain the connection: why does being made in God's image make murder wrong in a way that goes beyond just "it harms someone"? 5 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 2 Bible — imago Dei (the image of God), what it means for human dignity.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Imago Dei means that every human being — regardless of age, nationality, gender, ability, or any other characteristic — bears the image of God. When God made humans in his image (Genesis 1:27), he placed something of his own character into them: the capacity for relationship, creativity, moral reasoning, and love. Why murder is more than just harm: To murder someone is not just to harm a biological organism — it is to destroy something that bears God's own image. It is an attack on what God has chosen to reflect himself through. Genesis 9:6 makes this explicit: "Whoever sheds human blood, by humans shall their blood be shed; for in the image of God has God made mankind." The reason given is not social (it disrupts society) or practical (it hurts someone) — it is theological: the victim bore God's image. This gives every human being an inherent dignity that cannot be taken away by any earthly authority, and it makes the protection of human life a fundamentally religious obligation, not merely a social one.
5
Commandment 9 says "do not give false testimony against your neighbour." This was originally about lying in court. But how does the spirit of this commandment apply to everyday life today? Give THREE modern examples of situations where this commandment's principle applies. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Original meaning: In ancient Israel, courts relied entirely on witnesses. False testimony could send an innocent person to prison or death. The commandment protected the legal system and people's reputations from being destroyed by lies. Modern example 1 — Social media: Spreading false rumours about someone online — sharing a screenshot out of context, posting lies about someone's character — is a form of bearing false witness. It can destroy someone's reputation just as effectively as a courtroom lie. Modern example 2 — Workplace or school: Falsely blaming a colleague or classmate for a mistake you made, or reporting something they did not do, is bearing false witness in a different setting. Modern example 3 — Gossip and exaggeration: Telling stories about someone that are partly true but exaggerated or misleading — making them look worse than they are — is a softer but real form of false testimony. What this shows about truth: God values truth because trust in community depends on it. Lies destroy relationships, harm reputations, and make cooperation impossible. Truth is not just a rule — it is the foundation on which every community rests.
6
Jesus deepens several commandments in Matthew 5 (the Sermon on the Mount). He says: "You have heard that it was said, 'Do not murder'... But I tell you that anyone who is angry with a brother or sister will be subject to judgment." What principle is Jesus demonstrating about the relationship between inner life and outward behaviour? How does this connect to Commandment 10 (do not covet)? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Jesus is demonstrating that the commandments have always been about the heart, not just the hands. Every outward sinful act begins as an inward one: murder begins as unresolved anger; adultery begins as lust; stealing begins as coveting. When Jesus says "you have heard... but I tell you," he is not replacing the commandments — he is explaining what they always meant at the deepest level. Connection to Commandment 10: Commandment 10 already pointed to this principle — it is the only commandment explicitly about a desire (coveting), not an action. The Tenth Commandment is prophetic of what Jesus teaches in the Sermon on the Mount: God always cared about the inner life, not just outward conformity. The commandments work from outside in AND from inside out — they call people to outward behaviour (do not steal) and inner character (do not covet). What this means: God's law is not a checklist of actions — it is a description of a transformed heart. This is why Paul writes (Romans 2:15) that the law is "written on the heart" — the goal is not to follow rules but to become the kind of person who naturally lives by their principles.

Two expressions are equal: 5x+3 = 3x+11. Find the value of x by working backwards. Then verify your answer. Explain each step clearly. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
5x+3 = 3x+11. Step 1: subtract 3x from both sides: 5x−3x+3 = 11 → 2x+3 = 11. Step 2: subtract 3 from both sides: 2x = 8. Step 3: divide both sides by 2: x = 4. Verification: Left side: 5(4)+3 = 23. Right side: 3(4)+11 = 23. ✓ Both sides equal 23, so x=4 is correct.

Day 2Deeper AnalysisKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Distillation and chromatography recall. Focus on process and purpose.
📚 Distillation & Chromatography Recall
💡 ESL Tip: Condenser is the key piece of equipment in distillation. It is a glass tube surrounded by cold water. When steam (hot vapour) travels through the condenser, it cools down and turns back into a liquid. This is called condensation — the reverse of evaporation. Cold → liquid. Hot → gas. The condenser converts gas BACK to liquid so we can collect it.
1
Describe the complete process of simple distillation step by step. Name the key pieces of equipment used at each stage and explain what happens at each step. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Equipment: round-bottom flask, heat source (Bunsen burner or hotplate), thermometer, condenser (Liebig condenser), cold water supply, collection beaker/flask. Step 1: Pour the salt water (or mixture) into the round-bottom flask. Apply heat — the water is heated to its boiling point (100°C for pure water). Step 2: The water turns to steam (water vapour) and rises up the tube. The salt cannot vaporise at this temperature — it stays dissolved in the remaining liquid in the flask. Step 3: The steam travels into the condenser — a glass tube surrounded by cold water. The cold water jacket cools the steam, causing it to condense back into liquid (pure water). Step 4: The condensed pure water drips out of the end of the condenser into a collection beaker. Remaining in flask: The salt (and any remaining concentrated solution) stays in the flask — it could not evaporate at 100°C.
2
Explain how paper chromatography separates a mixture of dyes. In your answer, explain: what Rf value is, why different dyes travel different distances, and what you can read from the result. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
How it works: A spot of the ink or dye mixture is placed at the bottom of chromatography paper (above the solvent line). The paper is placed in a small amount of solvent (water or alcohol). The solvent travels up the paper by capillary action, carrying the dissolved substances with it. Why different distances: Each substance has different attractions to the paper (stationary phase) and the solvent (mobile phase). A substance that "prefers" the solvent is carried further up; one that "prefers" sticking to the paper moves less. This means each substance in the mixture travels a characteristic distance. Rf value: Rf (Retention Factor) = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent. Rf values are always between 0 and 1. Each pure substance has a known, fixed Rf value in a given solvent — so you can identify an unknown substance by comparing its Rf to known values. Reading the chromatogram: The number of spots shows how many substances were in the mixture. The position of each spot (its Rf) allows identification. If two substances have the same Rf, they may be the same compound.

🔍 Application & Evaluation
3
A forensic scientist is analysing ink from three different pens (A, B, C) found at a crime scene. She runs a chromatography test and gets these results:

• Solvent travelled 8 cm
• Pen A: one spot at 6 cm
• Pen B: two spots at 3 cm and 6 cm
• Pen C: two spots at 3 cm and 7.2 cm

(a) Calculate the Rf value for each spot. (b) Which pen or pens share a component dye? (c) The crime scene note was written with an ink that has components at Rf = 0.375 and Rf = 0.9. Which pen was most likely used? Explain your reasoning. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Pen A: 6÷8 = 0.75. Pen B: 3÷8 = 0.375; 6÷8 = 0.75. Pen C: 3÷8 = 0.375; 7.2÷8 = 0.9. (b) Pens B and C both have a component with Rf = 0.375 (the spot at 3 cm). They share this dye. Pens A and B share a component with Rf = 0.75 (the spot at 6 cm). (c) The crime scene note has components at Rf = 0.375 and Rf = 0.9. Pen C has spots at exactly Rf = 0.375 and Rf = 0.9. Therefore Pen C is most likely the pen used to write the crime note. Pen B has the 0.375 component but its second component is 0.75, not 0.9 — so it is not a match.
4
A student wants to obtain pure water from sea water. She has access to a Bunsen burner, a beaker, an evaporating dish, a thermometer, some glass tubing, and ice. She does NOT have a proper condenser.

(a) Which technique should she use — evaporation or distillation? (b) How could she set up a basic distillation without a proper condenser, using only the equipment listed? (c) What would be wrong with using evaporation instead? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) She should use distillation — because she wants to collect pure WATER, not the salt. Evaporation would let the water escape and leave the salt behind, giving her nothing to drink. (b) Improvised distillation: Heat the sea water in a beaker. Place a piece of glass tubing at an angle so steam rises through it. The ice acts as an improvised condenser. (c) Problem with evaporation: Evaporation lets the water vapour escape into the air — it is NOT collected. You would be left with dry salt crystals and no water at all. If her goal is to collect pure drinking water, evaporation gives her the wrong product. Distillation specifically captures and recondenses the water vapour.

🏆 Real-World Science
5
Water purification is one of humanity's most important industrial processes. Drinking water is separated from contaminated or salty water using various techniques.

(a) Explain how distillation could in theory provide unlimited clean water from sea water.
(b) Describe TWO practical limitations of using distillation on an industrial scale for producing drinking water.
(c) A different technique — reverse osmosis — forces sea water through a very fine membrane to remove salt particles. Which separation technique studied this week is most SIMILAR in principle to reverse osmosis? Explain the similarity.
(d) Why does access to clean water matter geographically? Connect to this week's History/Geography lesson. 11 marks
🔗 Cross-subject link: History/Geography — settlement factors: water supply as the primary reason people settle where they do.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) The ocean contains virtually unlimited salt water. Distillation could heat sea water, collect the steam (pure water vapour), and condense it back into pure drinking water. The salt and minerals remain behind. In principle, any amount of sea water could be purified this way, providing limitless fresh water in coastal areas. (b) Limitation 1 — Energy cost: Heating large quantities of sea water to boiling point requires enormous amounts of energy. On an industrial scale, this is extremely expensive and, if fossil fuels are used, environmentally damaging. Limitation 2 — Scale and speed: Distillation is slow — you can only produce as much fresh water as you can boil and condense at any one time. Meeting the water needs of a large city this way would require an enormous number of distillation units. (c) Reverse osmosis is most similar to filtration. In filtration, a filter medium (paper) has holes small enough to let water through but too small for solid particles. In reverse osmosis, a very fine membrane has holes too small for salt ions to pass through but large enough for water molecules. Both techniques use the principle of physical separation by particle/ion size — smaller particles pass through; larger ones are blocked. (d) Geographical connection: This week's lesson showed that water supply is the number one factor in where people have settled throughout history — all ancient civilisations began near rivers (Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus). Today, regions with reliable fresh water support the highest population densities on Earth. Countries in the Middle East and North Africa face water scarcity despite their oil wealth — and countries like Saudi Arabia use desalination (industrial-scale distillation and reverse osmosis) to supplement limited natural water. The lesson from history is unchanged: where water goes, settlement follows.

💡 ESL Tip: Urbanisation has two parts: "urban" (city) + "-isation" (the process of becoming). Urbanisation = the process of becoming more city-based. Give FOUR specific reasons why urbanisation has happened so rapidly since the Industrial Revolution (roughly 1760 onwards). For each reason, explain the mechanism — not just what happened, but WHY it led to urban growth. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Factory jobs (pull factor): Factories in cities needed large numbers of workers. Machines replaced farm labourers in rural areas (push factor), while urban factory wages were higher than farm wages — so people moved for economic survival and improvement. 2. Railways and transport: Trains allowed people to move to cities quickly and cheaply, and allowed food to be transported into cities to feed large urban populations that could not grow their own food. 3. Better services in cities: As cities grew, hospitals, schools, and shops concentrated there — creating more pull factors for families wanting access to healthcare and education. 4. Rural to urban migration cycle: Once some people moved to cities, they wrote home and encouraged family and friends to follow. Chain migration (following family) became a powerful pull factor in itself. Also: farm mechanisation continued to reduce the number of rural workers needed, pushing more people off the land.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Positive — improves health outcomes, education levels, and quality of life. People in rural areas may travel hours to see a doctor; in a city, healthcare is nearby. b) Negative — slums lack clean water, sanitation, and safe housing. Millions of urban migrants worldwide live in informal settlements (shanty towns, favelas) with poor living conditions. c) Positive — cities create diverse economies less dependent on weather or a single crop. Many great inventions and cultural movements began in cities. f) Negative — urban sprawl reduces the land available for food production, creating tensions between housing needs and food security. This is a growing issue in countries with rapidly expanding cities like Pakistan, India, and Nigeria.

The lesson says urbanisation is a GLOBAL trend, but it is happening at different speeds in different parts of the world. High-income countries (like the UK, USA, Japan) urbanised mostly between 1800-1950. Lower-income countries (like Pakistan, Nigeria, Bangladesh) are urbanising rapidly NOW.

(a) Why might urbanisation be happening faster in lower-income countries today than it did in the UK in the 1800s?
(b) What challenges might a rapidly urbanising lower-income country face that a wealthy country did not face in the same way?
(c) Connect to science: how does access to clean water (separation techniques) become even more important in rapidly growing cities? 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Several factors accelerate urbanisation in lower-income countries today compared with Victorian Britain: communication technology (mobile phones and social media allow people in rural areas to know immediately about job opportunities in cities); global markets (global competition has made small-scale farming less viable, pushing people off rural land faster); population growth (higher birth rates in rural areas create more people than the land can support, generating stronger push factors). The speed of transport has also increased. (b) Challenges: Lower-income countries urbanising rapidly often lack the pre-existing infrastructure (piped water, sewage systems, electricity grids, roads) that wealthy countries built slowly over 150 years. Cities like Karachi or Lagos grow faster than governments can build housing, schools, or water systems — leading to large informal settlements. Governments also have fewer financial resources to invest in infrastructure at the speed needed. Environmental laws may be weaker, leading to worse pollution. (c) Clean water connection: As cities grow rapidly, groundwater sources and rivers can become contaminated by sewage, industrial waste, and agricultural chemicals. The techniques of filtration and distillation are the fundamental scientific processes behind water treatment plants — filtration removes suspended particles and some bacteria; further treatment (using chemicals and sometimes distillation) removes dissolved contaminants. A city of 10 million people needs industrial-scale water purification running constantly. Without the science of separation, urbanisation would quickly become a public health crisis — contaminated water causes cholera, typhoid, and other diseases that historically killed millions in rapidly growing industrial cities.
