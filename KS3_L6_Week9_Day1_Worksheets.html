📚 Vocabulary & Memory Verse
💡 ESL Tip: The phrase "be strong and courageous" appears FOUR times in Joshua chapter 1 — once from God, and the rest from the people and from God again. Repetition in the Bible is a signal: when something is said again and again, it means it matters deeply. Joshua needed real encouragement for a real responsibility.
1
Write the memory verse from Joshua 1:9 in full. Then explain in your own words what it means to be "strong and courageous" even when you still feel afraid. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"Have I not commanded you? Be strong and courageous. Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged, for the LORD your God will be with you wherever you go." (Joshua 1:9, NIV). Being strong and courageous despite fear does not mean you feel no fear at all — it means fear does not get to make your decisions for you. Courage is not the absence of fear; it is acting rightly even while feeling afraid, because you trust something (or someone) bigger than the fear itself. For Joshua, that "something bigger" was the promise of God's presence — "wherever you go."
2
Match each vocabulary word to its correct definition: successor · covenant · ark of the covenant · siege · consecrate 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
successor = the person who takes over a role or position after someone else — Joshua was Moses' successor. covenant = a serious, binding agreement between God and his people — the Promised Land was part of God's covenant with Abraham. ark of the covenant = a sacred wooden chest representing God's presence with Israel, carried by priests. siege = a military operation surrounding a city to force its surrender — Jericho's "siege" was won unusually, through marching and trumpets, not weapons. consecrate = to set apart as holy or dedicated to God — Israel consecrated themselves before crossing the Jordan.

List the four main events/stages of this week's story in order: Joshua becomes leader, crossing the Jordan, the fall of Jericho, the twelve stones memorial. For each, write one sentence describing what happened. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Joshua becomes leader: After Moses dies, God commissions Joshua, repeatedly commanding him to be strong and courageous because God's presence will go with him. 2. Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Understanding and explanation. Think carefully before writing.
🔍 Understanding & Explanation
4
The crossing of the Jordan River (Joshua 3) closely echoes the crossing of the Red Sea under Moses (Exodus 14). Compare the two events: what is similar, and why might God have chosen to repeat a similar miracle for the new leader Joshua? 5 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 7 Bible — Moses and the Exodus, the parting of the Red Sea.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Similarities: In both events, a body of water that should have blocked Israel's path stops flowing/parts, allowing the entire nation to cross on dry ground. In both cases, the miracle happens at a moment of transition — leaving Egypt (Red Sea) and entering Canaan (Jordan). Both miracles are connected to a leader carrying out God's instructions exactly. Why God repeated it: The repetition powerfully confirms that the SAME God who worked through Moses is now working through Joshua — Israel (and Joshua himself) needed reassurance that God's power had not left with Moses' death. It also publicly established Joshua's authority: Joshua 3:7 says God did this "so that they may know that I am with you as I was with Moses." The miracle was as much about confirming Joshua's leadership as it was about the practical crossing.
5
God's instructions for capturing Jericho were unusual: marching, trumpets, and shouting — no weapons or siege engines. Explain what this method teaches about the SOURCE of Israel's victory. Why might God have chosen such an unusual method instead of a normal military attack? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The unusual method makes it impossible for Israel to credit the victory to their own military skill or strength. If Jericho had fallen through a clever siege strategy or superior weapons, Israel could later claim some of the glory for themselves. Instead, the walls collapsed through an act that had no natural military explanation — marching in a circle and shouting cannot break stone walls. This teaches that the land was a GIFT from God, not Israel's achievement, which is a central theme of the whole book of Joshua. By requiring strange obedience rather than military cleverness, God ensured that everyone — Israelites and Canaanites alike — would recognise the victory as coming from God himself, building Israel's trust in God's power and Joshua's authority as God's chosen leader.
6
The lesson compares Abraham, Joseph, Moses, and Joshua as four examples of faith. What is the KEY DIFFERENCE between what faith required of Joshua compared to what it required of Abraham? 3 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 5 Bible — Abraham called to leave his homeland for an unknown destination.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Abraham's faith required leaving everything familiar and travelling toward a destination he did not yet know — trusting a promise about a FUTURE he could not picture. Joshua's faith was different: the promise had ALREADY been clearly defined (the specific land of Canaan) and Israel was already standing at its border. Joshua's faith was about actively taking hold of a promise that had been spoken centuries earlier and was now within reach — requiring courage to act and obey rather than the uncertainty of an unknown destination. Abraham's faith was about trusting an unclear future; Joshua's faith was about courageously stepping into a promise that was clear but required action to receive.
Section C
🔴 HardSection C — Extended analytical thinking. Write in full sentences.
🏆 Analysis & Application
7
The twelve stones (Joshua 4) were set up specifically so that future generations would ASK about them and be told the story. Why might God have cared so much about future generations remembering this event, rather than just the people who experienced it directly? Write at least 5 sentences. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Faith is rarely sustained by a single generation's direct experience alone — it must be passed down, retold, and re-anchored in physical reminders, or it fades. By instructing Israel to build a memorial specifically designed to provoke a question ("what do these stones mean?"), God ensured that the story of his faithfulness would not simply be forgotten once those who witnessed it had died. Children growing up generations later, who never personally saw the Jordan stop flowing, would still encounter the same truth through their parents' retelling — making the original event present and real for them too. This reflects a recurring biblical pattern: festivals, memorials, and rituals (Passover, the Sabbath, this stone monument) are designed to keep faith communal and intergenerational rather than purely individual and momentary. The stones did not just commemorate a past event; they functioned as an ongoing invitation for every future generation to ask, remember, and trust the same God who acted at the Jordan.
8
Evaluate: "Joshua's story shows that courage and obedience are not separate things — they are the same response in two different situations." Do you agree? Use specific evidence from the lesson (the Jordan crossing AND Jericho) to support your view. Write at least 6 sentences. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
This statement is well supported by the evidence in Joshua's story. At the Jordan, courage was needed to step into a flooded river BEFORE it stopped flowing — the priests had to walk forward in faith, trusting the water would part as they obeyed God's instruction to carry the ark in. This is courage expressed as obedience: doing what God said even before seeing the result. At Jericho, the same pattern repeats — there was no logical reason to believe that marching silently around a city for six days, then shouting on the seventh, would bring down stone walls. Yet Israel obeyed precisely, without modification or improvisation. In both cases, the "courage" required was not a feeling of bravery in the face of danger in the usual sense (like charging into battle) — it was the willingness to follow an unusual, even illogical-seeming instruction without alternative justification. This supports the claim: in Joshua's story, courage is not a separate quality from obedience but is revealed precisely THROUGH acts of obedience that required trusting God more than visible evidence. The two cannot really be separated — courage was the inner posture that made the outward obedience possible, and the obedience was the only way the courage became visible and tested.

📚 Vocabulary & Structure
💡 ESL Tip: When you give a presentation, your audience can only hear each sentence ONCE. They cannot scroll back like with a written text. This is why clear "signposting" language ("first," "next," "finally") is even more important when speaking than when writing.
1
Define each term in your own words: audience · active listening · pace · filler words · eye contact 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
audience: the people listening to a presentation or speech. active listening: fully concentrating on and engaging with a speaker, not just passively hearing sounds. pace: the speed at which someone speaks — too fast is hard to follow, too slow loses interest. filler words: unnecessary sounds used when thinking, such as "um" or "like," which can make a speaker sound unprepared if overused. eye contact: looking at members of the audience while speaking, rather than only at notes — builds connection and confidence.
2
List the THREE parts of a well-structured oral presentation and describe what goes in each. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Opening: A hook (question, fact, or short story) to grab attention, followed by a clear statement of purpose telling the audience what the presentation will cover. Main points: Usually 2-4 clear points, each signposted with phrases like "my first point is..." or "next..." so the audience can follow the structure without seeing it written down. Closing: A brief summary of the main points, followed by a strong final sentence — a call to action, memorable statement, or invitation for questions.
3
For each delivery skill, identify ONE common mistake speakers make and explain how to fix it: volume · pace · eye contact · body language · filler words 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
volume: mistake = speaking too quietly from nerves; fix = practise projecting your voice so the back of the room can hear comfortably. pace: mistake = rushing through the whole presentation; fix = practise pausing briefly between points and slowing down for important information. eye contact: mistake = reading word-for-word from notes without looking up; fix = use brief notes/keywords only and practise looking at different parts of the audience. body language: mistake = crossing arms or fidgeting; fix = stand with open posture and use natural hand gestures to support points. filler words: mistake = overusing "um" or "like"; fix = pause silently instead of filling the gap with sound while thinking.

📚 Vocabulary & Identification
💡 ESL Tip: Remember from Week 8: a MIXTURE keeps the original properties of its parts. This week's key idea is different: a COMPOUND has completely NEW properties — different from the elements that made it. Sodium + chlorine (both dangerous alone) = table salt (safe to eat)!

Define each term and give one example: element · atom · compound · chemical symbol 4 marks

element: a pure substance made of only one type of atom. Example: oxygen, iron, gold. atom: the smallest particle of an element that still has that element's properties. Example: a single carbon atom. compound: two or more elements chemically joined together in a fixed ratio. Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Properties and comparing metals and non-metals.
🔍 Properties & Comparison
4
Explain the difference between a compound and a mixture, using sodium chloride (table salt) as your example. Why does the lesson say a compound has "completely different" properties from the elements that formed it? 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A compound forms when two or more elements undergo a CHEMICAL REACTION and join together in a fixed ratio, creating a new substance with new properties. A mixture, by contrast, simply combines substances physically without a chemical reaction — each substance KEEPS its own original properties. Sodium chloride example: Sodium (Na) on its own is a soft, highly reactive metal that explodes violently in water. Chlorine (Cl) on its own is a toxic, poisonous green gas. Yet when they chemically combine (forming an ionic bond) to create sodium chloride (NaCl) — ordinary table salt — the result is a harmless white crystal that we eat every day. Why properties are so different: When atoms chemically bond, their electrons rearrange and interact in completely new ways, creating a substance with entirely different physical and chemical properties from its original elements. This is fundamentally different from a mixture, where no new bonds form and the original substances remain chemically unchanged and separable by physical means.
5

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
METALS: 1. Shiny/lustrous — gold maintains a bright shine when polished. 2. Good conductor of electricity — copper is used in electrical wiring. 3. Good conductor of heat — aluminium pans conduct heat efficiently for cooking. 4. Malleable (can be bent/hammered) — gold can be hammered into thin sheets. 5. Ductile (can be drawn into wire) — copper is drawn into electrical wire. NON-METALS: 1. Dull appearance — sulfur has a dull yellow appearance. 2. Section C
🔴 HardSection C — Extended scientific reasoning about the periodic table.
🏆 Periodic Table Reasoning
6
The periodic table organises elements into GROUPS (columns) and PERIODS (rows).

(a) Explain what determines which group an element belongs to.
(b) Explain what determines which period an element belongs to.
(c) Group 1 elements (lithium, sodium, potassium) are called "alkali metals" and are all highly reactive, especially with water. Group 0/8 elements (helium, neon, argon) are called "noble gases" and are almost completely unreactive. Using the idea that elements in the same group share similar properties, explain why scientists find it useful to organise elements this way — what can a scientist predict about an UNKNOWN element if they know its group? 9 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) An element's group (vertical column) is determined by the number of electrons in its outermost shell (its "valence electrons"). Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, which is the main reason they share similar chemical properties and tend to react in similar ways. (b) An element's period (horizontal row) is determined by the number of electron shells (energy levels) its atoms have. Moving across a period, elements have the same number of shells but an increasing number of electrons in the outer shell. (c) Organising elements by group is extremely useful for SCIENTIFIC PREDICTION. If a scientist discovers a new element and determines it belongs to Group 1 (the alkali metals), they can confidently predict — even before extensive testing — that it will be a soft, highly reactive metal that reacts violently with water, similar to lithium, sodium, and potassium. This predictive power was historically how the periodic table proved its scientific value: Dmitri Mendeleev, who first organised the modern periodic table, successfully predicted the existence and properties of several elements that had not yet been discovered, based purely on the gaps and patterns in his table. This shows that the periodic table is not just a storage system for facts — it reveals an underlying pattern in how atomic structure determines chemical behaviour.

📚 Vocabulary & Feudal Pyramid
💡 ESL Tip: A "vassal" is anyone who receives land in exchange for loyalty — but the SAME person can be both a vassal (to the person above them) AND a lord (to the people below them). A knight is a vassal to his lord, but the knight himself might be a "lord" over the serfs working his own small piece of land.
1
Define each term and give one example or detail: feudalism · monarch · lord/noble · vassal · knight · serf · manor · clergy 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
feudalism: a system of medieval Europe in which land was exchanged for loyalty and service, organising society into a hierarchy. monarch: a king or queen — the supreme ruler of a kingdom. lord/noble: a person of high rank who controlled land granted by the monarch, in exchange for military service and loyalty. vassal: a person who received land from a lord in exchange for a pledge of loyalty and service. knight: a warrior who served a lord on horseback in armour, following a code of honour called chivalry. serf: a peasant at the bottom of the feudal system, legally bound to a lord's land. manor: a large estate of land controlled by a lord, including the lord's house, farmland, and the village. clergy: officials of the Church — priests, bishops, monks — who held significant power in medieval society.
2
Draw or describe the four levels of the feudal pyramid in order, from top to bottom. For each level, state what they received and what they gave in return. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Level 1 — Monarch: Owned all land in theory. Received loyalty from lords. Gave land grants in exchange. Level 2 — Lords/Nobles: Received large estates of land from the monarch. Gave loyalty and military support to the king. Level 3 — Knights: Received land from lords in exchange for military service. Gave a set number of days of fighting service each year (often 40 days). Level 4 — Serfs/Peasants: Received protection and the right to farm a small plot for their own family. Gave labour — farming the lord's land for free several days a week and a portion of their own crops.
Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Life in medieval society and the role of the Church.
🔍 Daily Life & the Church
3
Describe a typical day in the life of a serf, including their work obligations, legal restrictions, and what they received in return for their labour. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Work obligations: A serf farmed strips of land assigned to them by the lord, but had to give a portion of their own crops to the lord. They were also required to work several days each week on the lord's own fields completely without pay — this was called "labour service." Legal restrictions: Serfs could not leave the manor without the lord's permission, could not marry without permission, and were essentially legally tied to the land — passed down through generations along with the land itself, almost like property. What they received in return: Protection from raids, bandits, and invasions by the lord's knights and soldiers, plus the right to farm a small plot of land for their own family's food. This was the basic "exchange" at the heart of feudalism — labour and loyalty for protection and land use.
4
The medieval Church held both religious AND political/economic power. Give TWO examples of each type of power, and explain why this combination made the Church one of the most influential institutions of the period. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Religious power examples: 1. The Church conducted baptisms, weddings, and funerals — central ceremonies at every stage of a person's life. 2. The Church taught that obedience to God and the Church secured salvation and avoided eternal punishment, giving it enormous spiritual authority over how people lived. Political/economic power examples: 1. The Church owned vast amounts of land across Europe — in many regions, more land than any single king. 2. The Church collected tithes (a tenth of people's income) from the entire population, making it extremely wealthy. Why this combination mattered: Because the Church combined spiritual authority (control over salvation and morality) with massive material wealth and land ownership, it could exert influence that rivalled or even exceeded that of kings. The Church could crown monarchs (giving their rule religious legitimacy) or excommunicate them (a powerful political weapon, since it could turn subjects against an excommunicated king). This unique combination of spiritual and material power made the medieval Church a genuinely independent political force, not merely a religious institution.

🔴 HardSection C — Extended analytical thinking and cross-week connections.
🏆 Analysis & Extended History
5
The lesson notes that feudalism developed "in the absence of strong central government" after the fall of Rome. Connect this to Week 8's Geography lesson: explain why, without a strong central government providing protection, LOCAL physical geography (hills, rivers, castles) became even more important for safety and survival in medieval Europe than it might have been under a strong, unified empire like Rome. Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 8 Geography — settlement factors, especially natural defence. Week 4 History — the Roman Empire and its fall.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
When the Roman Empire was strong and unified (Week 4), a single central authority maintained roads, legions, and law across vast distances — meaning individual towns did not need to defend themselves entirely on their own, since Roman military power and infrastructure provided broad protection. After Rome's collapse, this centralised protection disappeared, and local communities were left far more exposed to raids, invasions, and conflict between rival lords. In this environment, the physical geography of a location became dramatically more important for survival, exactly as the settlement factors studied in Week 8 (natural defence, water supply, fertile land) predict. A hilltop location, a river bend, or a defensible position became not just convenient but essential, because there was no longer a larger power providing security from a distance. This explains why medieval castles were deliberately built in highly defensible natural locations — on hills, surrounded by rivers, or along coastlines — investing enormous resources in fortification that simply was not necessary to the same degree under the unified protection of the Roman state. The shift from empire to feudalism therefore represents geography becoming more locally decisive: when political unity fragments, physical terrain reasserts itself as the primary determinant of safety, just as it had for the very earliest human settlements before large empires existed at all. In this sense, feudal Europe in some ways returned to settlement patterns more similar to ancient pre-imperial societies, where local defensible geography mattered enormously, rather than the geography-overriding infrastructure of a unified empire like Rome.
