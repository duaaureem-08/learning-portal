A student says: "In inverse proportion, I just divide instead of multiply — that's the only difference from direct proportion." Identify the mistake in this thinking. Give a worked example showing what happens if you incorrectly use the direct proportion method on an inverse proportion problem — and then show the correct method and answer. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The student's error: inverse proportion is NOT just "divide instead of multiply." The fundamental difference is that in direct proportion you find ONE unit and multiply; in inverse proportion you must find the TOTAL (the invariant product) first, then divide. Applying "divide instead of multiply" would mean: 1 painter takes 12÷3=4 days, so 9 painters take 4÷9=0.44 days — which is nonsensical. Wrong method (direct applied to inverse): 1 painter = 12÷3 = 4 days. 9 painters = 4×9 = 36 days. This says MORE painters take MORE days — which contradicts logic. Correct method (inverse): Total painter-days = 3×12 = 36. With 9 painters: 36÷9 = 4 days. The key principle: in inverse proportion the PRODUCT of the two quantities is always constant (3×12 = 9×4 = 36). You find that constant first, then divide. The two methods give completely different answers (36 days vs 4 days) because they assume completely different relationships between the variables.

💡 Key principle to remember: All state changes going from solid → liquid → gas ABSORB heat. All state changes going from gas → liquid → solid RELEASE heat. Going from more ordered to less ordered = absorb. Going from less ordered to more ordered = release.
1
Answer these short questions from memory. 7 marks

a) Melting point of water = 0°C. Boiling point = 100°C (at sea level). b) A gas can be compressed because its particles are far apart with lots of empty space between them — so they can be pushed closer together. A solid cannot be compressed because its particles are already touching and tightly packed — there is no space for them to move into. c) The reverse of condensation is evaporation (or boiling). Condensation: gas → liquid. Reverse: liquid → gas. d) During a flat section, the substance is changing state. All the energy being added is going into breaking the bonds between particles (to allow them to move more freely) rather than raising the temperature. The temperature stays constant until all particles have completed the state change. e) Latent heat (latent heat of fusion at the melting point; latent heat of vaporisation at the boiling point). f) Everyday examples of sublimation: dry ice (solid CO₂) going directly from solid to gas; moth balls (naphthalene) slowly disappearing; some air fresheners dissolving without a liquid stage. g) False. When ice melts, the H₂O molecules are exactly the same — only their arrangement and energy level change. Melting is a physical change, not a chemical one. 🔍 Applying the Particle Model
3
Use the particle model to explain each everyday observation. You must refer to particles, their movement, and/or their spacing in every answer. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Perfume particles are in the gas state and move very quickly in all directions, bouncing off each other and air particles. This random, high-speed movement (called diffusion) gradually spreads perfume particles throughout the room until they are evenly distributed. b) The metal lid is solid — its particles are tightly packed. Hot water transfers energy to the lid particles, making them vibrate faster and move slightly further apart — the lid expands (becomes very slightly larger). This breaks the seal and makes it easier to open. c) Evaporation happens when water particles at the surface gain enough energy to escape as gas. On a hot day, more particles have enough energy to escape (faster evaporation). Wind removes the escaped particles from the surface, preventing them from re-condensing into the fabric — so drying is faster. d) The steam is water vapour (gas). When it touches the cooler mirror surface, the gas particles lose energy, slow down, and come close enough together to become liquid water — condensation. Your finger removes this thin water layer, leaving a clear line. e) Air is a gas — particles are far apart with lots of empty space. Squeezing forces the particles closer together into that space. However, you cannot compress air to zero because the particles still repel each other at very short distances. f) Air (gas): compressible because particles are far apart. Water (liquid): particles are already very close together — there is almost no empty space between them. You cannot push them closer, so water (like all liquids and solids) cannot be meaningfully compressed.

Section C: Temperature rises again — liquid heating up. Particles slide past each other, moving faster and faster. Section D: Temperature stays flat at 115°C — boiling is occurring. Energy goes into overcoming all remaining forces between liquid particles so they can escape as gas. Section E: Temperature rises — gas heating up. Particles move very fast in all directions. (c) At 25°C, the substance is a solid — because 25°C is below its melting point (42°C). The particles are still locked in a fixed arrangement, vibrating on the spot but unable to flow. A real-world substance with similar properties might be wax or certain fats, which are solid at room temperature but melt easily when warmed.

Equipment: thermometer, test tube or beaker, Bunsen burner or water bath, stopwatch, graph paper (or data logger). Measurements: heat the substance at a steady rate; record the temperature every 30 seconds (or every minute). Plot temperature on the y-axis and time on the x-axis. The graph would show: rising line (solid heating up), flat line (melting — this is the melting point), rising line (liquid heating up), flat line (boiling — this is the boiling point), rising line (gas heating up). The two flat sections are the most important features: the temperature at which the first flat section occurs is the melting point, and the temperature at which the second flat section occurs is the boiling point. The flat sections occur because during a state change, all energy input goes into breaking bonds between particles rather than raising temperature — so the temperature stays constant until the state change is complete. Using this method on a known substance (e.g. water) as a control test lets you verify your equipment is accurate before testing the unknown substance.

The particle model says particles in a solid are "locked in fixed positions." But if that is true, explain how it is possible to: (a) bend a piece of copper wire, (b) dissolve a solid (like salt) in water, (c) slowly deform a piece of soft lead with your hands. What does each of these observations tell us about the limitations of the simple particle model? Write at least 5 sentences total. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Bending copper wire: "Locked in fixed positions" does not mean particles cannot move at all — it means they vibrate around fixed average positions. In metals, the particles are arranged in layers that can slide over each other when a force is applied, allowing the shape to change without the solid crumbling. This is called plastic deformation, and the simple model does not explain it well because it treats all solids identically. (b) Salt dissolving: the water molecules have electrical polarity (positive and negative ends), and they attract the positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions in the salt crystal, pulling them away from the fixed positions and surrounding them individually in water. This is an electrostatic interaction that the simple sphere model completely ignores — it cannot explain why some solids dissolve and others don't. (c) Lead deforming: lead is a very soft metal — its layers of particles slide very easily over each other under gentle pressure. Again, the model does not distinguish between soft and hard solids. These observations reveal a key limitation: the simple particle model treats all particles as identical rigid spheres with no electrical charge or shape. In reality, particles have complex electrical properties, shapes, and inter-particle forces that determine behaviour. The model works for explaining states and temperature changes, but fails as soon as we ask WHY different substances within the same state behave so differently.

A student claims: "If you cool something down enough, everything becomes a solid eventually." Is this claim correct? Discuss it using specific examples — including at least one substance that challenges the claim — and explain what this tells us about the range of melting and boiling points in nature. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The claim is largely correct but oversimplified. Most substances do become solid if cooled enough — for example, nitrogen (a gas at room temperature) becomes solid at −210°C, and even helium solidifies at −272°C under sufficient pressure. Evidence supporting the claim: every substance has a melting point, so below that temperature it should be solid. A substance that challenges the claim: helium — at normal atmospheric pressure, helium never becomes solid no matter how cold it gets (even near absolute zero, −273°C). You need to apply very high pressure AND cool it to make it solidify. This is because helium's inter-atomic forces are so weak that quantum effects prevent it from solidifying at atmospheric pressure. This tells us that melting and boiling points vary enormously across nature — from iron (melts at 1,538°C) to helium (stays liquid to −269°C at normal pressure) — because they depend on the strength of the forces between particles, which vary enormously from substance to substance. The simple model does not explain why these forces differ.

3
Explain the rain shadow effect — one of the five climate factors from the lesson. (a) Describe what happens step-by-step as moist air reaches a mountain range. (b) Give a real-world example of where a rain shadow creates contrasting conditions on two sides of a mountain range. (c) Explain why the rain shadow effect means that latitude alone cannot predict a place's climate. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Step-by-step: moist air blowing from the sea reaches a mountain range and is forced to rise. As it rises, it cools. Cool air cannot hold as much water vapour as warm air, so the water condenses and falls as rain on the windward (sea-facing) side of the mountain. By the time the air crosses over the mountain peak, it has lost most of its moisture. It then descends on the leeward (inland) side, warming as it descends, and arrives as dry air. The leeward side receives very little rainfall — this is the rain shadow. (b) Real-world example: the Andes Mountains in South America — the Pacific-facing western side of the Andes (Chile) has lush vegetation and high rainfall, while the eastern leeward side of the mountains includes some of the driest land in the world (the Atacama Desert, one of the driest places on Earth). Another example: the Cascades range in the USA, with wet forests on the Oregon coast and semi-desert in eastern Oregon. (c) Two places can be at exactly the same latitude but have completely different climates if one is on the windward side of a mountain range and the other is in the rain shadow. Latitude tells you about the angle of the sun — important for temperature. But precipitation (and therefore vegetation and biome) depends on many factors beyond latitude: mountain ranges, distance from the sea, and prevailing winds can create deserts or forests at any latitude.

Write at least 4 sentences. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Sample — Biome: Tropical Rainforest. (a) Tropical rainforests exist on three continents: South America (the Amazon Basin in Brazil, Peru, Colombia), Africa (the Congo Basin in central and west Africa), and Asia (Borneo, Sumatra, the Philippines, and parts of India). (b) All of these regions share the same key climate conditions: they lie near the equator (low latitude, high solar energy), receive over 2,000mm of rainfall per year, and have high temperatures year-round (25–30°C). The climate is what determines the biome — these regions all share the same climate pattern regardless of which continent they're on, so the same type of biome develops. (c) The plants and animals are similar but not identical. The Amazon has jaguars; the Congo has leopards. The Amazon has capybaras; Asia has orangutans. Each continent's rainforest evolved independently — the climate conditions are the same, so the ecological niches are similar (large predator, frugivore, insect-eater, etc.), and the species that fill each niche have evolved similar adaptations. But they are different species, because each continent's wildlife evolved separately over millions of years. This is called convergent evolution — different species independently evolving similar traits in response to the same environmental pressures.

The lesson ends with the statement that "biomes are not fixed forever" — human activity and climate change are causing biome shifts. Using the lesson's climate → vegetation → biome chain, explain step-by-step what would happen to the TUNDRA biome if average global temperatures rose by 3°C over the next 50 years. Consider: what would change about the climate, then about the vegetation, then about the animals, then about the wider food chain. Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
🔗 Cross-subject link: Week 7 Science (particle model) — temperature affects how fast particles move and how substances change state. The same principle applies at a planetary scale: a 3°C global temperature rise melts permafrost (the permanently frozen ground in the tundra), releasing stored carbon dioxide and methane — gases that accelerate further warming. This is called a feedback loop.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Step 1 — Climate changes: A 3°C rise would significantly exceed the tundra's already fragile temperature range (currently −30°C to 5°C in summer). The permanently frozen ground (permafrost) — which sits just below the surface and defines tundra — would begin to thaw. The growing season would lengthen. Precipitation patterns would shift. Crucially, as permafrost thaws, it releases enormous stores of methane and CO₂ that have been locked in the frozen ground for thousands of years — creating a feedback loop that could accelerate warming beyond the initial 3°C. Step 2 — Vegetation changes: The short, ground-hugging tundra plants (mosses, lichens, low shrubs) are adapted to the cold, short growing season. As temperatures rise and the soil deepens (permafrost thawing), shrubs and eventually trees can take root. The tundra "greens" — this is already being observed in the Arctic and is called "Arctic greening." Eventually, forests begin to invade what was previously open tundra, fundamentally altering the biome. Step 3 — Animal changes: Cold-adapted species like polar bears, Arctic foxes, reindeer, and snowy owls lose their habitat and food sources. Species from warmer biomes — wolves, moose, birds from temperate forests — begin to move northward into the former tundra. Some cold-adapted species may have nowhere to retreat to (especially marine mammals dependent on sea ice). Step 4 — Food chain effects: The entire tundra food web was built on a very thin producer base (sparse tundra plants). As that producer base is replaced by new plant communities, every level of the food chain is disrupted. Species that evolved to eat specific tundra plants find their food source disappearing. Predators that relied on prey adapted to tundra find those prey species declining. New species arriving from warmer biomes compete with existing species at every level. Step 5 — The biome's future: In effect, the tundra biome as it currently exists would shrink and potentially disappear in some regions, replaced by a different biome (boreal forest / taiga). The lesson's chain completes: changed climate → changed vegetation → changed biome. This is not a future prediction alone — it is already being observed. The tundra biome demonstrates more clearly than almost any other how directly climate controls the living world.
