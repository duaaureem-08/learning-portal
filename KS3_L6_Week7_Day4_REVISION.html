🔍 Section 2 — Understanding Each Week
3
Week 1 — Creation
The lesson distinguishes between the Bible's creation account and a science textbook. What is the Bible NOT trying to answer in Genesis 1, and what IS it trying to answer? Why does the lesson say asking "which is right, Genesis or science?" is the wrong question? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Genesis 1 is NOT trying to answer: how old the universe is, what mechanism God used to create, or how species evolved — these are scientific questions about process and timeline. Genesis 1 IS trying to answer: why anything exists at all, who created it, what the relationship between God and creation is, and what value and meaning the created world has. The lesson says asking "which is right, Genesis or science?" is the wrong question because they are answering different kinds of questions — comparing them is like asking "which is more accurate, a map of London or a painting of London?" They serve different purposes and cannot contradict each other because they are not making the same kind of claim.
4
Week 2 — Imago Dei
The lesson argues that imago Dei is the foundation of human rights. Explain this argument in your own words: what does being made in God's image imply about every human being's value, and what does this mean for how we should treat people who are powerful vs people who are weak? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Being made in God's image means that every human being — regardless of age, ability, nationality, wealth, or status — carries inherent dignity that was given by God, not earned by achievement. This dignity cannot be taken away. For the powerful, it means their value does not come from their power — and the powerful have no right to treat others as less than human. For the weak — the poor, the disabled, the elderly, the unborn — it means they have exactly the same dignity as the most powerful person on earth, because dignity comes from being made in God's image, not from what you can contribute. The lesson calls this the "foundation" of human rights because if human dignity is grounded in God's image, it is universal and inalienable — no government can grant or remove it. If dignity is merely a social agreement, it can be revoked whenever convenient.
5
Week 3 — The Fall
The lesson describes the Fall as a story about a choice, not just a mistake. What was the choice Adam and Eve actually made (beyond eating fruit), and what were its three consequences as described in the lesson? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The deeper choice was: to place themselves — their own judgment about what was good — above God's word. The serpent's offer was "you will be like God, knowing good and evil" — the temptation was to become the arbiter of right and wrong themselves, rather than trusting God's definition. This is why the lesson says the Fall is not primarily about fruit but about who is in charge of defining good and evil. Three consequences: (1) Broken relationship with God — they hid from God, felt shame, and were eventually sent out of the Garden; the close relationship of walking with God was ruptured. (2) Broken relationship with each other — they immediately blamed each other; the first human relationship became one of blame and self-protection. (3) Broken relationship with creation — the ground became harder to work; pain entered childbirth; the harmony between humans and nature was disrupted. The lesson says all three kinds of brokenness we see in the world today trace back to this original fracture.

Weeks 4–7 — The Pattern of Faith
The lesson traces a repeating pattern across Noah, Abraham, Joseph, and Moses. Complete this table from memory. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Noah: Hardship = facing a global flood, ridicule while building an ark for ~100 years. Faith = building without rain, trusting God's word alone. God did = sent the flood, preserved Noah and his family and all animals, made a covenant never to flood the earth again (rainbow). Abraham: Hardship = called to leave his homeland with no destination; later called to sacrifice his son Isaac. Faith = left without knowing where he was going (Hebrews 11:8); was willing to sacrifice Isaac, trusting God would provide. God did = led him to Canaan, gave him descendants as numerous as stars, stopped the sacrifice of Isaac and provided a ram, made the covenant of the promised land and nation. Joseph: Hardship = sold into slavery by his own brothers, falsely accused by Potiphar's wife, imprisoned for years. Faith = maintained integrity throughout (refused Potiphar's wife; interpreted dreams faithfully; forgave his brothers fully). God did = elevated Joseph to second-in-command of Egypt; used the suffering to save many lives including Joseph's own family during the famine. Joseph's summary: "you intended to harm me, but God intended it for good" (Genesis 50:20). Moses: Hardship = called to confront the most powerful empire on earth on behalf of an enslaved people; went before Pharaoh ten times. Faith = kept going after ten rejections; declared faith at the Red Sea when there was no escape visible (Exodus 14:14). God did = sent ten plagues, delivered Israel through the Red Sea, destroyed Pharaoh's army, gave the Ten Commandments at Sinai.

7
Weeks 1–7 — Synoptic
The lesson describes a single overarching story running from Genesis to Exodus: creation → fall → covenant → rescue. Using specific examples from each week (Weeks 1–7), trace this story arc. Show how each week's content connects to the next step in the arc. Write at least 6 sentences. 10 marks
🌟 Synoptic question — this asks you to connect ALL seven weeks into one argument. Don't just list what happened each week — explain how each week LEADS INTO the next step of the story.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Creation (Weeks 1–2): God creates a world that is "very good" (Genesis 1:31) — ordered, beautiful, and filled with beings made in His image. Humans are given both dignity (imago Dei) and responsibility (stewardship) — they are neither gods nor animals, but image-bearers with a unique calling to care for creation and relate to God. This is the world as it was meant to be. Fall (Week 3): The perfection of creation is shattered by human choice — Adam and Eve choose their own judgment over God's word. The three relationships that define good human existence — with God, with each other, and with creation — are all damaged. The rest of the Bible is the story of how God responds to this damage. Covenant (Weeks 4–5): God does not abandon the world He created. He responds to increasing human corruption not with permanent destruction but with covenant — first with Noah (preserving creation and humanity; the rainbow promise), then with Abraham (choosing one man and one family through whom He will bless "all peoples on earth"). The covenant is God committing to fix what the Fall broke, using human history as the vehicle. Joseph (Week 6): Joseph's story is a bridge — through extraordinary suffering and providence, God preserves the family of Abraham (Jacob's 12 sons, who become the 12 tribes of Israel) in Egypt. What looks like disaster is God preparing the stage for the next act. Moses and Rescue (Week 7): The rescue begins. God acts in history on the most public possible scale — ten plagues, the Red Sea, the destruction of the most powerful army in the world — to deliver His people. The Exodus is not just a political liberation; it is the covenant with Abraham being fulfilled: God is building His nation. The law given at Sinai defines what the rescued community should look like. The arc holds together: God creates → humans break → God restores through covenant and rescue. The Exodus is the Old Testament's defining answer to the question raised by the Fall: does God have both the will and the power to fix what has been broken? The answer is a public, dramatic yes.

⚡ Section 1 — Core Skill Consolidation
1
Weeks 1–7 — Key Terms
Define each term precisely and give a one-line example or illustration for each. 8 marks

purpose · audience · inference · rhetorical question · emotive language · counter-argument · fact · opinion
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
purpose: the reason a text was written — what the writer wants to achieve. Five main purposes: inform, persuade, explain, entertain, instruct. Example: a charity leaflet has the primary purpose of persuading people to donate. audience: the intended reader or group of readers a text is aimed at. Example: a medical instruction leaflet targets adult patients and carers, not children. inference: a conclusion drawn from evidence in the text that is not directly stated. Example: if a character slams a door and doesn't speak at dinner, you can infer they are angry — the text never says so. rhetorical question: a question asked to make a point, not to receive an answer. Example: "Can we really afford to ignore the climate crisis?" emotive language: words chosen to provoke an emotional response (fear, sympathy, outrage, hope). Example: "innocent children are suffering needlessly every day." counter-argument: acknowledging the opposing view before refuting it. Example: "While some argue uniforms are expensive, the evidence shows they reduce long-term clothing costs." fact: a statement that can be verified as true or false. Example: "The population of London is approximately 9 million." opinion: a statement of belief or judgement that cannot be objectively verified. Example: "London is the best city in the world."
2
Weeks 3 & 7 — Inference & Analysis
Read this short extract and answer the questions below it.

"The factory had been running for thirty years. Fourteen-hour shifts, no weekends, no complaint — until the morning Ahmed did not show up. The foreman checked the roster. Ahmed had been there every single day. Every single one."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Inference 1: Ahmed was an extremely dedicated and reliable worker. Evidence: "every single day" for thirty years of fourteen-hour shifts — this is an extraordinary record of attendance. Inference 2: Something serious has happened to Ahmed — either he is ill, injured, dead, or has left under dramatic circumstances. Evidence: the foreman "checked the roster" as if he couldn't believe it — the act of checking implies the absence is so unusual it seems impossible. Ahmed's perfect record makes any absence alarming. b) The repetition of "Every single one" creates emphasis and weight — it slows the reader down and forces them to feel the significance of Ahmed's record. It also creates a contrast: the sentence before lists thirty years of routine; the repetition turns one absence into something that feels like a rupture in reality. The rhythm also mimics the relentlessness of Ahmed's attendance. c) Ending without resolution creates mystery and unease — the reader is left wondering what happened, which is more powerful than being told. It also mirrors the foreman's experience: he is also in the moment of discovering something wrong, with no explanation yet. The open ending makes the reader complicit — we feel the same unsettling uncertainty the foreman feels. This technique is called an open or ambiguous ending; it invites the reader to engage imaginatively.

Week 7 — Non-Fiction
The revision article below combines techniques from across Weeks 1–7. Read it carefully then answer Questions 4–6.

Headline: LIBRARIES ARE NOT A LUXURY — THEY ARE A LIFELINE

Every week in this country, another library closes. Another community loses its free meeting place, its quiet refuge, its only reliable source of books and internet for people who cannot afford them at home.

Libraries are not relics. They are not the preserve of the elderly or the bookish. According to the Chartered Institute of Library and Information Professionals, over 15 million people visited public libraries in England last year — and 40% of them were under 25.

Some local councils argue that budget pressures leave them no choice. But cutting a library is a false economy. Research from the Reading Agency shows that every £1 invested in public library services generates £5 of social value — through reduced loneliness, improved mental health, greater literacy, and stronger communities.

"When a library closes, it is not just books that disappear," said Dr Fatima Hassan, Professor of Information Science at University College London. "It is the only safe, free, public space that some of the most vulnerable people in our society have access to."

You can argue about budgets. You can argue about priorities. But you cannot argue that taking away the only safe space from the people who have the least is a good idea. Close a library — and you close a door on the people who most need it open.
This question refers to the article above.
4
Week 7 — Purpose & Audience
Identify the primary and secondary purpose of this article, and describe the intended audience. Support each answer with one piece of specific evidence from the text. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Primary purpose: to persuade. Evidence: the article ends with a direct, emotive call to action — "Close a library — and you close a door on the people who most need it open." This is not informing; it is arguing for a position and trying to make inaction feel morally indefensible. Secondary purpose: to inform. Evidence: the article cites specific statistics — "15 million people visited public libraries" and "every £1 generates £5 of social value" — to provide factual backing for its argument. The named sources (CILIP, Reading Agency) give it credibility beyond opinion. Intended audience: adults — particularly local councillors, voters, and policy-interested citizens. Evidence: the reference to "local councils" arguing about "budget pressures" positions the article as addressing people who can influence or vote on library funding decisions. The vocabulary level (e.g. "false economy," "social value," "Chartered Institute") assumes an educated adult reader.
5
Week 7 — Language Features
Identify FOUR different language techniques used in the article. For each: name the technique, quote the exact phrase (keep it short), and explain the effect on the reader. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any four of: Rule of three: "its free meeting place, its quiet refuge, its only reliable source" — creates rhythm and completeness, making the library feel like it provides multiple irreplaceable services. Rhetorical question (implied): "You can argue about budgets. You can argue about priorities. But you cannot argue..." — the repeated "you can argue... but" structure builds to a point that feels unanswerable. Expert quotation: Dr Fatima Hassan, UCL Professor — gives authority; makes the emotional claim about "the most vulnerable" sound like credentialed analysis, not just sentiment. Counter-argument: "Some local councils argue that budget pressures leave them no choice" — acknowledging the opposing view makes the writer appear fair, then the "false economy" rebuttal dismisses it efficiently. Emotive language: "the people who most need it open" — framing library closure as harm specifically to the most vulnerable triggers protective instincts and moral discomfort in the reader. Statistics: "every £1 generates £5" — a specific ratio feels precise and evidence-based, making the "false economy" claim concrete rather than vague. Repetition: "Close a library — and you close a door" — the verb "close" repeated frames the action as a double closure, physical and metaphorical.
6
Weeks 3 & 7 — Critical Reading
Apply the critical reader's question: "What is this article NOT telling me?" Identify TWO significant omissions. For each, explain what a more complete picture would include — and whether the omission makes the article dishonest or just persuasive. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Omission 1: No acknowledgment of digital alternatives. The article doesn't address whether e-books, free online resources, or mobile libraries could partially replace physical libraries — a reasonable point a counter-arguer would make. A more complete picture would acknowledge these exist and then explain why they don't fully substitute (not everyone has a device or data; digital literacy support requires a physical space; social isolation cannot be addressed online). This omission is persuasive but not dishonest — the article doesn't claim digital alternatives don't exist; it simply doesn't engage with them. Omission 2: The "£1 generates £5" figure is uncontextualised. The article presents this statistic as definitive without explaining how "social value" is calculated — it is an economic concept that can be measured in various ways, and different methodologies can produce very different results. A more complete picture would acknowledge this is one study's estimate with a specific methodology. However, this is a common limitation of statistics in persuasive writing, not fabrication. Persuasive, not dishonest — the figure appears to be real research, but selective citation of one favourable study is a normal feature of advocacy writing.

Weeks 1–7 — Synthesis
You have studied three non-fiction articles in depth across Weeks 7A, 7B, and 7C, plus the library article above. Write a paragraph (minimum 5 sentences) explaining what makes a non-fiction text effective — using specific techniques from at least THREE of these four articles as examples. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
An effective non-fiction text combines credibility with emotional engagement in proportions calibrated to its specific audience. The plastic oceans article demonstrates how a dramatic opening statistic — only 9% of plastic ever produced has been recycled — functions as a hook that makes the reader feel the scale of a problem immediately; without this emotional and factual opening, the article's call to action would feel baseless. The young people article shows that effective persuasion often works through what it concedes as well as what it asserts — by acknowledging "yes, there are challenges" before pivoting to its counter-argument, the writer builds trust with a sceptical reader. The library article demonstrates the power of expert quotation: Dr Hassan's comment is not simply informative but strategically placed at the article's emotional centre, immediately after a factual section, to transition from reason to feeling. Across all four articles, the most effective technique is the same: choosing the right emotional register for the specific audience. The school holiday article uses alarm and urgency because its audience (policy-makers and parents) needs to feel the cost of inaction; the young people article uses measured evidence because its audience needs to be persuaded, not panicked. Effective non-fiction is not simply well-argued — it is precisely targeted, combining verifiable facts with techniques that make those facts land as feelings.

Week 1 — Scientific Method
A student investigates whether the colour of light affects plant growth. (a) Write a hypothesis. (b) Name the independent, dependent, and three control variables. (c) Why should the student repeat each colour three times and take a mean? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Sample hypothesis: "Plants grown under blue light will grow taller than plants grown under red or green light because blue light wavelengths are most efficiently absorbed by chlorophyll for photosynthesis." (b) Independent = colour of light. Dependent = plant growth (measured in cm of height, or mass, or leaf number — specify one). Controls: same species of plant, same volume of water, same temperature, same length of light exposure per day, same soil/nutrient content. (c) Repeating and taking a mean: individual results can be anomalous due to chance variation (one plant might be a poor grower regardless of light colour). Repeating three times and averaging removes the impact of random variation and makes the results more reliable and representative of the true pattern.

Week 2 — Cells & Biology
Compare a plant cell and an animal cell. (a) List THREE structures found in plant cells but NOT in animal cells. (b) For each, state its function. (c) Why do plant cells need chloroplasts but animal cells do not? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a)+(b): Cell wall — made of cellulose; provides structural support and maintains the shape of the cell, preventing it from bursting. Chloroplasts — contain chlorophyll; the site of photosynthesis, where light energy is converted into glucose. Large permanent vacuole — contains cell sap; maintains cell rigidity through osmotic pressure (turgor). (c) Plant cells need chloroplasts because they are autotrophs — they make their own food through photosynthesis, using sunlight, water, and CO₂ to produce glucose. Animal cells are heterotrophs — they obtain glucose by eating and digesting food from other organisms. Since animals get their glucose externally, they have no need for the machinery to manufacture it from sunlight.

A car accelerates from 10 m/s to 34 m/s in 8 seconds. (a) Calculate the acceleration. (b) The car has mass 1,200kg. Calculate the resultant force causing this acceleration. (c) Explain what "resultant force" means. (d) If the engine force is 6,500N, what is the friction force? 6 marks
acceleration = Δv ÷ t    force = mass × acceleration
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) a=(34−10)÷8=24÷8=3 m/s². (b) F=1200×3=3,600N. (c) The resultant force is the single net force acting on an object after all forces are added together (taking direction into account). Week 4 — Ecosystems
A food web: grass → rabbit → fox. Grass → grasshopper → frog → heron. (a) Name one producer, one primary consumer, one secondary consumer, one tertiary consumer. (b) What would happen to the frog population if all foxes were removed? Explain the knock-on effects through TWO other organisms. (c) What is the difference between a food chain and a food web? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Producer: grass. Primary consumer: rabbit or grasshopper. Secondary consumer: fox or frog. Tertiary consumer: heron. (b) If foxes are removed: rabbit population increases (no predation) → rabbits eat more grass → grass population decreases. OR: foxes also eat rabbits which may have competed with grasshoppers for grass → with fewer rabbits, grasshoppers may increase → frogs increase (more food) → herons increase (more frogs). Either chain is acceptable; the key is showing cascade effects through at least two organisms with explanations. (c) A food chain shows a single sequence of feeding relationships (A eats B, B eats C). Week 5 — Energy
A 2kg ball is lifted to a height of 5m above the ground. (a) Calculate its gravitational potential energy (g = 10 N/kg). (b) It is then dropped. Calculate its kinetic energy just before it hits the ground (assume no energy is lost). (c) Calculate its speed just before impact. (d) In practice, the ball hits the ground with less kinetic energy than calculated. Where has the missing energy gone? 6 marks
GPE = mgh    KE = ½mv²    rearranged: v = √(2×KE÷m)
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) GPE=2×10×5=100J. (b) By conservation of energy (no losses): KE=GPE=100J. (c) v=√(2×100÷2)=√100=10 m/s. (d) In practice, air resistance acts on the falling ball, converting some kinetic energy into thermal energy (heat) in the air and in the ball. The ball also deforms slightly on contact, converting energy into sound and heat. Energy is never destroyed — it is transferred to less useful forms. This is why real falling objects arrive slower than theoretical calculations predict.

Week 6 — Waves & Light
(a) State the wave equation and use it: a wave has frequency 500Hz and wavelength 0.68m. Calculate its speed. (b) Light travels at 3×10⁸ m/s. Calculate the wavelength of red light with frequency 4.3×10¹⁴ Hz. Give your answer in nm (1m = 10⁹ nm). (c) Explain the difference between transverse and longitudinal waves, giving one example of each. 6 marks
wave speed = frequency × wavelength   (v = fλ)
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) v=500×0.68=340 m/s (speed of sound in air). (b) λ=v÷f=3×10⁸÷4.3×10¹⁴=6.977×10⁻⁷m. Convert to nm: ×10⁹=697.7nm ≈ 698nm (correctly in the red visible light range). (c) Transverse wave: the oscillation (vibration) is perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of wave travel. Example: light waves, water surface waves, waves on a string. Longitudinal wave: the oscillation is parallel to the direction of wave travel — the medium compresses and rarefies (spreads out) in the same direction the wave moves. Example: sound waves, seismic P-waves. Key memory aid: transverse = cross (perpendicular); longitudinal = along (parallel).

Week 7 — States of Matter
(a) Describe the arrangement, movement, and spacing of particles in a GAS. (b) A substance melts at 801°C and boils at 1,465°C. At room temperature (25°C), what state is it in? (c) Name this substance (clue: it is a very common compound). (d) Explain why this substance has such a high melting point compared to water (0°C) — use the particle model to compare the forces involved. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Gas: arrangement = random, no fixed positions. Movement = very fast, in all directions, completely free. Spacing = very far apart (much larger than in liquid or solid — mostly empty space). (b) 25°C is below the melting point of 801°C, so the substance is a solid at room temperature. (c) This is sodium chloride (NaCl) — common table salt. Melting point 801°C, boiling point 1,465°C are the correct values for NaCl. (d) NaCl is an ionic compound — its particles are Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions held together by very strong electrostatic forces (ionic bonds) between opposite charges. To melt salt, you must provide enough energy to overcome these powerful ionic attractions across an entire lattice of millions of ions. Water (H₂O) is a molecular compound — its molecules are held together by much weaker intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces). These are far easier to overcome, so water melts at just 0°C. The enormous difference in melting points reflects the enormous difference in the strength of the forces between particles.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Sample: Phenomenon — a thunderstorm. Week 5 (Energy): a thunderstorm is powered by enormous transfers of energy. Warm, moist air rises rapidly (convection — thermal energy transfer), carrying water vapour high into the atmosphere. As it rises and cools, the vapour condenses, releasing latent heat (Wk 7 — states of matter) which powers further rising — a positive feedback loop that builds cumulonimbus clouds kilometres high. The state changes of water are central to every stage of storm development. The 3km per second difference in the speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s) vs sound (340 m/s) explains the delay between seeing lightning and hearing thunder — you can calculate the storm's distance by counting the seconds and multiplying by 340m. All three weeks' concepts — energy transfer, state changes, and wave behaviour — are essential to a complete explanation of a thunderstorm.

Week 7 — Climate & Biomes
Quick-fire biome questions. Answer each in one or two sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Weather = short-term, day-to-day atmospheric conditions. Climate = long-term average of weather patterns over 30+ years. "Climate is what you expect; weather is what you get." b) Five factors: latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, ocean currents, prevailing winds. c) Near the equator, the sun's rays hit at a direct, concentrated angle — high energy per unit area. Example: Atacama Desert (eastern Andes), Nevada/Great Basin (eastern Cascades). f) Tropical rainforest. Found in the tundra biome (Arctic regions of Russia, Canada, Alaska, Scandinavia). h) The chain means: climate (temperature and rainfall patterns) determines what vegetation can grow → the type of vegetation defines the whole ecosystem (its food chains, soil type, species composition) → therefore climate ultimately shapes the entire biome. Change the climate, and the vegetation changes, and the whole biome transforms.
