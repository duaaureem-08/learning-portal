⭐ Memory Verse
"The LORD is my shepherd, I lack nothing."
Psalm 23:1 — NIV
🧠
Word-by-word: David — who had literally BEEN a shepherd caring for sheep — describes GOD as his shepherd. "I lack nothing" does not mean David had everything he ever wanted; it means his deepest needs were met by God's care, the same way a well-cared-for sheep lacks nothing under a good shepherd. This verse comes from someone who knew shepherding firsthand.
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Learn these words before you continue
anoint / anointed
To pour oil on someone's head as a sign that God has chosen them for a special role, like king or priest. Samuel anointed David as the future king.
shepherd
A person who cares for and protects sheep. David was literally a shepherd before he became king — the job shaped how he later led people.
psalm
A sacred song or poem, often addressed to God. David wrote many of the 150 psalms in the book of Psalms — prayers, praises, and honest cries to God.
repentance
Genuinely turning away from wrongdoing and turning back toward God — more than just saying sorry, it means a real change of heart and direction.
covenant
A serious, binding agreement between God and people. God made a covenant promise with David about his royal descendants.
Goliath / champion
A "champion" was a chosen fighter who represented an entire army in single combat. Goliath was the Philistines' champion — a giant warrior.
worship
Expressing love, honour, and devotion to God — through songs, prayer, actions, or a whole life lived for God. David is described as a great worshipper.
integrity
Being the same honest, good person whether or not anyone is watching. The Bible describes David as someone with "integrity of heart," even though he also made serious mistakes.
🐑 From Shepherd Boy to Anointed King — 1 Samuel 16
📍
When God rejects Saul as king, he sends the prophet Samuel to Bethlehem to anoint a replacement — one of Jesse's sons. Samuel expects the tallest, most impressive-looking son to be God's choice.
🔑
The key verse of this story: "The LORD does not look at the things people look at. People look at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). David — the youngest, overlooked, out tending sheep while his brothers were presented to Samuel — is God's chosen king.
👀 What People Looked At
Height and physical stature
Birth order (David was the youngest of 8)
Who was already present and prepared
Outward, visible impressiveness
❤️ What God Looked At
The condition and character of the heart
Faithfulness in a small, unnoticed job (shepherding)
Trust in God rather than self-reliance
Who God had already chosen, unseen by others
⚔️ David and Goliath — 1 Samuel 17
1
A terrifying standoff
Goliath, a giant Philistine champion, challenges Israel to single combat for 40 days. Israel's army — including David's older brothers — is paralysed with fear.
2
David's confidence — rooted in past faithfulness
David explains he has already killed a lion and a bear while protecting his father's sheep — "the LORD who rescued me... will rescue me" from Goliath (17:37).
3
Refusing borrowed armour
David rejects King Saul's armour because it doesn't fit him and he hasn't tested it — going instead with what he knows: a sling and five smooth stones.
4
Victory through one stone
David declares he comes "in the name of the LORD Almighty" (17:45) — not his own strength — and defeats Goliath with a single sling stone.
✅
KS3 key idea: David's courage was not blind — it was built on remembering specific past experiences of God's faithfulness (the lion, the bear). Courage often grows from remembering, not forgetting, what has already been overcome.
📜 David the Psalmist — Honest Prayers
💡
David wrote roughly half of the 150 psalms in the Bible. Unlike a polished public speech, psalms are raw and honest — full of joy, fear, anger, doubt, and praise, sometimes all in the same psalm.
PsalmWhat It Expresses
Psalm 23Trust — "The LORD is my shepherd, I lack nothing."
Psalm 51Repentance — written after David's sin with Bathsheba; a raw confession asking God to "create in me a pure heart."
Psalm 34Praise — "I will extol the LORD at all times" written during a time of danger and fear.
Psalm 13Honest lament — "How long, LORD? Will you forget me forever?" — David questioning God openly.
✅
KS3 key idea: The Psalms model a kind of prayer that does not hide difficult emotions from God. David brings his full self — doubt and confidence, sin and repentance — into his relationship with God rather than only presenting a "cleaned-up" version.
💔 David's Great Failure and Repentance — 2 Samuel 11-12
⚠️
David — by now king — commits serious sins: he takes another man's wife, Bathsheba, and then arranges for her husband Uriah to be killed in battle to hide his wrongdoing.
Stage 1
The prophet Nathan confronts David
Nathan tells David a story about a rich man stealing a poor man's only lamb. David is outraged — until Nathan says "You are that man!" (2 Samuel 12:7).
Stage 2
David's genuine repentance
Unlike many other kings of the time, David does not deny it, make excuses, or punish Nathan. He confesses: "I have sinned against the LORD" (12:13).
Stage 3
Consequences remain, but the relationship is restored
David faces real, painful consequences for his sin — but he is not rejected by God the way Saul was. Psalm 51 is his prayer of repentance from this exact moment.
🔑
The "heart after God" idea: David is called "a man after God's own heart" (1 Samuel 13:14) — not because he was perfect (he clearly was not), but because of how he responded when confronted with his sin: honestly, without excuses, genuinely turning back to God.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these terms for reading and writing poetry
rhyme
When words end with the same or similar sounds. "Cat" and "hat" rhyme. Rhyme creates a musical, memorable pattern.
rhythm
The pattern of beats or stressed/unstressed syllables in a line of poetry — like a musical pulse running through the words.
alliteration
Repeating the SAME beginning consonant sound in nearby words. "Peter Piper picked a peck" — repeated "p" sound.
onomatopoeia
A word that sounds like the noise it describes. "Buzz," "crash," "sizzle," "hiss" — the word IS the sound.
simile
A comparison using "like" or "as." "Brave as a lion." Two different things compared directly with a linking word.
metaphor
A comparison WITHOUT "like" or "as" — saying one thing IS another. "The LORD is my shepherd" (Psalm 23) is a metaphor.
stanza
A group of lines in a poem, like a paragraph in prose. Poems are often divided into several stanzas.
verse / line
A single line of a poem. Several lines together form a stanza.
🎵 Rhyme — Patterns of Sound
💡
Rhyme schemes are described using letters — each new rhyme sound gets a new letter. AABB means lines 1&2 rhyme with each other, and lines 3&4 rhyme with each other (a different sound).
Rhyme SchemePatternExample Structure
AABBRhyming couplets — pairs of linesLine 1 rhymes with Line 2; Line 3 rhymes with Line 4
ABABAlternating rhymeLine 1 rhymes with Line 3; Line 2 rhymes with Line 4
ABCBOnly lines 2 and 4 rhymeCommon in ballads and simple songs
Free verseNo fixed rhyme scheme at allMany modern poems use free verse deliberately
💡
ESL tip: Rhyme is not required for something to be a poem! Many powerful poems (and much of the Bible's poetry, like the Psalms) do not rhyme at all in the original Hebrew — they use OTHER devices like rhythm and repetition instead.
🥁 Rhythm — The Musical Pulse
🔑
Golden idea: Rhythm in poetry comes from the pattern of STRESSED (emphasised) and unstressed syllables. Read a line aloud and clap on the naturally emphasised syllables — that clapping pattern IS the rhythm.
"Twinkle, twinkle, little star,
How I wonder what you are."
✅
KS3 key idea: This regular "da-DUM-da-DUM" pattern is called a metre. Not all poems have such a strict rhythm — but even free verse poems use rhythm deliberately, often through varying line lengths and pauses, to control the pace at which a reader experiences the poem.
🔤 Sound Devices — Alliteration & Onomatopoeia
DeviceWhat It DoesExample
AlliterationRepeats a beginning consonant sound, creating rhythm and emphasis"The wild wind whipped and wailed."
OnomatopoeiaA word that imitates the actual sound it describes"The kettle hissed and the bacon sizzled."
AssonanceRepeats a VOWEL sound within nearby words (advanced device)"The rain in Spain falls mainly on the plain."
RepetitionRepeats a word or phrase for emphasis"Be strong and courageous... be strong and courageous" (Joshua 1)
🎭 Simile vs Metaphor — Two Ways to Compare
Simile — uses "like" or "as"
"He was as brave as a lion."
"Her eyes shone like diamonds."
Directly compares two DIFFERENT things using a linking word
Metaphor — no linking word
"The LORD is my shepherd." (Psalm 23)
"Time is a thief."
States that one thing simply IS another — a stronger, more direct comparison
✅
KS3 key idea: Metaphors are often considered more powerful than similes because they collapse the distance between two things entirely — Psalm 23 doesn't say God is LIKE a shepherd; it says God IS the shepherd, making the comparison feel total and complete.
📖 Worked Example — Poetic Devices in Psalm 23
[METAPHOR]"The LORD is my shepherd" — God is described directly as a shepherd, not "like" one. [IMAGERY]"He makes me lie down in green pastures, he leads me beside quiet waters" — vivid visual and peaceful imagery lets the reader picture the scene. [REPETITION]"Surely goodness and love will follow me" echoes earlier lines about God's constant care, reinforcing the poem's central idea of trust.

CoordinatesFour QuadrantsReflectionsKS3 Level 6
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
The building blocks of coordinate geometry
coordinate
A pair of numbers (x, y) that describes the exact position of a point on a grid.
x-axis
The HORIZONTAL number line on a grid — running left to right.
y-axis
The VERTICAL number line on a grid — running up and down.
origin
The point where the x-axis and y-axis cross — coordinates (0, 0).
quadrant
One of the four sections the axes divide the plane into. Numbered 1 to 4, going anticlockwise from the top-right.
plot
To mark a point's exact position on a grid using its coordinates.
reflection
Flipping a point or shape over a line (like a mirror image) — the axis acts as the "mirror."
📍 Plotting a Coordinate — (x, y)
🔑
Golden rule — "along the corridor, up the stairs": Every coordinate is written (x, y). ALWAYS move along the x-axis (horizontal) FIRST, then up/down the y-axis (vertical) SECOND. The order matters — (3, 5) is a completely different point from (5, 3)!
CoordinateHow to Plot It
(4, 2)Move 4 right along the x-axis, then 2 up.
(−3, 5)Move 3 LEFT along the x-axis, then 5 up.
(0, 6)Don't move left/right — go straight up 6 (stays on the y-axis).
(−2, −4)Move 2 left, then 4 DOWN.
🗺️ The Four Quadrants
💡
The x-axis and y-axis divide the coordinate plane into FOUR regions called quadrants. Each quadrant has a distinct sign pattern for its coordinates.
Quadrantx-valuey-valueExample Point
Quadrant 1 (top-right)Positive (+)Positive (+)(3, 4)
Quadrant 2 (top-left)Negative (−)Positive (+)(−3, 4)
Quadrant 3 (bottom-left)Negative (−)Negative (−)(−3, −4)
Quadrant 4 (bottom-right)Positive (+)Negative (−)(3, −4)
⚠️
Common mistake: Quadrants are numbered ANTICLOCKWISE starting from the top-right, not clockwise. Many students guess clockwise — always double check!
🪞 Reflections on the Coordinate Plane
Reflecting a point over the X-AXIS: flip the sign of y, keep x the same
Point (3, 5) → reflected over x-axis → (3, −5)

Reflecting a point over the Y-AXIS: flip the sign of x, keep y the same
Point (3, 5) → reflected over y-axis → (−3, 5)

Reflecting over BOTH axes (i.e. through the origin): flip both signs
Point (3, 5) → reflected through origin → (−3, −5)
✅
KS3 key idea: A reflection is like a mirror image — the DISTANCE from the axis (mirror line) stays exactly the same on both sides. If a point is 5 units above the x-axis, its reflection is 5 units below it.
🌍 Coordinates in Real Life
1
Map references
Latitude and longitude (studied in Week 5 Geography) work exactly like coordinates — two numbers describing an exact position.
2
Video game design
Every character and object's position in a 2D video game is tracked using x,y coordinates on screen.
3
Graphic design and art
Digital images are built from pixels, each with its own coordinate position, allowing precise editing.

Physical ChangesChemical ReactionsReversibility
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Science Guide
Builds on Week 9 — elements, compounds, and how they combine
physical change
A change in FORM or APPEARANCE only — no new substance is created. The material can usually be changed back.
chemical change / reaction
A change that creates a completely NEW substance with different properties. Usually cannot be easily reversed.
reversible
Able to be changed back to the original substance or state. Most physical changes are reversible.
irreversible
NOT able to be changed back to the original substance. Most chemical changes are irreversible.
precipitate
A solid that forms and separates out of a liquid during a chemical reaction — often a sign a reaction has happened.
exothermic
A reaction that RELEASES heat energy to its surroundings — things get warmer. "Exo" = out.
endothermic
A reaction that ABSORBS heat energy from its surroundings — things get colder. "Endo" = in.
combustion
A chemical reaction where a substance burns rapidly, combining with oxygen and releasing heat and light.
🔍 Physical vs Chemical Changes — The Core Difference
🔑
Golden rule: In a physical change, the SUBSTANCE stays the same — only its shape, size, or state changes. In a chemical change, a completely NEW substance is formed, with new chemical properties.
🔄 Physical Change
No new substance is formed
Usually reversible
Only appearance, shape, or state changes
Examples: melting ice, tearing paper, dissolving sugar, crushing a can
⚗️ Chemical Change
A new substance IS formed, with new properties
Usually irreversible
Involves breaking and forming chemical bonds
Examples: burning wood, rusting iron, baking a cake, an egg cooking
🚩 Five Signs of a Chemical Reaction
1
Colour change
An unexpected change in colour often signals new substances forming — e.g. iron rusting turns orange-brown.
2
Gas produced (bubbles/fizzing)
Bubbles forming — especially without heating — often indicate a gas is being released as a new product.
3
Temperature change
The mixture gets noticeably hotter (exothermic) or colder (endothermic) without external heating or cooling.
4
Precipitate forms
A solid suddenly appears in a liquid that was previously clear — a new insoluble substance has formed.
5
Light or smell produced
A flame, glow, or new/different smell appearing suggests a chemical reaction is happening (e.g. combustion).
⚠️
Common mistake: Bubbles alone don't always mean a chemical reaction! Boiling water produces bubbles too, but that's a PHYSICAL change (state change) — no new substance forms, just liquid water becoming water vapour.
📊 Sorting Examples — Physical or Chemical?
ExampleTypeWhy
Ice melting into waterPhysicalStill H₂O — just changed from solid to liquid state. Can be re-frozen.
Iron rustingChemicalIron + oxygen forms iron oxide (rust) — a new substance with different properties.
Cutting a piece of paperPhysicalStill paper — just smaller pieces. No new substance formed.
Burning a piece of paperChemicalPaper becomes ash, smoke, and gases — completely new substances. Cannot be reversed.
Dissolving salt in waterPhysicalSalt is still salt (just spread throughout the water) — can be recovered by evaporation (Week 8!).
Baking a cakeChemicalRaw ingredients undergo irreversible chemical reactions (e.g. proteins changing structure) to form a cake — you cannot "un-bake" it.
🔥 Exothermic vs Endothermic Reactions
Exothermic (releases heat — gets WARMER):
Combustion (burning), most oxidation reactions, adding acid to water

Endothermic (absorbs heat — gets COLDER):
Photosynthesis (absorbs light energy), some dissolving reactions (e.g. certain salts in water), thermal decomposition
✅
KS3 key idea: "Exo" means OUT (exit) — energy goes OUT to the surroundings. "Endo" means IN — energy is taken IN from the surroundings. A cold pack you squeeze to cool an injury uses an endothermic reaction!

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL History Guide
Builds on Week 9 — medieval Europe and the feudal system
Crusade
A series of religious wars (1095-1291) in which European Christians tried to capture and hold the "Holy Land" (Jerusalem and surrounding areas) from Muslim control.
Holy Land
The region around Jerusalem, considered sacred by Christians, Muslims, and Jews alike, as central to each religion's history.
pilgrimage
A journey to a sacred place for religious reasons. Christian pilgrimage to Jerusalem was a major motivation behind the Crusades.
cultural exchange
The sharing of ideas, knowledge, goods, and customs between different peoples or civilisations who come into contact.
Islamic Golden Age
A period (roughly 8th-14th century) when Islamic civilisation led the world in science, medicine, mathematics, and philosophy.
scholar
A person devoted to learning and academic study. Islamic scholars preserved and expanded on ancient Greek and Roman knowledge.
algebra
A branch of mathematics whose name comes from the Arabic word "al-jabr," developed extensively by Islamic mathematicians.
trade route
An established path used regularly for the exchange of goods between regions — Crusader contact opened new trade connections between Europe and the Middle East.
⚔️ What Were the Crusades? — Causes
💡
In 1095, Pope Urban II called on European Christians to march to Jerusalem and reclaim the Holy Land from Muslim (Seljuk Turk) control. This launched the First Crusade and began roughly 200 years of intermittent religious warfare.
CauseExplanation
Religious motivationRecapturing Jerusalem — sacred to Christianity as the site of Jesus' death and resurrection — was framed as a holy duty.
Church encouragementThe Pope promised forgiveness of sins ("indulgences") to those who fought, a powerful incentive in medieval Christian belief (connects to Week 9's study of Church power).
Protecting pilgrimsEuropean Christians travelling to the Holy Land as pilgrims reported difficulty and danger under Muslim control.
Political & economic motivesSome knights and nobles sought land, wealth, glory, or a productive outlet for feudal warrior energy (connecting to Week 9's knights and chivalry).
🗺️ Timeline — Key Crusades
⚠️
There were multiple Crusades over roughly 200 years, with mixed and largely unsuccessful long-term outcomes for the Crusaders' goal of holding Jerusalem permanently.
CrusadeApprox. DateOutcome
First Crusade1096-1099Crusaders successfully captured Jerusalem, establishing Crusader states.
Second Crusade1147-1149Largely unsuccessful; failed to achieve its goals.
Third Crusade1189-1192Famous conflict between Richard the Lionheart and Sultan Saladin; Jerusalem remained under Muslim control, though a truce allowed Christian pilgrim access.
Later Crusades1200sGradually less successful; Crusader states in the Holy Land were eventually lost entirely by 1291.
🌟 What Europe Gained — Cultural Exchange
🔑
The key historical lesson: Although the Crusades themselves largely failed militarily, extended contact between Europe and the Islamic world triggered one of history's most significant transfers of knowledge, goods, and ideas — arguably more historically significant than the wars themselves.
AreaWhat Europe Gained from the Islamic World
MathematicsAlgebra (the word itself is Arabic), and the Hindu-Arabic numeral system (0-9) that replaced clumsy Roman numerals across Europe.
MedicineAdvanced medical knowledge — hospitals, surgical techniques, and pharmacology far beyond what medieval Europe had developed independently.
Preserved classical knowledgeIslamic scholars had preserved and translated ancient Greek and Roman texts (philosophy, science) that had been lost in Western Europe after Rome's fall — this knowledge flowed back to Europe.
Trade goodsSpices, silk, sugar, and new agricultural products became available in Europe through expanded trade connections.
Architecture & navigationNew building techniques and improved navigational tools (like the astrolabe) spread into European use.
⚖️ Balanced View — Costs and Legacy
💔 The Human Cost
Enormous loss of life on all sides over two centuries of conflict
Violence against Muslim, Jewish, and even some Christian communities
Long-lasting tension between Christian Europe and the Islamic world
🌉 The Lasting Exchange
Expanded European trade networks and economic growth
Transfer of scientific and mathematical knowledge that helped enable the later Renaissance
Greater (though still limited) awareness between civilisations
✅
KS3 key idea: Historians often note the irony that the Crusades, launched as religious warfare, ended up accelerating one of history's greatest peaceful exchanges of ideas — showing that historical consequences can be very different from a conflict's original intentions.
