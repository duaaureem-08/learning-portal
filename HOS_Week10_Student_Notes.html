📖 David — A Heart After God

1 Samuel 16-17, 2 Samuel 11-12, Psalms · Shepherd to King · Repentance and Worship
Psalm 23:11 Samuel 16-17RepentanceKS3 Level 6

Memory Verse

"The LORD is my shepherd, I lack nothing."
Psalm 23:1 — NIV
What this means: David — who had literally been a shepherd — describes God as HIS shepherd. "I lack nothing" means his deepest needs are met by God's care, just as a well-cared-for sheep lacks nothing.

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample / Note

From Shepherd Boy to Anointed King — 1 Samuel 16

When God rejects Saul as king, Samuel is sent to Bethlehem to anoint one of Jesse's sons. Samuel expects the tallest, most impressive son — but God's key lesson is: "People look at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (16:7). David — the youngest, out tending sheep — is God's chosen king.

👀 What People Looked At

  • Height and stature
  • Birth order
  • Who was already present
  • Outward impressiveness

❤️ What God Looked At

  • The heart's condition
  • Faithfulness in a small job
  • Trust in God, not self
  • Who God had already chosen

David and Goliath — 1 Samuel 17

1
The standoff: Goliath challenges Israel for 40 days; Israel's army is paralysed with fear.
2
David's confidence: He recalls killing a lion and bear while shepherding — "the LORD... will rescue me" (17:37).
3
Refuses borrowed armour: Uses what he knows — a sling and five smooth stones.
4
Victory: "In the name of the LORD Almighty" (17:45) — one stone defeats Goliath.
Key idea: David's courage was built on remembering specific past experiences of God's faithfulness — courage grows from remembering, not forgetting.

David the Psalmist — Honest Prayers

PsalmWhat It Expresses
Psalm 23Trust — "The LORD is my shepherd."
Psalm 51Repentance — written after David's sin, asking for a "pure heart."
Psalm 34Praise — written during danger and fear.
Psalm 13Honest lament — "How long, LORD?"
Key idea: The Psalms model prayer that does not hide difficult emotions from God — David brings his full self, not just a "cleaned-up" version.

David's Failure and Repentance — 2 Samuel 11-12

David, now king, takes Bathsheba (another man's wife) and arranges her husband Uriah's death to hide it. The prophet Nathan confronts him with a story about a stolen lamb — then says "You are that man!" (12:7). David confesses honestly: "I have sinned against the LORD" (12:13). Consequences remain, but the relationship is restored.

The "heart after God" idea: David is called this (1 Samuel 13:14) not because he was perfect, but because of HOW he responded when confronted: honestly, without excuses, genuinely turning back to God.

4. How did David respond when Nathan confronted him about his sin? Why does this matter?
5. What does "a man after God's own heart" mean, given David's serious mistakes?

✏️ Poetry — Forms & Devices

Rhyme, Rhythm, Alliteration, Onomatopoeia · Reading and Writing Simple Poems
Rhyme & RhythmPoetic DevicesKS3 Level 6

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample
rhymeWords ending with the same or similar sounds."Cat" and "hat."
rhythmThe pattern of beats/stressed syllables in a line.A musical pulse running through words.
alliterationRepeating the SAME beginning consonant sound."Peter Piper picked a peck."
onomatopoeiaA word that sounds like the noise it describes."Buzz," "crash," "sizzle."
simileA comparison using "like" or "as.""Brave as a lion."
metaphorA comparison WITHOUT "like" or "as" — one thing IS another."The LORD is my shepherd."
stanzaA group of lines, like a paragraph in prose.

Rhyme Schemes

SchemePattern
AABBRhyming couplets — pairs of lines rhyme.
ABABAlternating rhyme — lines 1&3 rhyme, 2&4 rhyme.
ABCBOnly lines 2 and 4 rhyme — common in ballads.
Free verseNo fixed rhyme scheme at all.
ESL tip: Rhyme is NOT required to be a poem! Much of the Bible's poetry (the Psalms) does not rhyme in the original Hebrew — it uses rhythm and repetition instead.

Rhythm — The Musical Pulse

"Twinkle, twinkle, little star,
How I wonder what you are."

Read aloud and clap on the naturally emphasised syllables — that clapping pattern is the rhythm (called "metre").

Sound Devices

DeviceWhat It DoesExample
AlliterationRepeats beginning consonant sound."The wild wind whipped and wailed."
OnomatopoeiaWord imitates the sound it describes."The kettle hissed."
RepetitionRepeats a word/phrase for emphasis."Be strong and courageous..." (Joshua 1)

Simile vs Metaphor

Simile — uses "like"/"as"

  • "Brave as a lion."
  • "Eyes shone like diamonds."

Metaphor — no linking word

  • "The LORD is my shepherd."
  • "Time is a thief."
Key idea: Metaphors are often more powerful — they collapse the distance between two things entirely.

4. Write a short 4-line poem using at least one example of alliteration and one simile or metaphor.
5. Why might a poet choose free verse instead of a strict rhyme scheme?

🧮 Coordinates & the Number Plane

Four Quadrants · Plotting Coordinates · Reflections
CoordinatesReflectionsKS3 Level 6

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample
coordinateA pair of numbers (x,y) describing an exact position.(3,5)
x-axisThe HORIZONTAL number line.Left to right.
y-axisThe VERTICAL number line.Up and down.
originWhere the axes cross.(0, 0)
quadrantOne of 4 sections the axes create.Numbered 1-4 anticlockwise from top-right.
reflectionFlipping a point over a line (mirror image).

Plotting a Coordinate

Golden rule — "along the corridor, up the stairs": Always move along the x-axis FIRST, then up/down the y-axis SECOND. (3,5) ≠ (5,3)!

CoordinateHow to Plot It
(4, 2)4 right, then 2 up.
(−3, 5)3 LEFT, then 5 up.
(0, 6)Straight up 6 (stays on y-axis).
(−2, −4)2 left, then 4 DOWN.

The Four Quadrants

QuadrantxyExample
1 (top-right)++(3, 4)
2 (top-left)−+(−3, 4)
3 (bottom-left)−−(−3, −4)
4 (bottom-right)+−(3, −4)
Common mistake: Quadrants number ANTICLOCKWISE from top-right, not clockwise!

Reflections

Reflect over X-AXIS: flip sign of y. (3,5) → (3,−5)
Reflect over Y-AXIS: flip sign of x. (3,5) → (−3,5)
Reflect through ORIGIN: flip both signs. (3,5) → (−3,−5)
Key idea: A reflection is a mirror image — distance from the axis stays exactly the same on both sides.

Practice Questions

1. Plot (or describe how to plot) the points (5,3), (−2,4), (−3,−1), (4,−2).
2. Which quadrant does (−5, −2) lie in?
3. Reflect the point (6, 3) over the x-axis. Give the new coordinates.
4. Reflect the point (−4, 7) over the y-axis. Give the new coordinates.
5. If a point lies exactly on the x-axis, what must be true about its y-coordinate?

🔬 Physical vs Chemical Changes

Signs of a Chemical Reaction · Reversible vs Irreversible Changes
ChemistryChemical ReactionsKS3 Level 6

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample
physical changeChange in form/appearance only — no new substance.Melting ice.
chemical changeCreates a NEW substance with different properties.Burning wood.
reversibleCan be changed back to the original.Most physical changes.
irreversibleCannot be changed back.Most chemical changes.
precipitateA solid that forms and separates from a liquid.Sign of a chemical reaction.
exothermicReleases heat — gets warmer.Burning.
endothermicAbsorbs heat — gets colder.Some dissolving.

Physical vs Chemical — The Core Difference

🔄 Physical Change

  • No new substance formed
  • Usually reversible
  • Only shape/size/state changes
  • e.g. melting ice, tearing paper, dissolving sugar

⚗️ Chemical Change

  • New substance formed, new properties
  • Usually irreversible
  • Bonds break and form
  • e.g. burning wood, rusting iron, baking

Five Signs of a Chemical Reaction

1
Colour change: Unexpected colour shift, e.g. iron rusting.
2
Gas produced: Bubbles/fizzing, especially without heat.
3
Temperature change: Gets hotter or colder without external cause.
4
Precipitate forms: A solid appears in previously clear liquid.
5
Light or smell: A flame, glow, or new smell appears.
Careful: Bubbles alone don't always mean a chemical change — boiling water bubbles too, but that's physical (state change).

Sorting Examples

ExampleTypeWhy
Ice meltingPhysicalStill H₂O, reversible by freezing.
Iron rustingChemicalForms new substance — iron oxide.
Cutting paperPhysicalStill paper, just smaller.
Burning paperChemicalForms ash, smoke — irreversible.
Dissolving saltPhysicalSalt is still salt, recoverable by evaporation.

Practice Questions

1. Explain the key difference between a physical and chemical change.
2. List all five signs that a chemical reaction has occurred.
3. Sort these into physical or chemical: boiling water, baking a cake, tearing fabric, milk turning sour.
4. Explain the difference between exothermic and endothermic, with one example of each.
5. Why is bubbling NOT always a reliable sign of a chemical reaction? Give an example.

🏛️ The Crusades & Cultural Exchange

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample

What Were the Crusades? — Causes

In 1095, Pope Urban II called on Christians to reclaim Jerusalem from Muslim control, launching the First Crusade — about 200 years of intermittent religious warfare followed.

CauseExplanation
Religious motivationJerusalem sacred to Christianity — recapturing it framed as holy duty.
Church encouragementPope promised forgiveness of sins to those who fought.
Protecting pilgrimsChristian pilgrims reported danger under Muslim control.
Political/economic motivesKnights sought land, wealth, glory.

Timeline — Key Crusades

CrusadeDateOutcome
First1096-1099Captured Jerusalem — Crusader states established.
Second1147-1149Largely unsuccessful.
Third1189-1192Richard the Lionheart vs Saladin — Jerusalem stayed Muslim, truce for pilgrims.
Later1200sCrusader states lost entirely by 1291.

What Europe Gained — Cultural Exchange

AreaWhat Europe Gained
MathematicsAlgebra and Hindu-Arabic numerals (0-9) replacing Roman numerals.
MedicineAdvanced hospitals, surgery, pharmacology.
Preserved knowledgeIslamic scholars preserved lost Greek/Roman texts.
Trade goodsSpices, silk, sugar.
Architecture/navigationNew building techniques, the astrolabe.
Key idea: Though the Crusades largely failed militarily, contact between civilisations triggered one of history's greatest knowledge transfers — arguably more significant than the wars themselves.
