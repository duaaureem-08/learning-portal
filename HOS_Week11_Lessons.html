⭐ Memory Verse
"Then I heard the voice of the Lord saying, 'Whom shall I send? And who will go for us?' And I said, 'Here am I. Send me!'"
Isaiah 6:8 — NIV
🧠
Word-by-word: This is Isaiah's response immediately AFTER seeing a vision of God's holiness and being cleansed of his own sin (Isaiah 6:1-7). "Here am I. Send me!" is not a hesitant or reluctant answer — it is immediate and willing, coming directly out of an encounter with God rather than a sense of personal ambition or duty alone.
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Learn these words before you continue
prophet
A person chosen by God to deliver his message to people — often calling them back to faithfulness, warning of consequences, or announcing hope for the future.
idolatry
Worshipping a false god (an idol) instead of, or alongside, the true God. Elijah confronted Israel's widespread idolatry involving the god Baal.
repentance
Genuinely turning away from wrongdoing and turning back to God — a central message of nearly every prophet.
vision
A powerful, often symbolic experience in which God reveals something to a person, sometimes while awake, sometimes like a dream. Isaiah received a vision of God in the temple (Isaiah 6).
holiness / holy
Being set apart, pure, and completely different from ordinary or sinful things. The angels in Isaiah's vision cry "Holy, holy, holy" about God.
exile
Being forced to leave one's home country, often as a punishment or consequence. Isaiah's prophecies address a nation eventually facing exile.
covenant
A serious, binding agreement between God and his people. Prophets often called Israel back to the covenant obligations they had broken.
Messiah
A promised future rescuer/king. Isaiah's prophecies include some of the clearest Old Testament descriptions of a coming Messiah.
📯 Why Did God Send Prophets?
📍
After David (Week 10), Israel's kings grew increasingly unfaithful — many led the nation into idolatry and injustice. Rather than abandoning his people, God repeatedly sent prophets to speak his truth directly into these situations.
Purpose of a ProphetExplanation
Call people back to GodProphets confronted idolatry and injustice, calling Israel to repentance and covenant faithfulness.
Warn of consequencesProphets often warned that continued disobedience would bring real, painful consequences (such as exile).
Offer hope for the futureEven in warning, prophets frequently included messages of restoration and hope — God's judgment was never the final word.
Speak God's message directlyProphets claimed to speak not their own opinions but words directly from God — often introduced with "This is what the LORD says."
⚡ Elijah and the Prophets of Baal — 1 Kings 18
⚠️
King Ahab and Queen Jezebel had led Israel deep into worship of Baal, a Canaanite storm-and-fertility god. Elijah confronts this directly with a dramatic public test on Mount Carmel.
1
The challenge — "How long will you waver?"
Elijah confronts Israel: "How long will you waver between two opinions? If the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal is God, follow him" (18:21). Israel says nothing — showing their confused, divided loyalty.
2
The contest — fire from heaven
450 prophets of Baal and Elijah alone each prepare an altar. Whichever God answers by fire is the true God. Baal's prophets cry out for hours — nothing happens. Elijah prays a short prayer — fire falls instantly, consuming the offering, the wood, and even the stones.
3
The people's response
Seeing this, the people fall on their faces and declare "The LORD — he is God! The LORD — he is God!" (18:39) — a dramatic public turning point.
🌬️ Elijah's Despair and God's Gentle Whisper — 1 Kings 19
🔑
Surprising turn: Immediately after this dramatic public victory, Elijah flees in fear from Jezebel's death threat and falls into deep despair, asking God to let him die (19:4). Even a prophet who has just witnessed fire fall from heaven can experience genuine exhaustion, fear, and hopelessness.
What HappensWhat It Shows
God sends an angel with food and rest (19:5-8)God's first response to Elijah's despair is practical care — physical rest and food — not correction or a lecture.
God is not in the wind, earthquake, or fire (19:11-12)Dramatic, powerful displays (like the fire on Carmel) are not the only or even the primary way God speaks.
God speaks in a "gentle whisper" (19:12)God meets Elijah's genuine exhaustion with quiet, gentle communication rather than more spectacle.
✅
KS3 key idea: Even the most faithful, spiritually powerful people can experience genuine burnout and despair — and God's response models compassionate care alongside truth, not simply more demands.
👁️ Isaiah's Vision and Calling — Isaiah 6
Step 1
Isaiah sees God's holiness
Isaiah has an overwhelming vision of God on his throne, with angels crying "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD Almighty" (6:3).
Step 2
Isaiah recognises his own unworthiness
Confronted with God's holiness, Isaiah cries "Woe to me!... I am a man of unclean lips" (6:5) — seeing God clearly reveals his own need.
Step 3
Isaiah is cleansed
An angel touches Isaiah's mouth with a burning coal, declaring his sin "atoned for" (6:7) — cleansing comes BEFORE the calling.
Step 4
Isaiah volunteers
Hearing God ask "Whom shall I send?", Isaiah responds immediately and willingly: "Here am I. Send me!" (6:8).
✅
KS3 key idea: The order matters — Isaiah's willingness to be sent comes AFTER encountering God's holiness and being cleansed, not before. Genuine willingness to serve often follows genuine encounter, not the other way around.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these terms for accurate, clear writing
pronoun
A word that REPLACES a noun to avoid repeating it. Examples: he, she, it, they, this, those.
antecedent
The noun that a pronoun refers back to. In "Sara lost her keys," "Sara" is the antecedent of "her."
agreement
A pronoun must MATCH its antecedent in number (singular/plural) and gender. "The dog wagged ITS tail" (not "their").
singular
Referring to ONE person or thing. Pronouns: he, she, it.
plural
Referring to MORE THAN ONE person or thing. Pronoun: they.
vague reference
When a pronoun's antecedent is unclear or ambiguous — the reader cannot tell exactly what/who it refers to.
indefinite pronoun
A pronoun that doesn't refer to one specific person/thing. Examples: everyone, someone, each, nobody — most are treated as SINGULAR.
🔗 Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement — The Golden Rule
🔑
Golden rule: A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in NUMBER (singular or plural) and, where relevant, GENDER. If the antecedent is singular, the pronoun must be singular. If plural, the pronoun must be plural.
he / him / his
singular, male
she / her / hers
singular, female
it / its
singular, object/animal/idea
they / them / their
plural (or singular gender-neutral)
❌ Incorrect✅ CorrectWhy
"Each student must bring their own book" — sometimes debated"Each student must bring his or her own book" (formal) OR "Each student must bring their own book" (accepted informally/gender-neutral)"Each" is technically singular; formal writing traditionally prefers "his or her," though "their" as gender-neutral singular is now widely accepted.
"The team celebrated their win.""The team celebrated its win." (UK formal) / "The team celebrated their win." (common in US and informal UK)Collective nouns like "team" can be treated as singular (UK formal) or plural (common usage) — consistency matters most.
"Neither of the boys brought their homework.""Neither of the boys brought his homework.""Neither" is singular — treats each boy individually, not as a group.
⚠️ Vague Pronoun Reference — The Hidden Trap
⚠️
A "vague reference" happens when a reader CANNOT be sure exactly what a pronoun refers to — often because there are two or more possible antecedents, or no clear antecedent at all.
❌ Vague✅ ClearWhy the fix works
"When Sara spoke to Amy, she seemed upset.""When Sara spoke to Amy, Amy seemed upset." (or "Sara seemed upset," depending on meaning)"She" could mean EITHER Sara or Amy — the reader cannot tell which. Naming the person directly removes the ambiguity.
"I love reading and writing, which is my favourite subject.""I love reading and writing; English is my favourite subject.""Which" has no clear single antecedent — "reading and writing" together aren't one "subject." Naming it directly fixes the vagueness.
"They say it's going to rain.""The weather forecaster says it's going to rain.""They" refers to no specific, named person — this is a common but imprecise habit in informal speech.
🔍 Finding the Antecedent — A Step-by-Step Method
1
Identify the pronoun
Find the pronoun in question (he, she, it, they, this, etc.).
2
Look BACKWARD in the sentence/paragraph
Search the text immediately before the pronoun for the noun it most likely refers to.
3
Check for MORE THAN ONE possible match
If more than one noun could fit, the sentence has a vague reference problem and needs rewriting.
4
Check agreement
Confirm the pronoun matches the antecedent in number and gender.
📝 Worked Example — Fixing a Vague Sentence
[ORIGINAL — VAGUE]"Elijah told the king that he was wrong." (Who is "he" — Elijah or the king? Genuinely unclear!)

[FIXED — CLEAR]"Elijah told the king that the king was wrong." OR "Elijah told the king, 'You are wrong.'"

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Builds on Week 10 — coordinates already used negative numbers!
positive number
A number greater than zero. Usually written without a sign: 5 means +5.
negative number
A number LESS than zero, written with a minus sign. Example: −5.
integer
A whole number that can be positive, negative, or zero (no fractions or decimals). Example: −3, 0, 7.
number line
A line showing numbers in order, with negative numbers to the left of zero and positive numbers to the right.
absolute value
The DISTANCE a number is from zero, always positive. The absolute value of −7 is 7.
📏 Ordering Negative Numbers on a Number Line
🔑
Golden rule: On a number line, numbers get LARGER as you move RIGHT and SMALLER as you move LEFT — even with negative numbers. −2 is GREATER than −5, because −2 is further to the right.

⚠️
Common mistake: Students often think −8 is "bigger" than −3 because 8 is bigger than 3. But with negatives, the OPPOSITE is true: −3 > −8, because −3 is closer to zero (further right on the number line).
➕➖ Adding and Subtracting Negative Numbers
Rule 1: Adding a negative = subtracting
5 + (−3) = 5 − 3 = 2

Rule 2: Subtracting a negative = adding
5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8

Rule 3: Starting negative, adding positive — move RIGHT on the number line
−4 + 6 = 2

Rule 4: Starting negative, subtracting more — move further LEFT (more negative)
−4 − 3 = −7
✅
KS3 memory trick: "Two minuses make a plus" — subtracting a negative number is the SAME as adding a positive number. Always rewrite double signs first: "− (−3)" becomes "+3" before calculating.

🌍 Negative Numbers in Real Life
1
Temperature
−5°C means 5 degrees BELOW freezing (0°C). A rise from −5°C to 3°C is a change of 8 degrees.
2
Bank balances
A balance of −£20 means you OWE £20 (overdrawn). Depositing £30 brings the balance to +£10.
3
Elevation / sea level
−400m means 400 metres BELOW sea level (like the Dead Sea). Mount Everest is roughly +8,849m.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Science Guide
Builds on Week 10 — chemical changes and reactions
acid
A substance with a pH less than 7 — sour-tasting, can react with metals. Examples: lemon juice, vinegar, stomach acid.
alkali
A soluble base — a substance with a pH greater than 7, often feels soapy/slippery. Examples: soap, baking soda, oven cleaner.
base
A broader category than alkali — any substance that can neutralise an acid. All alkalis are bases; not all bases are alkalis (some don't dissolve in water).
pH scale
A scale from 0 to 14 measuring how acidic or alkaline a substance is. 7 = neutral. Below 7 = acidic. Above 7 = alkaline.
indicator
A substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acid or alkali — used to test pH. Example: litmus paper, universal indicator.
neutralisation
A chemical reaction between an acid and a base/alkali that cancels out their acidic/alkaline properties, typically producing a salt and water.
corrosive
Able to damage or destroy materials (like metal or skin) through chemical reaction. Strong acids and strong alkalis can both be corrosive.
📊 The pH Scale — From 0 to 14
🔑
Golden rule: The pH scale runs from 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most alkaline), with 7 exactly in the middle being NEUTRAL (neither acidic nor alkaline) — pure water has a pH of 7.

⚗️ Neutralisation — Acid + Base = Salt + Water
General word equation:
ACID + BASE → SALT + WATER

Specific example:
Hydrochloric acid + Sodium hydroxide → Sodium chloride + Water
(HCl) + (NaOH) → (NaCl) + (H₂O)
💡
Real-world example: Farmers add alkaline lime to acidic soil to neutralise it, making the soil better for growing crops. Toothpaste is often slightly alkaline to neutralise acids produced by bacteria in the mouth, protecting tooth enamel.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL History Guide
Builds on Week 10 — the Crusades and cultural exchange
Silk Road
A network of ancient trade routes connecting China to Europe and the Middle East, active for over 1,500 years, used for goods AND ideas.
globalisation
The process by which the world's regions become increasingly connected through trade, travel, and the exchange of ideas.
Age of Exploration
A period (roughly 15th-17th century) when European explorers sailed to previously unknown (to them) parts of the world, seeking trade routes and new territory.
colonisation
When a country establishes control over another territory and its people, often for economic or political gain.
indigenous peoples
The original inhabitants of a region, present before colonisation or settlement by outside groups.
circumnavigate
To travel all the way around something, especially the Earth, by sea.
Columbian Exchange
The widespread transfer of plants, animals, diseases, and culture between the Americas and the rest of the world following Columbus's voyages.
🐫 The Silk Road — Ancient Global Trade
💡
Active from around 130 BCE to the 1450s CE, the Silk Road was not one single road but a network of overland and sea routes connecting East Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East, and Europe.
What Moved Along the Silk RoadDetail
Silk (from China)Highly prized in Europe and the Middle East — China guarded silk production techniques as a closely-kept secret for centuries.
Spices, tea, porcelainValuable goods travelling east to west, often changing hands many times between different traders and regions.
Ideas and religionsBuddhism, Islam, and other belief systems spread significantly through Silk Road contact and trade networks.
TechnologyPapermaking and gunpowder — both Chinese inventions — spread westward via Silk Road contact.
DiseaseThe same routes that carried goods also carried diseases, including likely contributing to the spread of the Black Death (14th century).
✅
KS3 key idea: The Silk Road is one of history's earliest and clearest examples of globalisation — showing that extensive, long-distance cultural and economic connection between distant civilisations existed centuries before the term "globalisation" was invented.
⛵ The Age of Exploration — Causes and Key Figures
⚠️
By the 1400s, overland trade routes to Asia (including parts of the Silk Road) had become more difficult and expensive to use, partly due to the fall of Constantinople to the Ottoman Empire in 1453. European nations sought new SEA routes to Asia's valuable spice trade.

⚖️ Balanced View — Impact on Indigenous Peoples
⚠️
The Age of Exploration led to significant, often devastating, consequences for indigenous peoples in the Americas and elsewhere, alongside the new trade and knowledge exchange for Europeans.
ImpactDetail
DiseaseEuropean diseases (smallpox, measles) killed enormous numbers of indigenous people, who had no immunity — estimates suggest population losses of over 80-90% in some regions within decades.
Colonisation and loss of landEuropean powers claimed and settled indigenous territories, often through violent conquest, forced displacement, and broken agreements.
Forced labour and slaveryIndigenous peoples were frequently forced into labour systems; the trans-Atlantic slave trade also developed to supply labour to European colonies.
Loss of cultureIndigenous languages, religions, and traditions were frequently suppressed or destroyed through colonisation.
✅
KS3 key idea: The same events celebrated in Europe as an "Age of Exploration" and expansion of global trade had a profoundly different and often catastrophic meaning for the indigenous peoples who experienced colonisation — history often looks very different depending on whose perspective is centred.
