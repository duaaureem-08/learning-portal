📖 Moses & the Exodus

Exodus 1–15 · God Delivers His People · The Passover · Courage & Calling
Exodus 14:14Ten PlaguesPassoverKS3 Level 7

Memory Verse

"The Lord will fight for you; you need only to be still."
Exodus 14:14 — NIV

"The Lord will fight for you" = God promises to ACT on behalf of His people. "you need only to be still" = Said at the most hopeless moment — trapped between Pharaoh's army and the Red Sea — God asks for trust, not panic. Faith here means stillness, not fighting.

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample / Note

PharaohThe title of the king of ancient Egypt — absolute power over everyone.He refused to release Israel even after nine plagues.

Israel in Egypt — The Background

Joseph (Week 6) brought his family — 70 people — to Egypt during the famine. Over 430 years they became a nation of over a million. A new Pharaoh who "did not know Joseph" feared their numbers and enslaved them with forced labour. God heard their cries and remembered His covenant with Abraham.

Connection: The Exodus is the fulfilment of what God promised Abraham in Week 5 — a great nation, a land, and blessing. It took 430 years but God kept His promise.

Moses and the Burning Bush — Exodus 3–4

Who is Moses? An Israelite baby hidden in the Nile, raised as an Egyptian prince, fled to Midian after killing an Egyptian guard, worked as a shepherd for 40 years — called by God at age 80.

Moses' Five Excuses and God's Five Answers
Moses' ExcuseGod's Answer
"Who am I to go?" (Insecurity)"I will be with you." — God's presence is enough.
"What is Your name?" (Uncertainty)"I AM WHO I AM." — The eternal, self-existent name.
"What if they don't believe me?" (Fear)Three miraculous signs: staff → snake; leprous hand; water → blood.
"I am not a good speaker." (Inadequacy)"Who gave humans mouths? I will help you speak."
"Please send someone else." (Reluctance)Aaron (his brother) will speak for him. God does not give up.
KS3 key idea: Moses argued with God five times before saying yes. Calling does not require instant courage — it requires eventually saying yes, even feeling unqualified.

Each plague targeted something Egypt worshipped or relied on, showing God's power over every Egyptian god. The Israelites in Goshen were protected from plagues 4–10 — a visible sign that God was distinguishing His people.

The Passover — Exodus 12

The Israelites were given specific instructions for the night of the tenth plague. Every detail carries meaning, and Jewish people still celebrate Passover today. Christians connect the Passover to Jesus — "the Lamb of God."

🐑
A perfect lamb without defect — selected and kept for four days. Represents something of full, uncorrupted value.
🩸
Blood on the two doorposts and top of the door frame — when God saw the blood, He "passed over" that home. The blood, not the family's merit, was the protection.
🌿
Bitter herbs and unleavened bread — bitter herbs = remembering the bitterness of slavery. No yeast = no time for dough to rise. They were leaving in a hurry.
👟
Sandals on, staff in hand, eating in a hurry (Exodus 12:11) — faith looked like readiness. They were packed before they left.
Cross-reference: Jesus held His "Last Supper" during the Passover meal and deliberately connected His death to the Passover lamb — "This is my blood of the new covenant." He is called "the Lamb of God" throughout the New Testament.

The Red Sea Crossing — Exodus 14

After releasing Israel, Pharaoh changed his mind and sent 600 chariots. The Israelites were trapped — army behind them, Red Sea in front. Moses declared Exodus 14:14. Then God acted:

1
The pillar of cloud moved to stand between Israel and the army — giving the Egyptians darkness and Israel light.
2
Moses stretched out his hand — a strong east wind blew all night and the sea divided. The Israelites walked through on dry ground.
3
The Egyptians followed into the sea, but God threw them into confusion — wheels fell off chariots. Moses stretched out his hand again and the sea returned, drowning the entire army.
4
The Song of Moses (Exodus 15) — Israel's response was to sing. "The Lord is my strength and my song; he has become my salvation." Deliverance → praise.

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample
non-fictionWriting about real events, real people, and real facts — not made up.Articles, reports, biographies, speeches, textbooks.
purposeWHY the text was written — what the writer is trying to DO.To inform, persuade, instruct, explain, or entertain.
audienceWHO the text was written FOR — the intended readers.Adults, children, experts, general public.

Purpose — WHY Was This Written?

Most non-fiction has MORE THAN ONE purpose. Identify the PRIMARY (main) purpose and any secondary ones.

PurposeClues to Look For
To PersuadeOne-sided arguments, emotional language, opinions presented as facts, rhetorical questions, repetition
To InformStatistics, dates, factual statements, neutral balanced language, no obvious preference
To ExplainSimple vocabulary, step-by-step structure, definitions, diagrams, second-person address
To EntertainHumour, vivid descriptions, personal anecdotes, witty or informal tone

Audience — WHO Is This Written For?

Infer the audience from textual clues — it is rarely stated directly.

Audience ClueWhat It Tells You
Vocabulary levelSimple = non-specialist/younger. Technical jargon without definitions = expert audience.
Formal vs informal toneFormal = professional/older. Informal/conversational = younger/general public.
What is NOT explainedIf basic concepts are assumed, the audience already knows them.
Use of "you" vs "one""You" = direct and informal. "One" = very formal.
Publication typeAcademic journal vs tabloid newspaper vs children's magazine.

Fact vs Opinion vs Inference

✅ FACT

  • Can be checked — true or false
  • "Only 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled"
  • Contains specific numbers, dates, names
  • Can be verified against evidence

💬 OPINION

  • Personal view — cannot be proven
  • "Plastic is the world's most serious crisis"
  • Look for: "I believe," "should," "obviously," "clearly"
  • Even experts' judgements can be opinions
Inference: A conclusion you work out from evidence — not directly stated. Always show the evidence from the text that led to your inference. e.g. "Sales rose 40%" → inference: public interest is growing.
Spotting opinion disguised as fact: Look for value-loaded adjectives ("devastating," "brilliant"), blame or praise without evidence, and statistics without named sources. "Studies show..." without naming the study is a warning sign.

Language Features — What to Look For and Why

FeatureWhat It IsEffect on Reader
Rhetorical questionA question that does not expect an answerMakes reader feel the "obvious" answer supports the writer's view
StatisticA specific number or percentage used as evidenceMakes the argument seem objective — but check the source!
Expert quotationA direct quote from someone with authorityAdds credibility — "it's not just my opinion"
Emotive languageWords chosen to create an emotional reactionMakes reader feel sympathy, outrage, or hope — more likely to agree
Direct address ("you")Speaking directly to the readerCreates personal connection; makes reader feel responsible
Rule of threeThree examples or adjectives used togetherCreates rhythm and emphasis — feels more convincing
AnecdoteA short personal story used as an exampleMakes abstract issues feel real and human
Exam tip: Always explain WHY the writer chose a feature — what effect does it have on the reader? Never just name it. Full answer = feature + effect + how it serves the purpose.

Structure of a Non-Fiction Text

1
Headline/Subheadings — Read these first to get the outline quickly.
2
Opening paragraph — In news: most important info first (WHO, WHAT, WHERE, WHEN, WHY, HOW). In features/opinion: a dramatic hook or anecdote.
3
Body paragraphs — Each develops ONE point. First sentence of each paragraph usually states the main point of that paragraph.
4
Images and captions — Not decoration! Captions sometimes contain information NOT in the main text. Always read them.
5
Conclusion — What the writer most wants you to take away. Often: a call to action, a restatement of the main argument, or a look ahead.

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample
ratioCompares two or more quantities — parts to parts. Written with a colon.3 boys, 2 girls → ratio 3:2 (no units)
proportionCompares a PART to the WHOLE — what fraction of the total.3 boys in 5 total → proportion = 3/5
simplify a ratioDivide all parts by their HCF to get the simplest form.12:18 ÷ 6 = 2:3

inverse proportionAs one quantity increases, the other DECREASES.More workers = fewer days to finish a job.

Ratio vs Proportion — The Key Difference

⚖️ RATIO — Part to Part

  • Compares one group against ANOTHER group
  • 3 red, 5 blue → ratio = 3:5
  • Order matters: red:blue ≠ blue:red
  • No units — just a relationship

📊 PROPORTION — Part to Whole

  • Compares one group against the TOTAL
  • 3 red in 8 total → proportion = 3/8
  • Can be written as fraction, decimal, or %
  • Link to Week 6: 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%

Simplifying Ratios — Step by Step

1
Write the ratio clearly in the correct order as given in the question.
2
Find the HCF (Highest Common Factor) of all parts.
3
Divide every part by the HCF.
4
Check: can you divide any further? If no — done.
8:12 → HCF = 4 → 2:3
15:25 → HCF = 5 → 3:5
24:36:48 → HCF = 12 → 2:3:4
⚠️ Different units — always convert first! "3cm : 1.5m" → convert: "3cm : 150cm" → simplify: 1:50. This is a common exam trap.

Sharing in a Ratio — The 3-Step Method

Always use these 3 steps: (1) Total parts. (2) Value of ONE part. (3) Multiply for each share.
Worked Example 1 — Money

Share £60 between Aisha and Ben in ratio 2:3.

Step 1: Total parts = 2 + 3 = 5

Step 2: 1 part = £60 ÷ 5 = £12

Step 3: Aisha = 2 × £12 = £24 · Ben = 3 × £12 = £36

Check: £24 + £36 = £60 ✓

Worked Example 2 — When You Know One Person's Share

Tom and Sam share sweets in ratio 3:5. Tom gets 24. How many does Sam get?

Tom = 3 parts = 24 → 1 part = 24 ÷ 3 = 8

Sam = 5 × 8 = 40 sweets · Total = 24 + 40 = 64

The Unitary Method — Find ONE, Then Scale

1
Identify the two quantities and what you know vs what you need.
2
Divide to find the value of ONE unit — this gives you the "per one" rate.
3
Multiply by the required number of units. Always include the unit in your answer.
Example 1: 4 books cost £22. Cost of 7 books?
1 book = £22 ÷ 4 = £5.50
7 books = £5.50 × 7 = £38.50

Example 2: Recipe uses 300g flour for 12 biscuits. How much for 20?
1 biscuit = 300 ÷ 12 = 25g
20 biscuits = 25 × 20 = 500g
⚠️ Inverse proportion: "3 workers take 8 days. How long for 6 workers?" More workers = FEWER days. Total work = 3 × 8 = 24 worker-days. 6 workers: 24 ÷ 6 = 4 days. Always identify which type you have before solving.

The Three States of Matter — Comparison

🧊 SOLID

Movement: Vibrate on the spot only
Shape: Fixed ✅
Volume: Fixed ✅
Compressible: No ❌

Movement: Slide past each other

Key principle: The STATE of matter depends on how far apart particles are and how fast they move. Temperature controls BOTH of these. Higher temperature = faster particles = more likely to be liquid or gas.

Evaporation vs Boiling: Evaporation = slow, surface only, any temperature below boiling. Boiling = rapid, throughout liquid, only at the boiling point (100°C for water).

Heating Curve for Water — Reading the Graph

What Each Section of a Heating Curve Means
SectionWhat Is HappeningTemperature
A — Rising lineSolid (ice) heating up — particles vibrate fasterBelow 0°C, rising

⭐ The most important exam point: A FLAT LINE on a heating curve means a CHANGE OF STATE is happening at that temperature. Temperature does NOT change during a change of state because all energy goes into breaking bonds (latent heat), not raising temperature.

The Particle Model — Strengths and Limitations

✅ What It Explains Well

  • Why solids keep their shape
  • Why liquids flow but take up fixed volume
  • Why gases expand to fill containers
  • Why gases can be compressed
  • Why heating raises temperature
  • Why changes of state happen at fixed temperatures

⚠️ Limitations

  • Treats all particles as identical spheres — not accurate
  • Does not show forces between particles
  • Does not explain why some substances dissolve in others
  • Cannot explain plasma (the 4th state of matter — found in stars)
  • Ignores quantum mechanical behaviour
KS3 key idea: A model having limitations does not mean it is useless — like a map of your school. The particle model explains most observable behaviour of matter well. We use it because it works.

🏛️ Climate & Biomes

Key Vocabulary

WordWhat It MeansExample
weatherDay-to-day atmospheric conditions — temperature, rainfall, wind on a specific day."It is raining in Lahore today."

permafrostA layer of permanently frozen soil beneath the tundra surface — prevents tree growth.Found across Arctic tundra in Canada, Russia.

Weather vs Climate — The Most Important Distinction

🌩️ WEATHER — Short Term

  • Changes day to day — even hour to hour
  • "It is hot and sunny in Rawalpindi today"
  • Difficult to predict beyond 10 days
  • One unusual week does NOT change a climate description

🌡️ CLIMATE — Long Term

  • Average weather patterns over 30+ years
  • "Pakistan has hot summers and monsoon rains in July–September"
  • Used to design buildings, plan farming, classify biomes
  • Climate CHANGE = long-term shifts in these patterns
Common confusion: "It was cold last winter — what happened to global warming?" One cold winter is WEATHER. Global warming is a CLIMATE trend measured over decades. These operate on completely different timescales.
Memory phrase: "Climate is what you EXPECT; weather is what you GET."

The Major Biomes — Summary

🌧️ Tropical Rainforest
Climate: Hot (25–30°C), wet year-round, 2,000–4,000mm rain.
Location: Amazon, Congo, SE Asia — near equator.
Features: Four vegetation layers (emergent, canopy, understorey, floor). 80% of land species in 6% of land area.
Threat: Deforestation — loss of habitat and carbon store.
🦁 Savannah
Climate: Warm year-round with distinct wet and dry seasons. 500–1,500mm rain/year, mostly in wet season.
Location: Sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Brazil, northern Australia.
Features: Grasses + scattered trees (acacia, baobab). Fire-resistant. Africa's largest wildlife migrations.
Animals: Lions, elephants, zebras, wildebeest, cheetahs.
🏜️ Hot Desert
Climate: Defined by LOW RAINFALL (under 250mm) — NOT necessarily temperature. Day: up to 50°C. Night: near 0°C.
Location: Sahara, Arabian Desert, Atacama (driest), Australian Outback.
Adaptations: Cacti store water in stems; animals mostly nocturnal; camels store fat.
🍂 Temperate Forest
Climate: 4 distinct seasons. 5–20°C. ~800mm rain year-round.
Location: UK, Western Europe, eastern USA, Japan.
Features: DECIDUOUS trees (shed leaves in autumn to survive winter cold and frozen water).
Animals: Foxes, deer, owls, squirrels. Many hibernate or migrate.
🌲 Taiga / Boreal Forest
Climate: Very cold winters (−20 to −40°C), short cool summers. Largest land biome on Earth.
Location: Canada, Russia (Siberia), Scandinavia.
Features: CONIFEROUS (evergreen) trees with needles — adapted for cold. Wolves, bears, moose, lynx.
🧊 Tundra / Polar
Climate: Extremely cold year-round. Very low precipitation (cold desert). 24-hour daylight in summer; darkness in winter.
Features: PERMAFROST below surface — prevents drainage and tree growth → treeless. Low-growing mosses and lichens.
Animals: Polar bears, Arctic foxes, reindeer. Most have thick fur/blubber.

Factors That Affect Climate — Beyond Latitude

1
Latitude — most important. Near equator: sun's rays hit directly (concentrated, hot). Near poles: rays hit at shallow angle (spread out, cold).
2
Altitude — higher = colder. Temperature drops ~6.5°C per 1,000m. Explains snow on tropical mountains (Kilimanjaro, in Tanzania, near the equator).
3
Distance from sea — maritime (near sea) = mild, wet. Continental (far from sea) = extremes. Compare London vs Moscow (same latitude, very different climates).
4
Ocean currents — warm currents (like the Gulf Stream) heat nearby land. The Gulf Stream makes the UK much warmer than its latitude would suggest.
5
Prevailing winds — winds from the sea bring moisture; winds from land/desert bring dry air. Rain shadow effect: one side of a mountain = wet; the other = dry.

Cross-subject link (Science Week 6): Each biome is home to a specific FOOD WEB. The climate determines what producers (plants) grow; the plants determine what consumers (animals) can survive. Climate → biome → food web. These systems are connected.
