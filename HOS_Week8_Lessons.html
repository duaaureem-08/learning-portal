📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Learn these words before you continue
commandment
A rule or law given by God. The word means a strong instruction — not just advice, but a command. (Hebrew: mitzvah)
covenant
A serious, binding agreement between God and people. Like a treaty between a king and his people. The commandments are given inside God's covenant with Israel.
idolatry
Worshipping an idol — a statue or image — instead of the real God. Or treating anything as more important than God. (idol = false god)
sabbath
The seventh day of the week, set aside as a rest day. God rested on the seventh day of creation (Genesis 2:2) and commands people to do the same.
honour
To show great respect and value to someone. Honouring parents means treating them with love, respect, and obedience.
covet
To want something that belongs to someone else in an unhealthy, jealous way. Coveting is about desire — even before any action is taken.
bear false witness
To tell lies about another person, especially in a legal setting. Means saying untrue things that hurt someone's reputation.
moral law
Rules about right and wrong behaviour that come from God's character, not just from a government. The Ten Commandments are an example of moral law.
📍 Where and Why — Exodus 19-20
📍
After the Exodus from Egypt (Week 7), Israel camped at Mount Sinai. God called Moses up the mountain and gave him the Ten Commandments — written by God's own hand on two stone tablets (Exodus 31:18). This was three months after leaving Egypt.
1
God introduces himself (Exodus 20:2)
"I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt, out of the land of slavery." Before giving the law, God reminds Israel of what he has already done — the commandments come AFTER grace, not before it.
2
Two tablets — two categories
Commandments 1–4 concern the relationship with God (the first tablet). Commandments 5–10 concern relationships with other people (the second tablet). Both matter equally — you cannot love God and hate people.
3
A guide for all of life
The commandments cover worship, time, family, life, marriage, property, truth, and desire. Together they describe what a society looks like when people live in right relationship with God and each other.
📜 The Ten Commandments — Each One Explained
Commandment 1
No other gods before God
God alone is Lord. No person, object, or idea should take the place of God in our lives. This sets God apart as unique and supreme.
Commandment 2
No idols or images
Do not worship images or statues. God cannot be reduced to something humans make with their hands. He is greater than anything visible.
Commandment 3
Do not misuse God's name
The name of God is holy — it represents who he is. Using it carelessly, disrespectfully, or as a curse dishonours God's character.
Commandment 4
Remember the Sabbath
Rest one day in seven. God built rest into creation. The Sabbath protects people from becoming slaves to work and reminds us that God provides.
Commandment 5
Honour your father and mother
The first commandment with a promised blessing (Ephesians 6:2-3). Family is the foundation of society — respecting parents builds stable communities.
Commandment 6
Do not murder
Human life has value because people are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27). Unjustly taking a life destroys what God values most.
Commandment 7
Do not commit adultery
Marriage is a covenant — a sacred, binding promise. Adultery breaks that promise and causes deep harm to people and families.
Commandment 8
Do not steal
What belongs to another person should be respected. Stealing violates both the person's rights and your own integrity as someone made in God's image.
Commandment 9
Do not give false testimony
Truth-telling is essential for community trust. Lying about someone destroys their reputation and breaks the social fabric that holds people together.
Commandment 10
Do not covet
The only commandment about thought, not action. God cares about our desires, not just our actions. Contentment — satisfaction with what you have — is the antidote to coveting.
❤️ Jesus and the Law — Matthew 22:37-40
🔑
The Golden Summary: When asked "which is the greatest commandment?", Jesus did not pick one of the ten — he summarised all ten in two: Love God with everything, and love your neighbour as yourself. "All the Law and the Prophets hang on these two commandments" (Matthew 22:40).
📖 What the Law IS
A gift from God — not a burden
A guide for living in community
A reflection of God's own character
Given AFTER rescue, not before
⚠️ What the Law is NOT
Not a way to earn God's love
Not an arbitrary set of rules
Not impossible to understand
Not only for ancient Israel

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these terms for academic writing
expository
Writing that explains or informs. It gives facts and information clearly, without the writer's personal opinion. From Latin "exponere" — to set out/explain.
thesis statement
One or two sentences at the end of the introduction that tell the reader exactly what the essay will explain. The "main point" of the whole piece.
topic sentence
The first sentence of a body paragraph. It tells the reader what that ONE paragraph is about — like a mini-thesis for each paragraph.
supporting detail
Evidence, facts, or examples that prove or explain the topic sentence. Each topic sentence needs at least 2-3 supporting details.
cohesive device
Words and phrases that connect sentences and paragraphs together smoothly. Examples: "furthermore," "however," "in addition," "as a result."
conclusion
The final paragraph. It summarises the main points (without copying them word-for-word) and gives a final thought. Never introduce new ideas in a conclusion.
audience
The person or group of people who will READ your writing. Good writers always think: who am I writing for? What do they need to know?
formal register
Writing in a professional, academic style. No slang, no contractions (don't → do not), full sentences, precise vocabulary.
🏗️ The Three-Part Structure
💡
Every informative essay has three parts. Think of it like a building: Introduction = the door (welcome the reader and show them what's inside). Body = the rooms (each paragraph is one room, one idea). Conclusion = the exit (remind the reader of what they learned before they leave).
1
Introduction — Hook + Background + Thesis
Hook: Start with something interesting — a question, a surprising fact, or a powerful statement. Draw the reader in. Background: Give 2-3 sentences of context — what is this topic about? Thesis: State the main point of your essay in 1-2 clear sentences.
2
Body Paragraphs — One Idea Per Paragraph
Topic sentence (what this paragraph is about) → supporting detail 1 → supporting detail 2 → supporting detail 3 → linking sentence (connects to the next paragraph). For a KS3 essay, 2-4 body paragraphs is standard.
3
Conclusion — Summarise + Final Thought
Restate your thesis in new words (do NOT copy it). Summarise your 2-4 main points briefly. Final thought: Why does this matter? What should the reader think or do now?
🔗 Cohesive Devices — Connecting Your Writing
⚠️
Without linking words, your sentences feel like a list. With them, your writing flows like a river. Always use a variety — never start every sentence with "Furthermore."
PurposeExamplesUse it when…
Adding informationFurthermore, In addition, Moreover, Also…you are adding another point that supports your argument.
ContrastingHowever, On the other hand, In contrast, Nevertheless…you want to show a difference or a counterpoint.
Showing cause/effectAs a result, Therefore, Consequently, This means that…one thing leads to another.
Giving examplesFor example, For instance, Such as, Including…you want to support a point with evidence.
ConcludingIn conclusion, To summarise, Overall, In short…you are ending a paragraph or the whole essay.
✍️ Writing a Strong Topic Sentence
✅
The formula: Topic sentence = SUBJECT + WHAT YOU WILL SAY ABOUT IT. It must be specific enough to guide the paragraph but broad enough to be developed with evidence.
❌ Weak Topic Sentence✅ Strong Topic SentenceWhy it's better
"This paragraph is about the Nile.""The Nile River made early Egyptian civilisation possible by providing fertile farmland and a reliable water source."Specific claim — tells reader exactly what the paragraph will prove.
"Pollution is bad.""Air pollution from factories has significantly increased rates of respiratory illness in urban populations."Has a specific subject AND makes a clear, provable claim.
"There are many types of food chains.""Food chains in polar habitats are shorter than in rainforests because extreme cold limits producer diversity."Connects two ideas with reasoning, not just description.
📝 Worked Example — Annotated Expository Paragraph
[TOPIC SENTENCE]The Ten Commandments provided ancient Israel with a complete framework for social order. [SUPPORTING DETAIL 1]Commandments six through nine — prohibiting murder, adultery, theft, and false testimony — directly protected individuals from harm by others. [SUPPORTING DETAIL 2]Furthermore, the commandment to honour parents (number five) reinforced family loyalty, which was the basic unit of Israelite society. [COHESIVE DEVICE]As a result, a community following these laws would experience far less conflict than one without shared moral principles. [LINKING SENTENCE]This social dimension of the commandments connects closely to their theological foundation.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Algebra uses letters instead of unknown numbers
algebra
A branch of maths that uses letters (called variables) to represent unknown numbers. From Arabic "al-jabr" — meaning "reunion of broken parts."
variable
A letter that represents an unknown number. Common variables: x, y, n, a, b. Example: in "x + 3 = 7", x is the variable.
term
A single number, variable, or combination (like 3x or 5). Terms are separated by + or − signs.
expression
A group of terms combined with + or −. An expression has NO equals sign. Example: 3x + 2y − 5.
coefficient
The number IN FRONT of a variable. In 5x, the coefficient is 5. In x, the coefficient is 1 (we just don't write it).
like terms
Terms that have the SAME variable (and same power). 3x and 7x are like terms. 3x and 3y are NOT like terms — different variables.
simplify
To make an expression shorter by collecting like terms. "3x + 5x" simplifies to "8x".
substitution
Replacing a variable with a number to find the value of an expression. If x = 4, then 3x + 2 = 3(4) + 2 = 14.
💡 Why Algebra? — The Big Idea
💡
Before algebra, you can only solve problems where you know all the numbers. Algebra lets you work with UNKNOWN numbers. Instead of "I have ___ apples and add 5 more to get 12," you write: x + 5 = 12. Then you solve it.
Arithmetic
3 + 5 = 8

→

The unknown is found
✍️ Writing Algebraic Expressions
⚠️
Key writing rules: In algebra we NEVER use × between a number and a letter — write 5n not 5×n. We write the coefficient first: 3y not y3. When the coefficient is 1, we just write the letter: 1x = x.

📦 Collecting Like Terms
🔑
Golden rule: You can only add or subtract terms that have the SAME variable. Think of it like sorting fruit: you can add apples to apples and oranges to oranges, but you cannot add apples to oranges.
Example 1: Simplify 3x + 5x
3x + 5x = 8x ← same variable x, so add the coefficients: 3+5=8

Example 2: Simplify 7y − 3y
7y − 3y = 4y ← same variable y: 7−3=4

Example 3: Simplify 4a + 3b + 2a − b
4a + 2a + 3b − b = 6a + 2b ← group a-terms, then b-terms

Example 4: Simplify 5x + 3 − 2x + 7
5x − 2x + 3 + 7 = 3x + 10 ← numbers without variables are like terms too!
⚠️
Common mistake: 3x + 5 CANNOT be simplified further. x and a plain number are NOT like terms. The answer stays as 3x + 5.
🔢 Substitution — Putting Numbers Back In
💡
Once you have an expression, you can find its value by SUBSTITUTING a number for the variable. Always use brackets when substituting to avoid errors.
Find the value of 3x + 5 when x = 4
3(4) + 5 = 12 + 5 = 17

Find the value of 2a − b when a = 6, b = 3
2(6) − (3) = 12 − 3 = 9

Find the value of x² + 2x when x = 3
(3)² + 2(3) = 9 + 6 = 15

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Science Guide
Learn these chemistry terms first
mixture
Two or more substances that are mixed together but NOT chemically joined. They keep their own properties. You CAN separate them without a chemical reaction.
pure substance
A material made of only ONE type of substance — all the same. Pure water is ONLY water. Salt water is a MIXTURE (water + salt).
soluble
Able to dissolve in a liquid (usually water). Salt is soluble in water — it disappears into it. Sand is insoluble — it does not dissolve.
filtration
Separating an insoluble solid from a liquid using a filter. The liquid passes through tiny holes; the solid is left behind. Used for: sand from water.
evaporation
Heating a solution so the liquid turns to gas (vapour) and escapes, leaving the dissolved solid behind. Used for: getting salt from salt water.
distillation
Heating a mixture to boil off one liquid, collecting the vapour, and cooling it back to liquid. Used to: separate liquids or get pure water from salt water.
chromatography
Separating coloured substances in a mixture by letting them travel at different speeds through a material (like filter paper). Used for: identifying dyes or inks.
solvent / solute / solution
Solute = the solid that dissolves (e.g. salt). Solvent = the liquid it dissolves in (e.g. water). Solution = the mixture made (e.g. salt water).
🔍 Mixtures vs Pure Substances
🧪 Mixture
Two or more substances mixed together
Each substance keeps its own properties
No chemical reaction occurs
Can be separated by physical means
Examples: air, sea water, sand + salt, ink
💧 Pure Substance
Only one type of particle throughout
Always the same properties (melting/boiling point)
Cannot be separated further by physical means
Has a fixed, known melting/boiling point
Examples: pure water, pure gold, pure salt
🔬 The Four Separation Techniques
1
Filtration — separating an insoluble solid from a liquid
Equipment: filter funnel, filter paper, conical flask, beaker. How: Pour the mixture (e.g. muddy water) through filter paper. The tiny holes let the water pass through (this is called the filtrate). The solid (e.g. mud) is too big to pass through and stays in the paper (this is called the residue). Use when: the solid does NOT dissolve in the liquid.
2
Evaporation — getting a dissolved solid from a solution
Equipment: evaporating dish, Bunsen burner / heat source. How: Pour salt water into the dish and heat it. The water turns to steam and escapes into the air. The salt is left behind in the dish as white crystals. Use when: you want the SOLID, not the liquid. The water is allowed to escape.
3
Distillation — separating a liquid from a solution (collecting the liquid)
Equipment: flask, condenser (cooling tube), collection beaker. How: Heat the salt water — water boils first and turns to steam. The steam travels through a cold tube (condenser) and cools back to liquid — this is collected as pure water. The salt stays behind. Use when: you want the LIQUID (pure water) and also when separating two liquids with different boiling points (e.g. alcohol and water).
4
Chromatography — separating mixtures of coloured substances
Equipment: filter paper / chromatography paper, solvent (often water or alcohol), beaker. How: Put a spot of the ink or dye at the bottom of the paper. Place the paper in a little solvent. As the solvent moves up, it carries the different dyes at different speeds — the dyes separate into distinct coloured bands. Use when: identifying what dyes or chemicals are in a mixture (e.g. food colouring, crime scene ink).
✅
KS3 Key idea: Every separation technique uses a PHYSICAL property — particle size, boiling point, or how well a substance dissolves — not a chemical reaction. The substances are not changed, just sorted.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Geography Guide
Builds on Week 6 (physical geography) and Week 7 (climate and biomes)
settlement
A place where people live permanently. Can be a tiny hamlet of a few houses or a huge city of millions. Settlements grow where conditions are favourable.
population density
How many people live in a given area. High density = many people per km². Low density = few people spread over a large area. Often shown on a map using colour shading.
rural
Relating to the countryside — areas of farmland, forests, or villages, away from cities. Most people work in farming or land-based activities.
urban
Relating to cities and towns. High population density, many jobs in services and industry, more infrastructure (roads, shops, hospitals).
urbanisation
The process of more and more people moving FROM the countryside INTO cities. A global trend, especially in developing countries.
migration
The movement of people from one place to another to live. Internal migration = within one country. International migration = between countries.
push factors
Reasons that PUSH people AWAY from where they currently live. Examples: poverty, conflict, drought, lack of jobs.
pull factors
Reasons that PULL people TOWARDS a new place. Examples: better jobs, safety, better schools, family already there.
🗺️ Why People Settle Where They Do
💡
People throughout history have settled where their physical environment gave them the best chance of survival. The same factors that guided ancient settlements still shape modern cities today.
💧 Water Supply
Every settlement needs fresh water. Rivers, lakes, and springs were the first reason people stopped moving. All the earliest cities were built near water.
🌾 Fertile Land
Flat, fertile land allows farming. River valleys like the Nile and Tigris-Euphrates had rich soil from flooding — this is why civilisations began there.
🏔️ Natural Defence
Hills, cliffs, rivers, and coastlines could protect settlements from attack. Medieval cities were often built on high ground or surrounded by water.
🌡️ Mild Climate
Moderate temperatures, regular rainfall, and no extreme weather make an area easier to live in. Extreme environments (deserts, polar regions) have very low population density.
🚢 Transport Routes
Ports, river crossings, and road junctions attracted settlements. Trade brought wealth. Coastal cities like London and New York grew because of their harbours.
⛏️ Natural Resources
Coal mines, forests, fishing waters — places with useful resources attracted workers and grew into towns. The Industrial Revolution created new cities near coalfields.
🌆 Rural vs Urban — What's the Difference?
🌾 Rural (Countryside)
Low population density — few people per km²
Main jobs: farming, forestry, fishing
Few services (shops, hospitals may be far away)
Quieter, more natural environment
Examples: villages, small hamlets, farmland
🏙️ Urban (City/Town)
High population density — many people per km²
Main jobs: services, industry, finance, tech
Many services easily accessible
Busy, built environment
Examples: cities, large towns, suburbs
✅
KS3 key idea: Today, more than half of ALL people on Earth live in urban areas — this is a very recent change. In 1800, only about 3% of people lived in cities. Urbanisation is one of the biggest geographical changes in human history.
➡️ Migration — Push and Pull Factors
⬅️ Push Factors (reasons to LEAVE)
War, conflict, persecution
Poverty and lack of jobs
Drought, famine, natural disasters
Lack of education or healthcare
Political instability
➡️ Pull Factors (reasons to GO)
Better job opportunities and wages
Safety and political freedom
Better schools and universities
Joining family members already there
A milder or more comfortable climate
💡
ESL Tip: Refugees are forced to leave by push factors (especially violence or persecution). Economic migrants choose to leave, attracted mainly by pull factors (better wages). Both are forms of migration, but for different reasons.
