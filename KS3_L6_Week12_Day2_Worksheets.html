📚 Week 3 — The Fall, Genesis 3
Week 3
1
Summarise what happens in Genesis 3, and explain what changes for Adam and Eve as a result of their choice. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
In Genesis 3, the serpent tempts Eve to eat fruit from the one tree God had forbidden; Eve eats it and gives some to Adam, who also eats. As a result, their eyes are "opened" to their nakedness and shame, they hide from God, and God pronounces consequences: pain in childbirth, conflict between humans and the ground/work, and — significantly — separation from the garden of Eden. Trust in God's word is broken, and the harmony of the created order described in Genesis 1-2 (Week 2) is disrupted.
2
Write the memory verse Romans 3:23 from memory, and explain what it claims about ALL people, not just Adam and Eve. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." (Romans 3:23, NIV). This verse claims that sin is not limited to Adam and Eve's original choice — EVERY person, in every generation, shares in this same condition of falling short of God's standard. It universalises the Genesis 3 story: what happened in the garden is presented as describing something true of all humanity, not a one-off historical event with no ongoing relevance.
Week 4 — Noah
🌊 Week 4 — Noah: Faith & Obedience
Week 4
3
Explain what a "covenant" is, and describe the specific covenant God makes with Noah after the flood. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A covenant is a formal, binding agreement or promise — in the Bible, typically initiated by God and offered as a lasting commitment. After the flood, God promises Noah that he will never again destroy the earth by a flood, giving the rainbow as a visible sign of this covenant (Genesis 9:8-17) — a promise made to Noah, his descendants, and "every living creature," not conditional on human behaviour changing.
4
Write the memory verse Hebrews 11:7 from memory, and explain what it identifies as the key feature of Noah's faith. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
"By faith Noah, when warned about things not yet seen, in holy fear built an ark to save his family." (Hebrews 11:7, NIV). This verse identifies the key feature of Noah's faith as trusting and acting on a warning about "things not yet seen" — Noah built the ark before there was any visible evidence of coming rain or flood, showing that faith means trusting God's word ahead of physical proof.
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Explain the connection between the Fall (Genesis 3, Week 3) and Noah's story (Week 4). Why might the flood narrative be understood as God's response to the spread of sin described after Genesis 3? Write at least 5 sentences. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The Fall in Genesis 3 introduces sin and disorder into the created world, breaking the harmony described in Genesis 1-2. In the chapters between Genesis 3 and the flood narrative, this disorder is shown spreading and worsening — from Cain's murder of Abel to a description of widespread human wickedness "every inclination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil all the time" (Genesis 6:5). The flood can therefore be understood as God's serious response to how far this brokenness had spread since the Fall — a decisive act addressing the corruption unleashed in Genesis 3. Yet the covenant God makes with Noah afterward also shows God's ongoing commitment to the world despite its brokenness, setting a pattern (a broken relationship followed by a new promise) that recurs throughout the rest of the Bible's stories, including Abraham's covenant studied the following week.

📚 Week 3 — Reading Comprehension: Fiction
Week 3
1
Read this short passage, then answer the questions below:

"Aiden stared at the acceptance letter for a long time before folding it in half, then in half again, and again, until it was too small to unfold. He placed it at the bottom of his bag, beneath his books, where his mother would not think to look."
(a) What can you INFER about how Aiden feels about the letter, even though his emotions are never directly stated? (b) What does his action of hiding the letter suggest about his relationship with his mother? 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Although the text never states Aiden's emotions directly, the repeated folding of the letter "again, and again" suggests anxiety, avoidance, or reluctance to fully engage with its contents — this could indicate the news is unwelcome, overwhelming, or complicated in some way, rather than simply exciting good news. (b) Hiding the letter specifically from his mother, choosing a spot she "would not think to look," suggests he does not want her to know about it yet — implying either that he fears her reaction, wants to process it privately first, or feels the news might disappoint or worry her.
2
Define "author's intent" and explain why identifying a story's THEME is different from simply summarising its plot. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Author's intent refers to the purpose or message the writer hopes to convey through their story — what they want the reader to think about, feel, or learn. Theme is the underlying idea or message a story explores (e.g. "the cost of ambition," "the value of honesty"), while plot is simply what literally happens in the sequence of events. Two very different stories with completely different plots can share the same theme, showing that theme operates at a deeper, more abstract level than plot summary.

📚 Week 3 — Tissues, Organs & Organ Systems
Week 3
1
Explain the levels of biological organisation, in order from smallest to largest: cell, tissue, organ, organ system. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Cell: the basic building block of life. Tissue: a group of similar cells working together to perform a specific function (e.g. muscle tissue). Organ: a structure made of different tissues working together for a specific job (e.g. the heart, made of muscle tissue, nerve tissue, and connective tissue). Organ system: a group of organs working together to carry out a major body function (e.g. the digestive system).
2
Name THREE major organ systems in the human body, and state the main function of each. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any three of: Circulatory system — transports blood, oxygen, and nutrients around the body. Respiratory system — takes in oxygen and removes carbon dioxide. Digestive system — breaks down food into nutrients the body can use. Nervous system — controls and coordinates body activities using electrical signals. Skeletal system — provides support and protection for the body.
Week 4 — Nutrition & Digestion
🍽️ Week 4 — Nutrition & Digestion
Week 4
3
Name the main food groups needed for a balanced diet, and state one function of each. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Carbohydrates: provide energy. Proteins: support growth and repair of body tissues. Fats: provide long-term energy storage and insulation. Vitamins: support various body functions and immune health (e.g. Vitamin C for immune function). Minerals: support processes like bone strength (calcium) or blood health (iron). (Fibre and water are also acceptable additional answers.)
4
Describe the journey of food through the digestive system, naming at least FOUR organs in the correct order. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Food travels: mouth (chewing and initial digestion with saliva) → oesophagus (tube carrying food to the stomach) → stomach (churns food, adds acid and enzymes) → small intestine (most nutrient absorption occurs here) → large intestine (water absorption, waste formation) → out through the rectum/anus.
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Using the concept of "levels of organisation" (Week 3), explain how the digestive system (Week 4) demonstrates this idea — refer specifically to cells, tissues, and organs working together within this ONE organ system. Write at least 5 sentences. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The digestive system is an excellent example of the levels of biological organisation working together. At the smallest level, individual cells in the stomach lining produce digestive enzymes and acid, while muscle cells contract to churn food. Groups of similar cells form tissues — muscle tissue in the stomach wall allows churning motion, while epithelial tissue lines the intestines to absorb nutrients. These different tissues combine to form organs: the stomach itself is an organ made of muscular tissue, connective tissue, and nerve tissue all working together for the single function of breaking down food. Multiple organs — mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine — then work together as the complete digestive SYSTEM, each organ performing a specific stage of the overall process of turning food into usable nutrients and waste. This shows clearly how the organisation moves from the microscopic (cells) to the whole functioning system, with each level depending on the proper functioning of the level below it.

📚 Week 3 — Ancient Greece
Week 3
1
What was a "city-state" in Ancient Greece, and how did Athenian democracy work? 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A city-state (polis) was an independent city that governed itself, along with its surrounding territory, rather than being part of a larger unified nation — Ancient Greece was made up of many such city-states (e.g. Athens, Sparta), each with its own government and laws. Athenian democracy allowed male citizens to vote directly on laws and decisions in an assembly — a form of "direct democracy" quite different from modern representative democracy, though it notably excluded women, enslaved people, and non-citizens from participation.
2
Name ONE Ancient Greek philosopher and briefly describe one idea associated with them. Then explain ONE way Ancient Greece's legacy still influences the modern world. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any of: Socrates — taught through asking probing questions (the "Socratic method") to encourage critical thinking. Plato — wrote about ideal forms of government and reality in works like "The Republic." Aristotle — studied logic, biology, and ethics systematically. Modern legacy: democracy itself, the word "democracy" (from Greek demos, people + kratos, power), the Olympic Games, and foundational ideas in philosophy, science, and mathematics all trace back to Ancient Greece.
Week 4 — The Roman Empire
🏛️ Week 4 — The Roman Empire
Week 4
3
Briefly describe the rise and fall of the Roman Empire — what allowed it to grow so large, and what factors contributed to its eventual fall? 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Reasons for growth: a strong, well-organised military; an extensive network of roads enabling fast movement of troops and trade; effective governance and law across conquered territories; and engineering achievements (aqueducts, roads, buildings) that helped integrate and control a vast territory. Reasons for fall: the empire became too large to govern and defend effectively; repeated invasions by outside groups; economic difficulties; political instability and corruption; and the eventual split into Western and Eastern halves, with the Western Roman Empire falling in 476 CE.
4
Explain ONE way the Roman Empire impacted Britain specifically. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Accept any reasonable example: the Romans built a network of roads across Britain, many of which still underlie modern routes today. They founded or developed towns and cities, including Londinium (modern London). Hadrian's Wall was built to mark and defend the empire's northern frontier in Britain. Latin influenced the development of the English language, and Roman law and administrative practices left a lasting influence on British governance.
Synthesis
🏆 Extended Synthesis Question
5
Compare Ancient Greek city-state democracy (Week 3) with Roman imperial government (Week 4). What was the KEY difference in how each civilisation organised political power, and why might this matter for understanding the different legacies each left behind? Write at least 6 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Ancient Greek city-states, particularly Athens, organised political power through direct participation of citizens in an assembly — decisions were debated and voted on collectively by those eligible to participate, distributing power relatively broadly (albeit only among a limited group of male citizens). Rome, in contrast, particularly as it grew into an empire, increasingly concentrated power in the hands of an emperor and a centralised administrative and military structure governing vast, diverse territories far from Rome itself — a system built for control and unity across huge distances rather than direct citizen participation. This key difference matters for understanding their different legacies: Greek democracy left behind the philosophical and political IDEA of citizen participation and debate, later inspiring modern democratic systems, while Rome's legacy is more visible in practical infrastructure and administration — law codes, road networks, engineering, and the model of large, centrally-governed territories. Both civilisations shaped the modern world enormously, but through fundamentally different approaches to organising power: one prioritising participation and debate, the other prioritising order, control, and unification across scale.
