💡 ESL Tip: A covenant is much stronger than an ordinary promise — think of it as an unbreakable agreement. God's covenants in the Bible are always His initiative: He makes the promise, and He keeps it.
1
What does the word "covenant" mean? How is it different from an ordinary promise? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A covenant is a solemn, binding promise — much stronger than an ordinary promise. An ordinary promise can be broken, but a biblical covenant is a formal agreement, often sealed with a sign (like the rainbow). God's covenant with Noah was a promise to all living creatures on earth — it was unconditional and eternal.
2
What does it mean to be "righteous"? How does Genesis describe Noah compared to the people around him? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Righteous means living in right relationship with God — doing what is right even when others do not. Genesis 6:9 describes Noah as "righteous and blameless among the people of his time" who "walked faithfully with God." This is a striking contrast to the rest of his generation, which is described as corrupt and violent.
3
What does the Hebrew word "tebah" mean? What does this tell us about the purpose of the ark? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Tebah means "box" or "chest." This tells us the ark was NOT designed for sailing or exploring — it was built purely for protection and survival. It was a vessel of salvation, not adventure.

Write out the memory verse — Hebrews 11:7 — from memory. Then underline the THREE words or phrases you think are most important. 2 marks

Key phrases to notice: "things not yet seen" (faith before proof), "built an ark" (faith shown through action), "heir of righteousness" (right standing with God through faith).

What sign did God give to seal His covenant with Noah? What did this sign represent? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
God gave a rainbow as the sign of the covenant (Genesis 9:13–16). It represented God's promise never again to destroy all life with a flood. The rainbow was meant to remind both God and humanity of this covenant every time it appeared in the sky.

The lesson says God's covenant with Noah was made "to the whole earth" — not just to Noah personally. Why is this significant? 🟢 EasySection A — Key definitions and basic identification.
📚 Sentence Types — Definitions
💡 ESL Tip: An independent clause is a group of words with a subject AND a verb that makes complete sense alone. A dependent clause also has a subject and verb, but does NOT make complete sense alone — it needs the main clause to complete its meaning.
1
What is the difference between an independent clause and a dependent clause? Give one example of each. 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
An independent clause has a subject and verb and makes complete sense alone. Example: "The dog barked." A dependent clause also has a subject and verb but does NOT make complete sense alone — it depends on the main clause. Example: "...because it was hungry." This needs to be attached to an independent clause to make sense.
2
Define each sentence type and give an original example of each: 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Simple: ONE independent clause. Example: "The river flooded the valley." Compound: TWO or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS) or semicolon. Example: "The river flooded the valley, and the farmers lost their crops." Complex: One independent clause + one or more dependent clauses joined by a subordinating conjunction. Example: "Although the river flooded, the farmers managed to save some crops."
3
What does FANBOYS stand for? List all 7 words and the relationship each one shows. 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
For — gives a reason (like "because," but more formal). And — adds an equal idea. Nor — adds a second negative idea. But — shows contrast. Or — shows a choice or alternative. Yet — shows surprising contrast. So — shows result or consequence.
4
Identify the sentence type (simple / compound / complex) for each sentence below: 2 marks

a) "She studied hard, so she passed the exam."
b) "Although it was dark, he continued walking."
c) "The storm destroyed the bridge."
d) "He paused, yet he did not turn back."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Compound — two independent clauses joined by FANBOYS conjunction "so." b) Complex — dependent clause first ("Although it was dark"), followed by independent clause ("he continued walking"). c) Simple — one independent clause, one subject, one verb. d) Compound — two independent clauses joined by "yet."
Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Comma rules, complex sentence construction, and error correction.
🖊️ Punctuation & Construction
5
Explain the comma rule for complex sentences. Write the same idea TWO ways — dependent clause first, then independent clause first — and explain where the comma goes each time. 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Rule: When the dependent clause comes FIRST, use a comma after it. When the independent clause comes first, no comma is usually needed. Version 1 (DC first): "Because it was raining heavily, the match was cancelled." (comma after the dependent clause) Version 2 (IC first): "The match was cancelled because it was raining heavily." (no comma needed)
6
What is a comma splice? Identify and correct the THREE comma splices below using THREE different methods (add a conjunction; use a full stop; use a semicolon): 4 marks

a) "I was tired, I went to bed early."
b) "The weather was terrible, she went for a walk anyway."
c) "He read the letter, he did not reply."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A comma splice is joining two independent clauses with ONLY a comma — no conjunction. This is always an error. a) "I was tired, so I went to bed early." (FANBOYS conjunction) b) "The weather was terrible. She went for a walk anyway." (full stop — splits into two sentences) c) "He read the letter; he did not reply." (semicolon — joins two related independent clauses)
7
Rewrite this simple sentence THREE times — once as a compound sentence (using a different FANBOYS conjunction each time), and once as a complex sentence: 4 marks

Original: "Maya finished her homework."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Compound 1 (but): "Maya finished her homework, but she was too tired to revise further." Compound 2 (so): "Maya finished her homework, so she allowed herself to rest." Compound 3 (yet): "Maya finished her homework, yet she felt uneasy about tomorrow's test." Complex: "Although she was exhausted, Maya finished her homework." (OR: "Maya finished her homework before the deadline arrived.")
8
The lesson says "a direct question gets a question mark — but a reported question gets a full stop." Punctuate each sentence correctly and explain your choice: 3 marks

a) She asked what time the library closed ___
b) What time does the library close ___
c) He wondered whether the train was on time ___
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) "She asked what time the library closed." — This is a reported/indirect question, so it ends with a full stop, not a question mark. b) "What time does the library close?" — This is a direct question (the actual words asked), so it ends with a question mark. c) "He wondered whether the train was on time." — Reported thought/question — full stop.
Section C
🔴 HardSection C — Analytical writing about sentence structure and its effect on readers.
🔍 Craft & Analysis
9
A writer deliberately uses THREE simple sentences in a row before a long complex sentence. What effect might this have on a reader? Why might a writer choose simple sentences at a particular moment? Write at least 4 sentences in your response and use the word "rhythm" in your answer. 4 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 3 English — mood, author's intent, and how language choices create effects on readers.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Simple sentences create a short, punchy rhythm — each one delivers a single sharp idea with no elaboration. Three in a row can build tension or urgency, making the reader feel the pace accelerating. When followed by a long complex sentence, the contrast can feel like a release — the complexity mirrors reflection or consequence after action. A writer might choose simple sentences at a moment of crisis, shock, or decisive action because the brevity mirrors the speed of events. This is an intentional craft choice that controls how fast the reader "breathes" through a text.
10
Write a short paragraph (6–8 sentences) describing a moment of tension or decision. You MUST use: (a) at least ONE simple sentence for effect, (b) at least ONE compound sentence with a FANBOYS conjunction, (c) at least ONE complex sentence with a subordinating conjunction placed at the START. Then label each sentence type in brackets after each sentence. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Example: "The letter lay on the table. [simple] She had been avoiding it for three days, but she could not avoid it any longer. [compound] Although her hands were shaking, she reached forward and picked it up. [complex — DC first, comma used] She opened it slowly. [simple] The words swam before her eyes, so she had to read them twice. [compound] When she finally understood what it said, she sat down and did not move for a long time. [complex — DC first]" — Key marking points: simple sentence used for impact, comma + FANBOYS in compound, dependent clause at START of complex with comma correctly placed, variety of subordinating conjunctions used.
11
Explain why the comma splice is considered such a serious error in formal writing. Why can't writers just use a comma wherever they want to connect ideas? Use the lesson's "self-check" rule in your explanation and give TWO examples to illustrate. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A comma splice is serious because it misrepresents the grammatical relationship between two ideas — a comma signals a brief pause, not a full connection between two independent clauses. Using a comma alone between two complete sentences gives the reader no signal about HOW the two ideas relate: are they contrasting? Consequential? Additional? The lesson's self-check rule states: "Read each half separately. If BOTH make complete sense alone, you need a conjunction, semicolon, or full stop — not just a comma." Example 1 (error): "I revised for hours, I still felt unprepared." (the reader cannot tell if this is a contrast, a surprise, or simply an addition — "but" or "yet" would clarify). Example 2 (correction): "I revised for hours; I still felt unprepared." (the semicolon correctly joins two related independent clauses while preserving the ironic contrast).

What are "like denominators"? What are "unlike denominators"? Give one example of each. 2 marks

Like denominators = fractions with the SAME bottom number. Example: 2/9 and 5/9 (both have denominator 9). Unlike denominators = fractions with DIFFERENT bottom numbers. Example: 1/4 and 1/6 (denominators are 4 and 6).
2
Add these fractions with like denominators. Simplify your answer if possible: 3 marks

a) 2/7 + 3/7    b) 3/8 + 7/8    c) 5/12 + 5/12
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) 2/7 + 3/7 = 5/7 (already simplified) b) 3/8 + 7/8 = 10/8 = 5/4 = 1¼ (improper → mixed number, then simplify) c) 5/12 + 5/12 = 10/12 = 5/6 (HCF of 10 and 12 is 2, divide both by 2)
3
Subtract these fractions with like denominators. Simplify your answer: 3 marks

🟢 EasySection A — Key vocabulary and food group recall.
📚 Key Vocabulary & Food Groups
💡 ESL Tip: "Nutrient" = something in food your body NEEDS. "Nutrition" = the process of taking in and using nutrients. A "balanced diet" does NOT mean equal amounts of everything — it means the RIGHT PROPORTIONS of each food group.
1
What is the difference between a "nutrient" and a "balanced diet"? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A nutrient is a substance in food that the body needs — such as carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins or minerals. A balanced diet means eating the right VARIETY and PROPORTION of food groups for good health — not equal amounts of everything, but the correct proportions of each group.
2
Name the FIVE food groups and state the main function of each one. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Carbohydrates — main source of ENERGY. 2. Fruits & Vegetables — provide vitamins, minerals and fibre; support the immune system. 3. Protein Foods — GROWTH and REPAIR of muscles, skin and organs. 4. Dairy & Alternatives — calcium for strong bones and teeth. 5. Fats & Oils — energy storage, insulation, helps absorb fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).
3
What is "peristalsis"? Which part of the digestive system does it happen in? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Peristalsis is the wave-like muscular squeezing movements that push food along the digestive tract. It begins in the oesophagus and continues through the intestines — it is what moves food downward even if you were eating upside down.
4
What is an enzyme? Why does the body need enzymes for digestion? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
An enzyme is a biological molecule (a type of protein) that speeds up chemical reactions — in digestion, enzymes break large food molecules into smaller ones that are small enough to be absorbed into the blood. Without enzymes, chemical digestion would be too slow to sustain life.

6
Bile is NOT an enzyme — but it is essential for digestion. Explain what bile is, where it comes from, and exactly what it does in the digestive process. Why does it help lipase work more effectively? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Bile is a substance produced by the liver and stored in the gallbladder. It is NOT an enzyme — it does not break molecules apart chemically. Instead, it emulsifies fats — it breaks large fat globules into tiny droplets. This massively increases the surface area available for lipase to act on, making fat digestion much faster and more efficient. Without bile, lipase would only be able to work on the surface of large fat globules — a much slower process.

💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Absorption mainly happens in the small intestine — nutrients pass from the gut into the bloodstream through tiny finger-like projections called villi. In Week 3, we learned that the small intestine's function is to "absorb nutrients from digested food" — this week adds the WHY: enzymes have broken food into molecules small enough to pass through the villi into the blood. Structure (Week 3) + Chemical process (Week 4) = the complete picture of digestion.
Section C
🔴 HardSection C — Application and extended scientific reasoning.
🧠 Critical Thinking
8
A diet that contains only carbohydrates and fats but no protein, vitamins or minerals is technically "filling" but not "balanced." Using at least THREE specific consequences of missing nutrients, explain why this diet would be harmful to a teenager's health. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Missing protein: The body cannot grow or repair muscle and tissue — essential during teenage growth. Without enough amino acids, wounds heal slowly and muscles cannot develop. 2. Missing iron: Without iron, the body cannot make haemoglobin for red blood cells → anaemia → tiredness, poor concentration, low energy. 3. Missing vitamin D and calcium: Bones cannot strengthen properly during adolescence, increasing risk of fractures and long-term bone weakness (osteoporosis in later life). 4. Missing fibre: Digestion slows, the gut becomes unhealthy, and the risk of bowel problems increases. A teenager who "feels full" on carbohydrates and fats is still nutritionally deficient — fullness does not equal health.
9
Amylase begins breaking down starch in the MOUTH — before food even reaches the stomach. Design a simple experiment to test whether amylase in saliva actually breaks down starch. Name the equipment you would need, what you would do, and what result you would expect if amylase IS working. 5 marks
ESL Tip: Think about how you would know starch is present or absent. Iodine solution turns dark blue-black in the presence of starch. If starch has been broken down, iodine stays orange-brown.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Equipment: Starch solution, saliva (collected in a tube), iodine solution, test tubes, water bath at 37°C (body temperature), timer. Method: Mix starch solution with saliva in one test tube (the test). Mix starch with water in another test tube (the control — to make it fair). Place both in the water bath. After 5–10 minutes, add a few drops of iodine to each. Expected result: The control (starch + water) turns dark blue-black (starch still present). The test tube (starch + saliva) stays orange-brown or turns a lighter colour — showing the starch has been broken down by amylase into simple sugars.
10
The macronutrient "fat" is often seen negatively. Using the lesson, explain THREE ways fats are actually ESSENTIAL to the body. Then explain the difference between fats being "needed in small amounts" vs being harmful in excess. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
THREE essential uses of fat: 1. Long-term energy storage — the body stores fat as a reserve energy source when carbohydrates run out. 2. Insulation — fat under the skin protects the body from cold. 3. Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins — vitamins A, D, E and K can only be absorbed from food when fat is present. Small amounts = essential. Excess = harmful because the body stores excess fat, which over time can cause obesity, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes. The key is proportion — fat is never "bad" in the right amount.

Day 13 Difficulty LevelsKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Key vocabulary and basic timeline recall.
📚 Key Vocabulary & Timeline
💡 ESL Tip: "Republic" comes from the Latin res publica — "public matter" or "public affair." A republic is governed by representatives elected by citizens, not by a king. "Empire" comes from imperium — the power to command. These Latin roots appear in many modern words in English.
1
What is the difference between a "republic" and an "empire"? Use Rome as your example. 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A republic is governed by elected representatives rather than a single ruler. In Rome (509–27 BC), two elected Consuls and the Senate held power. An empire is ruled by one supreme authority — an emperor — often for life. Rome became an empire in 27 BC when Augustus took power after the civil wars following Julius Caesar's assassination.

What does SPQR stand for? What does it tell us about Roman identity and values? 2 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
SPQR = Senatus Populusque Romanus = "The Senate and People of Rome." It tells us that Roman identity was built around the idea that power belonged to both the Senate (the governing council) and the Roman people together — not to a single ruler. This was a source of great pride during the Republic.

The lesson explicitly connects Roman military discipline to the Spartan military culture from Week 3, but says they had "different underlying reasons." Explain the comparison AND the difference in underlying reasons. 3 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 3 Humanities — Sparta's military culture, discipline, and the purpose of Spartan training.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Both Roman soldiers and Spartans underwent rigorous, constant training and lived under strict military discipline. Rome's reason: conquest and control of a vast empire — Roman roads, camps, and formations were all built to allow rapid movement and control across enormous territory. Sparta's reason: internal control and survival — Spartan discipline was partly driven by fear of the helot (enslaved population) revolt. Both cultures valued military excellence, but for very different political and social reasons.

Why was Julius Caesar's assassination in 44 BC historically ironic? What did the assassins fear, and what did their action actually bring about? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The assassins (senators) feared that Caesar wanted to become a permanent king — destroying the Republic. The irony is that by assassinating him, they triggered the civil wars that ultimately led to the Republic's collapse. Caesar's adopted heir Octavian (Augustus) emerged victorious and became Rome's first emperor in 27 BC — the very thing the assassins had killed Caesar to prevent. Their action brought about the outcome they feared most.

The lesson compares Roman Republican government to Athenian democracy (Week 3). Using what you know about BOTH systems, explain ONE significant similarity and TWO significant differences. For each difference, explain its historical significance. Use the structure: Similarity → Difference 1 + Significance → Difference 2 + Significance. 5 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 3 Humanities — Athenian democracy, the agora, who could vote, and the role of citizens in government.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Similarity: Both used elected representatives and citizen assemblies — both valued the idea that citizens (though a limited group) should have a role in government, not just be ruled by kings. Difference 1: Rome's voting system favoured the WEALTHY more heavily than Athens' — richer citizens had more voting power in Rome's assemblies. Significance: This made Roman republican democracy less equal than Athenian democracy in practice, despite similar structures. Difference 2: Athens never had an equivalent to an all-powerful emperor — when democracy ended in Athens, it was replaced by oligarchy or Macedonian rule, not a single lifetime ruler. Significance: This shows how Rome's system had a structural vulnerability — once one man (Augustus) had enough military power, the Republic could not resist transformation into a monarchy in all but name.

Hadrian's Wall is described as marking and defending "the northern frontier." Why do you think the Romans chose to build a wall rather than simply conquering all of Britain? What does this tell us about the limits of Roman power? Write at least 5 sentences. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Hadrian's Wall tells us that even the Roman Empire had limits. The terrain of northern Britain (modern Scotland) was difficult, and the Pictish tribes there were formidable fighters who had resisted Roman conquest. Rather than sustain costly campaigns with uncertain results, Emperor Hadrian chose a defensive strategy — control what you hold rather than endlessly expand. This was actually a wider policy shift under Hadrian, who consolidated rather than expanded the empire. The wall also served as a symbol of Roman power and a checkpoint for trade and movement — not just a military barrier. Ironically, the very existence of the wall shows the boundary of Roman ambition: even Rome could not conquer everything, and knowing when to stop is itself a form of strategic wisdom.

1. Latin alphabet: Every English letter you write is directly derived from the Roman Latin alphabet. Roman law concepts: The idea that people are "innocent until proven guilty," that laws should be written and public, and the structure of courts and legal codes — all trace back to Roman legal tradition. Architecture: Roman architectural features — the arch, dome, concrete, colonnaded buildings — are visible in banks, courts, parliament buildings and universities worldwide. Architects still study and replicate Roman structural innovations. Roman roads: the reason many modern British roads are straight traces directly to Roman engineering.
