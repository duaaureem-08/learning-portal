⭐ Memory Verse
"The Lord will fight for you; you need only to be still."
Exodus 14:14 — NIV
🧠
Word-by-word breakdown: "The Lord will fight for you" = God is promising to act on behalf of His people — this is not a passive promise but an active one. He is about to do something. "you need only to be still" = The people of Israel are terrified, standing between a massive army behind them and the Red Sea in front of them. God is not asking them to fight — He is asking them to trust and stop panicking. This verse is said at the most hopeless-looking moment in the whole story — which is exactly what makes it so powerful.
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
📖
Learn these words before you continue. Many of these words appear in both the Bible text and in history lessons about ancient Egypt.
Core Vocabulary
oppression
Treating a group of people in a cruel, unjust, and controlling way, especially using power against them. The Egyptians oppressed the Israelites by forcing them into hard labour as slaves.
Pharaoh
The title given to the king of ancient Egypt. In Moses' story, Pharaoh had absolute power over everyone in Egypt — including the enslaved Israelites. (Pronounced: FAIR-oh)
plague
A terrible disaster or disease sent as a punishment or warning. In Exodus, God sends ten plagues — each one targeting something Egypt worshipped or depended on — to force Pharaoh to release the Israelites.
Passover
The name of both the final plague and the special meal the Israelites were commanded to eat the night before they left Egypt. The word "Passover" refers to God passing over the homes of the Israelites and protecting them from the final plague.
exodus
A large group of people leaving a place all at once. The word comes from Greek: ex (out) + hodos (road/journey). In the Bible, the Exodus refers specifically to the Israelites leaving Egypt after 430 years.
covenant
A serious, binding agreement or promise — much stronger than a normal contract. God's covenant with Abraham (Week 5) promised a land and a great nation. The Exodus is the beginning of God keeping that very promise.
deliver / deliverance
To rescue someone from danger, captivity, or suffering. In Exodus, God "delivers" His people — the same word used in many languages for saving someone from something they could not escape on their own.
calling / called
A deep sense that God has chosen you for a specific purpose or task. Moses' calling comes through a burning bush — but he does not feel qualified and argues with God repeatedly before accepting it.
intercession
Praying or speaking on behalf of someone else. Moses intercedes for the Israelites many times — he stands between God and the people, arguing for mercy.
⛓️ The People of Israel in Egypt — How It All Began
📍
Connecting to last week: Remember Joseph from Week 6? He was the one who brought his family to Egypt to survive the famine. That family — 70 people — grew over 430 years into a nation of possibly over a million people. But a new Pharaoh came who did not remember Joseph, and he was afraid of how numerous the Israelites had become.
😰 What Egypt Did to Israel
Put them into forced labour — building cities like Pithom and Rameses (Exodus 1:11)
Made their lives "bitter with hard service" in brick-making and farming (Exodus 1:14)
Ordered all newborn Israelite baby boys to be thrown into the River Nile (Exodus 1:22)
Refused to listen to Moses, even as the plagues fell — hardening his heart ten times
🙏 How God Responded
Heard the Israelites' cry for help and remembered His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Exodus 2:24)
Called Moses — an unlikely leader — through the burning bush (Exodus 3)
Sent ten plagues to demonstrate His power over every aspect of Egyptian life
Ultimately led the people out through the Red Sea — permanently destroying Pharaoh's army
🔑
KS3 thinking point: The Israelites did not escape Egypt because they were powerful or clever. They left because God intervened. The whole Exodus story is built around the idea that the most powerful empire in the ancient world — Egypt — could not hold God's people when God decided to act. This is what makes the story so radical.
🔥 Moses and the Burning Bush — Exodus 3–4
📍
Who is Moses? Moses was an Israelite baby whose mother hid him in a basket in the Nile to save him from Pharaoh's order. He was found by Pharaoh's own daughter and raised as an Egyptian prince. As a young man, he killed an Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew slave and then fled to Midian, where he worked as a shepherd for 40 years — before God called him at the burning bush at age 80.
1
The Strange Sight (Exodus 3:1-3)
Moses is tending his father-in-law's sheep on Mount Horeb (also called Sinai) when he sees a bush that is burning but is not burning up — it is not being destroyed by the fire. He turns aside to look more carefully. This is what gets his attention. God often works through the unexpected and the "strange" to get people to pay attention.
2
God Calls Him by Name (Exodus 3:4-6)
God calls "Moses! Moses!" from within the bush. Moses answers "Here I am." God then tells him: "Do not come any closer. Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy ground." God then introduces Himself as "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." He is not a new God — He is the same God who made promises generations earlier. Moses hides his face because he is afraid to look at God.
3
God Reveals His Plan (Exodus 3:7-10)
God says He has "seen the misery of my people... heard them crying out... I am concerned about their suffering." This is deeply important — God is not distant or unaware. He knows. Then He says: "So I have come down to rescue them." And then — the challenge: "So now, go. I am sending YOU to Pharaoh to bring my people the Israelites out of Egypt."
4
Moses' Five Excuses — and God's Answers
Moses does not say "yes" immediately. He gives five reasons why he is the wrong person for the job — and God answers every single one of them. This is one of the most honest and human exchanges in the entire Bible.
Moses' ExcuseWhat He Was Really FeelingGod's Answer
"Who am I to go to Pharaoh?" (3:11)Insecurity — "I'm a nobody shepherd, not a prince any more""I will be with you." God does not argue with Moses' self-assessment — He simply says His presence is enough.
"What if they ask Your name?" (3:13)Uncertainty — "How do I explain who sent me?""I AM WHO I AM." God gives Moses His eternal, self-existent name — the name that cannot be reduced to anything else.
"What if they don't believe me?" (4:1)Fear of rejection — "What if no one listens?"God gives Moses three miraculous signs to perform as proof: a staff that becomes a snake, a hand made leprous then healed, and water turned to blood.
"I am not a good speaker" (4:10)Inadequacy — "I'm slow of speech and tongue""Who gave human beings their mouths?... Now go; I will help you speak." God invented speech — He can certainly help one man with it.
"Please send someone else" (4:13)Pure reluctance — Moses simply does not want to goGod's anger burns against Moses, but He still provides a practical solution — his brother Aaron will speak for him. God does not give up on Moses even when Moses tries to give up on himself.
✅
KS3 key idea — what "calling" really looks like: Moses is one of the greatest figures in the Old Testament — and yet he argued with God five times before saying yes. This is actually very comforting. It shows that calling does not require instant courage. It requires eventually saying yes — even when you feel completely unqualified.
⚡ The Ten Plagues — Why Ten? What Do They Mean?
🧠
Before you read: Each plague is not random. Ancient Egypt was deeply religious, worshipping many gods who were connected to nature — the Nile, frogs, the sun, livestock, etc. The plagues systematically target exactly what Egypt worshipped and relied on — showing that the God of Israel is more powerful than all of Egypt's gods combined. This is a theological battle, not just a political one.
Plague 1 — Exodus 7:14-25
Water Turned to Blood 🩸
The Nile River — Egypt's lifeblood, worshipped as sacred — turns to blood. All the fish die. Egypt cannot drink from it. The Egyptian god Hapi was the god of the Nile floods. This plague directly targeted what Egypt revered most. Pharaoh's heart is hardened; he walks away.
Plague 2 — Exodus 8:1-15
Frogs 🐸
Frogs cover every surface of Egypt — beds, ovens, kneading troughs, everywhere. The frog goddess Heqet was worshipped in Egypt as a symbol of fertility. Pharaoh begs Moses to take the frogs away, promising to let the people go — but once the frogs die, he goes back on his word. This is the first time we see his pattern: promise under pressure, break the promise when pressure lifts.
Plague 3 — Exodus 8:16-19
Gnats / Lice 🪲
The dust of the ground becomes gnats or lice all across Egypt. For the first time, Egypt's own magicians — who had been copying some of Moses' signs — cannot replicate this plague. They say to Pharaoh: "This is the finger of God." Even his own court recognises something greater is at work. But Pharaoh refuses to listen even to his own advisors.
Plagues 4–6 — Exodus 8:20–9:12
Flies, Livestock Disease, Boils 🪰🐄🤒
A key change begins here: the Israelites living in the region of Goshen are specifically protected from the plagues of flies and livestock disease. This is a clear sign that God is distinguishing between His people and the Egyptians — the plagues are not just natural disasters but targeted judgements. Egypt's cattle die (targeting the god Apis, a sacred bull); boils break out on Egyptians and animals. Pharaoh's own magicians cannot even stand before Moses because of their boils.
Plagues 7–9 — Exodus 9:13–10:29
Hail, Locusts, Darkness ⛈️🌾🌑
The plagues now become catastrophic. Hail destroys crops and livestock still in the field. Locusts devour everything the hail left. Then — for three days — complete, tangible darkness covers Egypt (targeting Ra, the sun god, whom Pharaoh was supposed to represent). Not even candles can pierce it. Egypt literally cannot see. But in all the Israelites' homes, there is light. Each time, Pharaoh promises, then breaks his promise. His advisors beg him to let the people go. He refuses.
Plague 10 — Exodus 11–12
Death of the Firstborn 💔
The final plague — and the most devastating. God warns that the firstborn son in every Egyptian household, from Pharaoh's own son to the prisoner in the dungeon, will die at midnight. This is also the night of the very first Passover. The Israelites are given specific instructions to protect themselves — and God passes over their homes. At midnight, every Egyptian firstborn dies. Pharaoh — his own son included — finally, completely, releases the Israelites to leave.
⚠️
Understanding "Pharaoh's hardened heart": The Bible says both that God hardened Pharaoh's heart (e.g. 9:12) AND that Pharaoh hardened his own heart (e.g. 8:15). Scholars understand this to mean that Pharaoh's own stubborn nature — choosing power and pride over truth — was the same spiritual condition that God used as the vehicle for His judgement. It is like how the same sun both melts wax and hardens clay — the nature of the object determines the result.
🍞 The Passover — Exodus 12 — Every Detail Has a Meaning
📍
God gives the Israelites very specific instructions for the night of the tenth plague. Every detail carries a meaning — and these same details become central to Jewish worship for thousands of years afterward. Christians also connect the Passover directly to Jesus, calling Him "the Lamb of God" (John 1:29).
🐑
A Perfect Lamb — No Defects
Each household was to select a one-year-old male lamb without any defect on the 10th of the month and keep it until the 14th. The idea of a "perfect" sacrificial animal — without blemish — runs through the entire Old Testament sacrificial system. It represents something of full, uncorrupted value being given in place of the sinner.
🩸
The Blood on the Doorposts
The blood of the lamb was to be placed on the two doorposts and the top of the door frame — forming what some scholars note resembles a cross shape. When God saw the blood, He would "pass over" that house and the firstborn inside would be protected. The blood was not just symbolic — it was the specific thing that distinguished a protected house from an unprotected one.
🌿
Bitter Herbs and Unleavened Bread
The meal included bitter herbs (maror) — to remember the bitterness of slavery — and unleavened bread (matzah) — bread made without yeast, because they were leaving in such a hurry there was no time to let dough rise. These physical, edible symbols turn an event into a remembered experience. Every time the bread is eaten without yeast, the urgency of the night is felt again.
👟
Dressed, Ready to Leave
They were to eat the meal with their sandals on their feet, staff in hand, and in a hurry (Exodus 12:11). This is not a relaxed dinner — it is a people ready to move the moment the word comes. Faith, in this picture, looks like readiness. They did not eat and then start packing. They ate packed.
🔗
The Passover and Jesus — a connection to learn: In the New Testament, Jesus holds his "Last Supper" specifically during the Passover meal. When he holds up the bread and wine, he is deliberately connecting his own death to the Passover lamb — "This is my body broken for you... this is my blood of the new covenant." Christians believe Jesus is the ultimate Passover Lamb: perfect, without defect, whose blood protects those who trust in it. This is why Jesus is called "the Lamb of God" throughout the New Testament.
🌊 The Red Sea Crossing — Exodus 14 — Faith at the Impossible Moment
📍
After Pharaoh releases the Israelites, he changes his mind — again. He sends 600 of his best chariots and his entire army after the fleeing Israelites. The Israelites look up and see the Egyptian army behind them — and the Red Sea in front of them. There is nowhere to go. This is the exact moment of Exodus 14:14.
😱 The Israelites' Reaction (14:10-12)
They "were terrified and cried out to the Lord"
They blamed Moses: "Was it because there were no graves in Egypt that you brought us to the desert to die?"
They said it would have been better to stay as slaves in Egypt
This is the first of many times in the wilderness that the Israelites will look back fondly on slavery when things get hard
✊ Moses' Response (14:13-14)
"Do not be afraid. Stand firm and you will see the deliverance the Lord will bring today."
"The Egyptians you see today you will never see again."
"The Lord will fight for you; you need only to be still."
This is Moses at his finest — terrified himself, but choosing to declare faith rather than panic
1
The Cloud Moves (Exodus 14:19-20)
The pillar of cloud that had been leading the Israelites moves to stand between the Israelite camp and the Egyptian army — giving darkness to the Egyptians and light to the Israelites all through the night. God literally places Himself between His people and their enemy.
2
The Sea Splits (Exodus 14:21-22)
Moses stretches out his hand over the sea. A strong east wind blows all night, and the sea is divided. The water becomes walls on either side, and the Israelites walk through on dry ground. This is the centrepiece miracle of the Old Testament — a national memory so powerful that it is referenced over 100 times in the rest of the Bible.
3
The Egyptians Follow — and Drown (Exodus 14:23-28)
The Egyptian army follows the Israelites into the divided sea. But then God throws the Egyptian army into confusion — wheels fall off chariots, the army cannot move properly. Moses stretches out his hand again, the sea returns to normal, and the entire Egyptian army drowns. Not one soldier survives. The greatest military power in the world is destroyed in a single night.
4
The Song of Moses (Exodus 15:1-21)
The Israelites' response to deliverance is to sing. Moses leads a song of praise, and his sister Miriam leads the women with tambourines. "The Lord is my strength and my song; he has become my salvation." The Exodus doesn't end in silence — it ends in worship. This is a pattern throughout the Bible: deliverance → praise.
👤 The Character of Moses — What Made Him Great?
📍
Moses is described in Deuteronomy 34:10 as a prophet unlike any other — one whom God knew "face to face." Yet the Bible is also completely honest about his weaknesses, his doubts, his anger, and his failures. Understanding Moses means understanding that God uses very imperfect people to do extraordinary things.
Quality ShownWhere We See ItWhat It Means for Us
Courage despite fearStanding before Pharaoh ten times — the most powerful ruler in the known worldCourage is not the absence of fear. Moses was clearly afraid. Courage is acting rightly despite the fear.
HumilityNumbers 12:3 says he was "more humble than anyone else on the face of the earth"The most influential leader in the Old Testament was not proud. True leadership does not require arrogance.
IntercessionHe prays for the Israelites when God is angry at them (Exodus 32:11-14)A leader who uses their position to protect others — not just themselves — is worth following.
PersistenceGoes back to Pharaoh ten times after ten rejectionsFaithfulness sometimes just means keeping going when it would be easier to quit.
Human weaknessHis anger, his excuses, and his eventual failure (Numbers 20) — he never entered the Promised LandEven the greatest people in the Bible were flawed. Their stories are honest about this. This is part of why they are trustworthy records.
🎯
The golden idea of this lesson: Moses' story teaches us that God does not call the equipped — He equips the called. Moses had no qualifications, no military power, no political position. He had five excuses. Yet God used him to accomplish the defining event of the entire Old Testament. The difference was not Moses' ability. It was God's presence: "I will be with you" (Exodus 3:12).

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these — they unlock non-fiction reading
non-fiction
Writing about real events, real people, and real facts — not made-up stories. Examples: newspaper articles, science reports, encyclopaedia entries, biographies, textbooks, speeches, advertisements, and instruction manuals.
purpose
The reason a text was written — what the writer is trying to DO. Common purposes: to inform (give facts), to persuade (change someone's mind), to instruct (tell you how to do something), to entertain (even some non-fiction tries to be fun to read), or to explain (make something complex easier to understand).
audience
The group of people a text is written FOR. A text's audience affects everything — the vocabulary used, the level of detail, the tone, and what is assumed the reader already knows. A medical journal and a children's health leaflet can be about the same disease but written for completely different audiences.
tone
The "feeling" or attitude in a piece of writing — the way it "sounds." A text can be formal, informal, serious, humorous, urgent, reassuring, aggressive, neutral, or emotional. Tone is created through word choice, sentence length, and punctuation.
fact
A statement that can be checked and proven to be true or false. "Mount Everest is 8,849 metres tall" is a fact — you can measure it. Facts are the building blocks of reliable non-fiction.
opinion
A statement that expresses a personal view or judgement — it cannot be proven true or false, only agreed with or disagreed with. "Mount Everest is the most beautiful mountain in the world" is an opinion. Skilled readers always distinguish facts from opinions in non-fiction.
bias
When a writer shows a one-sided viewpoint — either leaving out information that would disagree with their argument, or using language that strongly favours one side. Bias is not always intentional; sometimes writers are biased without knowing it. Critical readers always ask: "What is this writer NOT telling me?"
headline
The title of a newspaper or online article, usually in large, bold text. Headlines are carefully chosen to attract readers — they often use wordplay, shock, or strong emotion. A headline is NOT always a neutral summary of the article's content.
source
Where information comes from. A reliable source has an author, a date, a verifiable organisation behind it, and matches information found in other reliable sources. Understanding sources is essential for evaluating whether to trust a text.
📰 What Is Non-Fiction? — The Many Types
📍
Key idea: Non-fiction is not just one thing. Different types of non-fiction have different structures, different purposes, and different rules about what they include. Recognising what type of non-fiction you are reading is the first step to reading it well.

🎯 Identifying Purpose — WHY Was This Written?
💡
Remember PEETLE: Purpose · Entertain · Explain · Teach · Inform · LEt others know. Most non-fiction has MORE THAN ONE purpose at the same time — a news article might both inform AND entertain; a report might both explain AND persuade. Your job is to identify the PRIMARY (main) purpose and any secondary ones.
📢
To Persuade — Changing Your Mind or Behaviour
The writer wants you to agree with them, buy something, vote for something, or change how you act. Clues: one-sided arguments, emotional language, opinions presented as if they were facts, use of repetition and rhetorical questions ("Surely we can all agree that...?"). Example: a charity asking for donations, an opinion piece in a newspaper arguing about climate policy.
📋
To Inform — Giving You Facts
The writer wants to tell you something you did not know. Clues: statistics, dates, factual statements, neutral (balanced) language, no obvious preference expressed. Example: a weather report, a news story about a court case, a Wikipedia article. Purely informative texts try to avoid taking sides.
📖
To Explain — Making Complex Things Clear
The writer wants to help you understand something complicated. Clues: simple vocabulary chosen deliberately, diagrams and examples, definitions of technical words, step-by-step structure, second-person address ("When you look at the diagram..."). Example: an instruction manual, an explainer article on how vaccines work.
😊
To Entertain — Even in Non-Fiction
Some non-fiction is written to be enjoyable as well as informative. Clues: humour, vivid descriptions, personal anecdotes, interesting character details, informal or witty tone. Example: a travel article, a funny personal essay, a nature documentary script. Entertainment does not make a text less trustworthy — but you still need to separate the facts from the fun.
👥 Identifying Audience — WHO Is This Written For?
📍
A text's audience is not always stated. You have to work it out by looking at clues inside the text itself. This is called inferring the audience — using evidence to reach a conclusion that is not directly stated.
Audience ClueWhat It Tells YouExample
Vocabulary levelSimple words → younger or non-specialist audience. Technical jargon without definitions → specialist audience already familiar with the subject."The patient presented with acute myocardial infarction" vs "He had a heart attack"
Tone — formal vs informalFormal = professional, official, or older audience. Informal or conversational = younger, general, or friendly audience.A government report vs a teenagers' magazine
What is assumed without explainingIf a text does not explain basic concepts, it assumes the reader already knows them. This tells you the audience is already knowledgeable.A medical journal assumes readers know what DNA is
Use of "you" vs "one""You" is direct and informal. "One should consider..." is very formal. "We" builds a sense of community with the reader."You can try this at home" vs "Members of the public are encouraged to..."
Images and designBright colours, large fonts, and cartoons → children. Black and white, dense text, small print → adults or professionals.A children's science magazine vs an academic paper
Publication typeWhere was this published? A tabloid newspaper, a scientific journal, a government website, and a social media post all have different intended audiences.The Daily Mail vs The Lancet medical journal
⚠️
KS3 key idea: Audience and purpose together determine almost everything about how a text is written. Before you read any non-fiction text in an exam, identify BOTH — write them down. Then check every feature of the text against those two things. Does the writer's choice of words, structure, and tone serve their purpose for their audience?
⚖️ Fact vs Opinion vs Inference — The Critical Reading Triangle
💡
This is the most important skill in non-fiction reading. Many students lose marks because they cannot tell the difference between facts, opinions, and inferences. In an exam, you will always be asked about at least one of these.
✅ FACT
Can be checked — it is either true or false
"The Amazon rainforest covers about 5.5 million km²"
"The 2022 FIFA World Cup was held in Qatar"
"Plastic takes 450 years to decompose"
Facts use numbers, dates, specific names, and research findings
💬 OPINION
Expresses a personal view — cannot be proven true or false
"The Amazon rainforest is the most beautiful place on Earth"
"The 2022 World Cup should not have been held in Qatar"
"Plastic packaging is the most serious environmental problem"
Opinions often contain: "I believe," "in my view," "should," "must," "it is clear that," "obviously"
🔍 INFERENCE — Somewhere In Between
An inference is a conclusion that is not directly stated in the text but that you can reasonably work out from the evidence given. For example, if an article says "Sales of electric cars rose by 40% in a single year," you can infer that public interest in electric cars is growing — even though the article does not directly say this. Good non-fiction readers make inferences constantly. Always show the evidence from the text that led to your inference.
🔑
Spotting opinion disguised as fact: This is one of the most important skills a reader can have. Look for: value-loaded adjectives ("devastating," "brilliant," "shocking"), words that assign blame or praise without evidence, and statistics presented without a source. "Studies show that..." without naming which study is a common red flag. Always ask: How do they know this? Where did this come from?
🏗️ Structural Features of Non-Fiction Texts
📍
Non-fiction texts are organised deliberately. Understanding the structure helps you find key information quickly — which is especially important in timed reading exams where you cannot read every word carefully.
1
Headline and Subheading
The headline grabs attention and gives the main topic. Subheadings break the article into sections and help the reader navigate. Reading ONLY the headline and all subheadings will give you a rough outline of the whole article — useful when you need to find specific information quickly.
2
Opening Paragraph (the "Hook")
The first paragraph of a news article usually contains the most important information — answering the questions: WHO? WHAT? WHERE? WHEN? WHY? HOW? This is called the inverted pyramid structure — the most important information is at the top, and the detail comes after. In a feature article or opinion piece, the opening might be a dramatic anecdote or shocking statistic designed to pull the reader in emotionally.
3
Body Paragraphs — Evidence and Development
Each paragraph usually develops one point or piece of information. In good non-fiction, each paragraph has a clear TOPIC (the main point of that paragraph, usually in the first sentence). Evidence, statistics, and quotations from experts are used to support the points. Watch for the relationship between paragraphs — does the writer agree, contradict, build on, or contrast from paragraph to paragraph?
4
Captions, Images, and Graphs
These are NOT decoration — they carry information. A chart can communicate data that would take a whole paragraph to explain in words. A photograph can set tone and emotion. A caption (the small text below an image) sometimes contains information NOT in the main text. Always read captions carefully in an exam.
5
Concluding Paragraph
A news article's conclusion often ends with a broader context or a look ahead. An opinion piece usually ends with a call to action or a strong restatement of the writer's view. A report's conclusion usually contains recommendations. The conclusion tells you what the writer most wants you to take away from the text.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
📖
Builds on Week 6 — make sure you are comfortable converting between fractions, decimals, and percentages before starting ratios.
Core Vocabulary
ratio
A way of comparing two or more quantities by showing how many times one is bigger than the other. Written with a colon: 3:2 is read as "three to two." A ratio does NOT have units — it is a pure comparison. If there are 3 boys and 2 girls in a group, the ratio of boys to girls is 3:2.
proportion
A proportion tells you what fraction of a whole something is. In the group above, the proportion of boys is 3 out of 5 total = 3/5. Proportion compares a part to the WHOLE. Ratio compares parts to parts.
simplify a ratio
Dividing all parts of a ratio by their HCF (Highest Common Factor) to get the simplest form. Just as 4/8 simplifies to 1/2, the ratio 4:8 simplifies to 1:2. The relationship between the numbers stays the same — only the numbers get smaller.
equivalent ratios
Ratios that express the same relationship using different numbers. 1:2, 2:4, 5:10, and 50:100 are all equivalent ratios — they all say "one part to every two parts." Like equivalent fractions.
share in a ratio
Dividing a total amount between two or more people or groups according to a given ratio. For example, sharing £30 in the ratio 2:3 means one person gets 2 parts and the other gets 3 parts — out of 5 total parts.
unitary method
A method where you first find the value of ONE unit, then multiply to find any other amount. "Unitary" means "one unit." It is the most reliable method for solving proportion problems.
direct proportion
Two quantities are in direct proportion when they increase or decrease by the same factor. If you double one, the other doubles. If you halve one, the other halves. This is the foundation of the unitary method.
⚖️ What Is a Ratio? — Understanding It Deeply
💡
The most important thing to understand: A ratio compares PARTS to PARTS. A proportion compares a PART to the WHOLE. Many students mix these up. Let's be very clear.
RATIO — Part to Part
In a bag with 3 red balls and 5 blue balls, the ratio of red to blue is 3:5
This compares the red balls AGAINST the blue balls
Order matters: red to blue (3:5) is different from blue to red (5:3)
A ratio has no unit — it just shows the relationship
PROPORTION — Part to Whole
In the same bag, the proportion of red balls is 3/8 (3 out of 8 total)
This compares red balls against the TOTAL in the bag
Can also be written as a percentage: 3/8 = 37.5%
Proportion is a fraction — and you can convert it using Week 6 skills!
🎨 Visualising a Ratio — Paint Mixing Example

A painter mixes red and blue paint in the ratio 2:3. For every 2 parts red, there are 3 parts blue. Total parts = 5.

2 parts red

If the painter needs 500ml total: each "part" = 500 ÷ 5 = 100ml. Red = 2 × 100 = 200ml. Blue = 3 × 100 = 300ml. Check: 200 + 300 = 500ml ✓

✂️ Simplifying Ratios — Step by Step
📍
Simplifying a ratio works exactly like simplifying a fraction. Divide ALL parts of the ratio by the same number (their Highest Common Factor) until you cannot divide any further.
1
Write the ratio clearly
Make sure the ratio is in the right order — the question will tell you which comes first. For example: "Simplify the ratio 12:18" — write it out exactly as given.
2
Find the HCF (Highest Common Factor) of all parts
For 12:18 — the factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. The factors of 18 are 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. The HIGHEST factor in BOTH lists is 6. So HCF = 6.
3
Divide every part by the HCF
12 ÷ 6 = 2. 18 ÷ 6 = 3. So 12:18 simplifies to 2:3. You cannot simplify further because 2 and 3 have no common factor other than 1. This is the simplest form.
4
Check — are the numbers as small as possible?
Ask: "Is there any number I can divide both parts by?" If yes, you have not finished simplifying. If no — you are done. The simplified form is 2:3, which represents the same relationship as 12:18 but is much cleaner to work with.
— Worked Examples —
8:12 → HCF = 4 → 2:3
15:25 → HCF = 5 → 3:5
24:36:48 → HCF = 12 → 2:3:4 (three-part ratio, same method)
1.5:2.5 → ×2 to remove decimals → 3:5 → already simplified → 3:5
⚠️
Ratios with different units — ALWAYS convert first! "3cm to 1.5m" cannot be simplified as 3:1.5. First convert to the same unit: 3cm to 150cm. Then simplify: 3:150 → divide by 3 → 1:50. This is a common exam trap — always check that both (or all) parts of a ratio are in the same unit before simplifying.
🍰 Sharing in a Ratio — The Key Method
💡
The golden method — always use these 3 steps: (1) Find the total number of parts. (2) Find the value of ONE part. (3) Multiply to find each share. This works for EVERY sharing-in-a-ratio problem, no matter how complex.
📌 Worked Example 1 — Money

Problem: Share £60 between Aisha and Ben in the ratio 2:3.

Step 1 — Total parts: 2 + 3 = 5 parts total.

Step 2 — Value of one part: £60 ÷ 5 = £12 per part.

Step 3 — Each person's share:
Aisha = 2 × £12 = £24
Ben = 3 × £12 = £36

Check: £24 + £36 = £60 ✓

📌 Worked Example 2 — Three-way share

Problem: Share 120 marbles among Zara, Jake, and Leila in the ratio 1:2:3.

Step 1 — Total parts: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 parts.

Step 2 — Value of one part: 120 ÷ 6 = 20 marbles per part.

Step 3 — Each share:
Zara = 1 × 20 = 20
Jake = 2 × 20 = 40
Leila = 3 × 20 = 60

Check: 20 + 40 + 60 = 120 ✓

📌 Worked Example 3 — When You Know One Person's Share

Problem: Tom and Sam share some sweets in the ratio 3:5. Tom receives 24 sweets. How many sweets does Sam receive? How many sweets were there altogether?

Find value of one part: Tom's share = 3 parts = 24 sweets. So 1 part = 24 ÷ 3 = 8 sweets.

Sam's share: 5 × 8 = 40 sweets

Total: 24 + 40 = 64 sweets

⚠️ Notice: you did not need the total first here — you worked backwards from the known share. This is a common exam variation.

1️⃣ The Unitary Method — Find ONE, Then Scale
📍
The unitary method is the most powerful tool for proportion problems. The idea is simple: find the value of ONE unit first, then multiply (or divide) to find any amount you need. It works because quantities in direct proportion always change by the same factor.
1
Identify the two quantities and what you know
Look at what is given and what is asked. Write it out in a small table or two columns. Which quantity changes? Which direction are you going — scaling up or scaling down?
2
Divide to find the value of ONE unit
Divide the given amount by the number of units. This always gives you the rate — "per one." For example, if 5 pens cost £3.50, then one pen costs £3.50 ÷ 5 = £0.70.
3
Multiply by the required number of units
Once you know the value of one, multiply by however many you need. For example, to find the cost of 8 pens: £0.70 × 8 = £5.60. Always state the unit in your answer.
— Unitary Method Worked Examples —

Example 1: If 4 identical books cost £22, how much do 7 books cost?
Cost of 1 book: £22 ÷ 4 = £5.50
Cost of 7 books: £5.50 × 7 = £38.50

Example 2: A recipe uses 300g of flour for 12 biscuits. How much flour for 20 biscuits?
Flour for 1 biscuit: 300 ÷ 12 = 25g
Flour for 20 biscuits: 25 × 20 = 500g

Example 3: 3 workers take 8 days to finish a job. How many days would 6 workers take?
⚠️ CAREFUL: This is INVERSE proportion — more workers = FEWER days
Total work = 3 × 8 = 24 worker-days
With 6 workers: 24 ÷ 6 = 4 days
⚠️
Inverse proportion — the opposite relationship: In DIRECT proportion, more of one thing means more of the other (more pens = more cost). In INVERSE proportion, more of one thing means LESS of the other (more workers = fewer days). Always read the question carefully to identify which type you have before applying the unitary method.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Science Guide
Core Vocabulary for States of Matter
matter
Anything that has mass and takes up space. Everything you can see, touch, smell, or measure is made of matter. Air is matter. Water is matter. This book is matter.
particle
An extremely tiny piece of matter — so small you cannot see it with the naked eye. Everything in the universe is made of particles (atoms and molecules). The way particles are arranged and how much they move determines which state of matter something is in.
solid
A state of matter where particles are packed very closely together in a fixed arrangement. Solids have a definite shape and volume that do not change unless force is applied.
liquid
A state of matter where particles are close together but can flow past each other. Liquids have a definite volume but take the shape of whatever container they are placed in.
gas
A state of matter where particles are far apart and move very fast in all directions. Gases have no fixed shape or volume — they spread out to fill any container completely.
melting point
The specific temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. For water (ice), the melting point is 0°C. Every pure substance has its own unique melting point.
boiling point
The specific temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas (boils/evaporates rapidly throughout the whole liquid). For water, the boiling point is 100°C at sea level.
evaporation
When a liquid turns into a gas slowly at the surface, at temperatures BELOW the boiling point. Wet clothes dry by evaporation — the water does not need to boil, it just slowly escapes as gas from the surface.
condensation
When a gas turns into a liquid by cooling down. The water droplets on a cold glass of water on a hot day are condensation — water vapour from the air has cooled against the cold glass and turned back into liquid.
sublimation
When a solid turns directly into a gas WITHOUT going through the liquid stage. Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) sublimates — it goes straight from solid to gas, which is why it seems to "smoke" and disappear.
🔵🔴🟡 The Three States of Matter — Everything You Need to Know
📍
Key principle: All matter is made of particles. The state of matter (solid, liquid, or gas) depends entirely on two things: how far apart the particles are from each other, and how fast they are moving. Temperature controls both of these.
🧊 SOLID
Arranged: In tight, regular rows — very close together
Movement: Only vibrate on the spot — cannot move freely
Shape/Volume: Fixed shape, fixed volume
Compressible: No — particles already touching
💧 LIQUID
Arranged: Close together but randomly — no fixed pattern
Movement: Flow and slide past each other — faster than in a solid
Shape/Volume: No fixed shape — takes container's shape. Fixed volume
Compressible: Not really — particles still close
💨 GAS
Arranged: Spread very far apart — random and widely spaced
Movement: Move very fast, in all directions, bouncing off each other and the container walls
Shape/Volume: No fixed shape, no fixed volume — fills any container completely
Compressible: Yes — lots of empty space between particles

🔄 Changes of State — Every Change Has a Name
💡
The golden principle of changes of state: When a substance changes state, it is NOT a new substance — it is the SAME substance with its particles at a different energy level. Water, ice, and steam are all H₂O. Only the arrangement and movement of the particles changes. The substance itself stays the same.
♻️
Changes of state are REVERSIBLE physical changes — they are not chemical changes. You can melt ice into water and freeze it back again endlessly. The water molecules are not changed or destroyed — just rearranged. This is different from a chemical change, like burning wood, where new substances are formed.
SOLID → melting → LIQUID → boiling/evaporation → GAS
GAS → condensation → LIQUID → freezing → SOLID
SOLID → sublimation → GAS (skipping the liquid stage entirely)
ProcessWhat ChangesHeat Absorbed or Released?Everyday Example

⚠️
Evaporation vs Boiling — what is the difference? Both turn liquid to gas — but evaporation happens at the surface only, at ANY temperature below boiling point, and slowly. Boiling happens throughout the entire liquid, only at the boiling point, and rapidly. You can evaporate water at room temperature — wet clothes prove this — but you cannot boil water at room temperature.
📈 Heating & Cooling Curves — Reading the Graph
📍
This is a favourite exam topic. A heating curve is a graph showing how the temperature of a substance changes as you continuously heat it. The shape of the curve is the same for all pure substances — what changes is the specific temperatures (melting and boiling points).
📊 What a Heating Curve for Water Looks Like (Description)

Section A — Rising line (Solid heating up): Temperature increases as the ice warms up. All particles are solid, vibrating faster and faster.

Section B — Flat line at 0°C (Melting — SOLID to LIQUID): Temperature stays FLAT even though you keep adding heat. This is because all the energy is being used to BREAK THE BONDS between particles (so they can move freely as a liquid) — not to increase temperature. This is called latent heat of fusion. The flat line always happens at the melting point.

Section C — Rising line (Liquid heating up): Temperature increases again. The particles (now liquid) are moving faster and faster.

Section D — Flat line at 100°C (Boiling — LIQUID to GAS): Temperature stays FLAT again. All energy is used to overcome the remaining forces between liquid particles so they can escape as gas. This is latent heat of vaporisation. This flat line happens at the boiling point.

Section E — Rising line (Gas heating up): Temperature increases again as the steam (gas) gets hotter.

🔑
The most important exam point about heating curves: The flat lines on a heating curve show a CHANGE OF STATE happening. During a change of state, the temperature does NOT change — even though you are continuously adding heat. This is because all the energy goes into changing the state, not raising the temperature. If you see a flat line on a heating curve, a change of state is occurring at that temperature.
💡
Cooling curve — exactly the reverse: If you graph the temperature as a substance cools down, you get the mirror image — two flat lines where the substance gives OUT latent heat as it changes from gas to liquid (condensation) and from liquid to solid (freezing). The flat lines still happen at the same temperatures — 100°C and 0°C for water.
⚛️ The Particle Model — What It Tells Us and Its Limitations
📍
The particle model of matter is a scientific model — a simplified way of explaining how matter behaves. Like all models, it is useful but not perfect. Understanding both what it explains well and where it falls short is a sign of strong scientific thinking.
✅ What the Particle Model Explains Well
Why solids keep their shape (particles locked in position)
Why liquids flow and take the shape of a container (particles move freely but stay close)
Why gases spread out to fill space (particles move fast and far apart)
Why gases can be compressed but liquids and solids cannot
Why heating increases temperature (particles move faster)
Why changes of state happen at specific temperatures
⚠️ Limitations of the Model
The model treats all particles as identical small spheres — real atoms have complex shapes and charges
It does not show forces between particles — why do particles attract or repel each other?
It does not explain why some substances dissolve in each other and others do not
Plasma — a fourth state of matter found in stars and lightning — is not explained by the basic model
It does not account for quantum mechanical behaviour of very small particles
✅
KS3 key idea: The fact that a model has limitations does not mean it is wrong or useless. A map of your school is a model — it doesn't show every brick, every person, every pipe in the wall, but it is still extremely useful for finding your way around. The particle model is one of the most useful tools in chemistry. We use it because it works well for explaining most of what we observe.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL History & Geography Guide
📍
Builds on Week 6's physical geography — you already know the seven continents and major landforms. This week we zoom in on climate and living systems.
Core Vocabulary
weather
The day-to-day conditions of the atmosphere in a particular place — temperature, rainfall, wind, and sunshine on a specific day. Weather can change quickly. "It is raining today" is a weather observation.
climate
The long-term, average weather patterns of a region over at least 30 years. Climate is predictable and stable. "Pakistan has hot, dry summers and mild winters" is a climate description. A useful saying: Climate is what you expect; weather is what you get.
climate zone
A large region of Earth that has a broadly similar climate. The three main zones are: tropical (near the equator — hot and wet), polar (near the North and South Poles — freezing cold), and temperate (in between — moderate seasons). There are also sub-zones: arid, semi-arid, Mediterranean, monsoon, etc.
biome
A large area of Earth characterised by specific climate conditions AND specific plant and animal life that have adapted to those conditions. A biome is both a climate AND an ecosystem. The same biome can appear on different continents — tropical rainforests exist in South America, Africa, and South-East Asia.
equator
The imaginary horizontal line (0° latitude) that runs around the exact middle of the Earth, equidistant from both poles. The equator is where the sun's rays hit most directly, making it the hottest part of the Earth. Tropical biomes are found near the equator.
latitude
Distance north or south of the equator, measured in degrees (0° to 90°). Latitude is the most important factor in determining climate — areas closer to the equator (low latitude) receive more direct sunlight and are warmer. Areas closer to the poles (high latitude) receive less direct sunlight and are colder.
precipitation
All forms of water that fall from the sky — rain, snow, sleet, and hail. Precipitation is a key factor in distinguishing between different biomes. A tropical rainforest can receive over 2,000mm of precipitation per year; a desert receives less than 250mm.
adaptation
A physical feature or behaviour that helps a living thing survive in its environment. Each biome's plants and animals have specific adaptations that suit that climate. Polar bears have thick fur; cacti have thick, water-storing stems. Adaptation links directly to this week's Science lesson on particles — even life adapts to matter and energy.
vegetation
The plants that grow naturally in a particular area. Vegetation is one of the defining characteristics of a biome — it both reflects the climate and shapes the habitat for animals.
☀️🌧️ Climate vs Weather — Why the Difference Matters
💡
This is the first and most important distinction in climate geography. Students consistently mix these two up. If you remember only one thing from this introduction, make it this: Weather is short-term. Climate is long-term.
🌩️ WEATHER — Short Term
Changes from day to day — sometimes from hour to hour
"It is cloudy and 18°C in Rawalpindi today"
Measured with thermometers, rain gauges, anemometers (wind speed)
Difficult to predict more than about 10 days ahead
A single unusual week does not change the climate description of a place
🌡️ CLIMATE — Long Term
The average weather patterns over at least 30 years
"Pakistan has a monsoon season every year from July to September"
Measured by averaging decades of weather data
Reliable enough to design buildings, plan farming, and classify biomes
Climate change refers to long-term shifts in these patterns — not just one hot summer
⚠️
Common misunderstanding: People sometimes say "it was cold last winter — what happened to global warming?" This confuses weather (one cold winter) with climate (the long-term pattern over decades). A single cold year does not disprove a long-term warming trend, just as a single hot day in winter does not prove anything about climate. You need at least 30 years of data to identify a climate trend.

🌡️ What Affects the Climate of a Place? — Beyond Latitude
💡
Latitude is the MOST important factor, but it is not the ONLY one. Two places at the same latitude can have very different climates. Here are the other key factors — and understanding them helps explain many geographical puzzles.
1
Latitude — Distance from the Equator
The most powerful factor. Near the equator, the sun's rays hit Earth at a steep angle — concentrated, intense, and warm. Near the poles, the same rays hit at a shallow angle, spread over a larger area — less concentrated and much colder. This is why the equator is hot and the poles are freezing, regardless of season.
2
Altitude — Height Above Sea Level
The higher up you go, the colder it gets. Air pressure decreases with altitude, and air holds less heat. Temperature drops approximately 6.5°C for every 1,000 metres of elevation. This is why mountains are cold even at the equator — the peak of Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania (near the equator) has permanent snow and ice despite being in Africa. It is the same principle that creates the cold "biomes" seen on mountain tops in tropical countries.
3
Distance from the Sea — Maritime vs Continental
The sea warms up and cools down very slowly. Areas near the coast (maritime climates) have mild, wet weather year-round — cooler summers and warmer winters than inland areas. Areas far from the sea (continental climates) have extreme temperature ranges — very hot summers and very cold winters. Compare London (mild and wet) with Moscow (same latitude, but far colder winters and hotter summers). The UK's island position gives it a typical maritime climate.
4
Ocean Currents — Rivers of Heat in the Sea
Ocean currents are like giant rivers flowing through the ocean. Warm currents carry heat from the tropics toward the poles — making coastal areas warmer than they would otherwise be. The Gulf Stream carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico across the Atlantic to Western Europe. This is why the UK, at the same latitude as parts of Canada that are buried in snow, has a relatively mild climate. Cold currents have the reverse effect.
5
Prevailing Winds — Which Direction Does the Wind Come From?
Winds that regularly blow from the same direction carry moisture or dryness from wherever they have been. Winds that have blown over large oceans carry moisture — bringing rain to coasts. Winds that have blown over large deserts or cold land masses carry dry, extreme air. This is why one side of a mountain range can be wet and green while the other side is dry — a phenomenon called the rain shadow effect: mountains force air up, causing rain on one side, and dry air descends on the other.

💡
The big geographical idea: Biomes are not fixed forever. They shift slowly as climate changes. Over the last 100 years, human activity has accelerated climate change, which is causing biomes to shift — tundra is shrinking, deserts are expanding, rainforests are being deforested. This is one of the most important issues in 21st-century geography, and it connects to next week's History lesson on human impact on the environment.
