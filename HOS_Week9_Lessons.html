📖 Bible Studies · Week 9
Joshua —
Courage & Promise
Joshua 1, 3-6 · Entering the Promised Land · God Keeps His Promises · Be Strong and Courageous
Joshua 1:9Joshua 3-6CourageKS3 Level 6
⭐ Memory Verse
"Have I not commanded you? Be strong and courageous. Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged, for the LORD your God will be with you wherever you go."
Joshua 1:9 — NIV
🧠
Word-by-word: "Be strong and courageous" is repeated FOUR times in Joshua 1 (verses 6, 7, 9, 18) — God knew Joshua needed to hear it again and again. "Do not be afraid" does not mean Joshua felt no fear — it means fear should not be what controls his decisions. "Wherever you go" — the promise of God's presence is not limited to easy places; it covers every step into the unknown.
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Learn these words before you continue
successor
The person who takes over a role or position after someone else. Joshua became Moses' successor as leader of Israel.
Promised Land
The land of Canaan, which God promised to give to Abraham's descendants (Genesis 12). Israel had waited generations to enter it.
covenant
A serious, binding agreement between God and his people. The Promised Land was part of God's covenant with Abraham.
ark of the covenant
A sacred wooden chest that represented God's presence with Israel. The priests carried it ahead of the people at the Jordan and at Jericho.
spies / scouts
People sent secretly to gather information about an enemy or unfamiliar land before an army arrives.
siege
A military operation in which an army surrounds a city to force it to surrender, often by cutting off supplies.
consecrate
To set apart as holy or dedicated to God. Joshua told the people to consecrate themselves before crossing the Jordan.
inheritance
Something passed down or given, often a possession that becomes permanently yours. The land of Canaan was Israel's inheritance.
🔄 From Moses to Joshua — A New Leader, the Same God
📍
Moses had led Israel out of Egypt and through 40 years in the wilderness, but he died before entering Canaan (Deuteronomy 34). Joshua, who had been Moses' assistant and one of the twelve spies sent into Canaan 40 years earlier (Numbers 13-14), was chosen by God to lead the people into the Promised Land.
👤 What Stayed the Same
God's promise to give Israel the land
The need for courage and obedience
God's presence going with the leader
The Law given through Moses still applied
🔄 What Changed
A new human leader: Joshua, not Moses
From wilderness wandering to active conquest
From receiving the law to living it out in the land
From promise spoken to promise being fulfilled
🔑
KS3 thinking point: God's first words to Joshua are not a battle plan — they are a repeated command to be courageous (Joshua 1:6,7,9,18). Leadership transitions are tested moments; God meets Joshua's uncertainty with reassurance of his unchanging presence.
🌊 Crossing the Jordan — Joshua 3-4
Step 1
The people consecrate themselves
Joshua tells Israel to prepare spiritually because "tomorrow the LORD will do amazing things among you" (3:5).
Step 2
The priests step into the river first
Carrying the ark of the covenant, the priests step into the flooded Jordan — and the water stops flowing, piling up upstream (3:13-16).
Step 3
All Israel crosses on dry ground
The entire nation crosses the riverbed while the priests stand still in the middle holding the ark (3:17).
Step 4
Twelve stones set up as a memorial
One stone is taken from the riverbed for each of Israel's twelve tribes, set up so future generations would ask "what do these stones mean?" and be told the story (4:1-7).
✅
KS3 key idea: This crossing deliberately echoes the Red Sea crossing under Moses (Exodus 14) — both times, water stops for Israel to cross on dry ground. The repetition shows the SAME God who acted through Moses is acting through Joshua.
🏰 The Battle of Jericho — Joshua 6
⚠️
God's instructions for taking Jericho were strange by any normal military standard: march around the walled city once a day for six days, then seven times on the seventh day, blow trumpets, and shout. No weapons. No siege engines. Just obedience.
1
Six days of marching
Armed men, seven priests with trumpets, and the ark circle the city once each day — in silence except for the trumpets.
2
The seventh day — seven times around
On the final day, Israel marches around Jericho seven times.
3
The shout and the trumpets
On the seventh circuit, the priests blow the trumpets, and Joshua commands the people to shout — and the walls of Jericho collapse (6:20).
📍
KS3 key idea: The victory came through obedience to an unusual instruction, not through Israel's military strength. The story repeatedly emphasises that the LAND was God's gift, not Israel's achievement — a major theme of the whole book of Joshua.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Master these terms for speaking and listening
presentation
A talk given to an audience to inform, explain, or persuade — usually with visual support like slides or notes.
audience
The people who are listening to a presentation or speech. A good speaker thinks about what the audience already knows and needs.
active listening
Fully concentrating on, understanding, and responding to a speaker — not just hearing words, but engaging with meaning. Shown through eye contact, nodding, and relevant questions.
articulation
Speaking clearly so each word and sound can be understood. Good articulation means not mumbling or rushing words together.
pace
The speed at which you speak. Too fast = audience cannot follow. Too slow = audience loses interest. Good pace varies for emphasis.
eye contact
Looking at members of your audience while speaking, rather than only at notes or the floor. Builds connection and trust.
visual aid
An image, slide, chart, or object used to support and clarify a spoken presentation.
filler words
Unnecessary sounds or words used when thinking, such as "um," "like," or "you know." Reducing fillers makes a speaker sound more confident.
🏗️ Structuring a Short Oral Presentation
💡
A spoken presentation needs the SAME logical structure as a written essay — but it must be even clearer, because listeners cannot re-read a sentence they missed. Tell them what you will say, say it, then tell them what you said.
1
Opening — grab attention and state your purpose
Start with a question, surprising fact, or short story. Then tell the audience clearly what your presentation is about: "Today I'm going to explain..."
2
Main points — usually 2-4 clear points
Signpost each point clearly: "My first point is..." / "Next, I want to talk about..." / "Finally..." This helps listeners follow your structure without seeing it written down.
3
Closing — summarise and end strongly
Briefly restate your main points. End with a strong final sentence — a call to action, a memorable statement, or an invitation for questions.
🗣️ Delivery Skills — How You Say It Matters

👂 Active Listening — The Other Half of Communication
🔑
Golden idea: Communication is a two-way skill. A presentation only succeeds if the audience listens actively — and being a good audience member is a skill you can practise, just like speaking.
✅ Active Listening Looks Like
Making eye contact with the speaker
Nodding or showing facial reactions
Asking relevant follow-up questions
Waiting for pauses before speaking/asking
Summarising what you heard to check understanding
❌ Passive / Poor Listening Looks Like
Looking at a phone or elsewhere
Interrupting before the speaker finishes a point
Thinking about your own response instead of listening
Asking a question already answered (not listening)
Showing visible boredom or distraction
📝 Worked Example — A Well-Structured Opening
[HOOK]Imagine walking around a city wall seven times in one day, blowing trumpets, with no weapon in your hand — and watching the wall collapse. [PURPOSE]Today I'm going to explain the story of Joshua at Jericho, and what it teaches us about obedience and courage. [SIGNPOST]I'll cover three things: first, who Joshua was; second, what happened at Jericho; and third, what this story means for us today.

EquationsBalancing MethodInverse OperationsKS3 Level 6
📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Guide
Builds on Week 8 — algebraic expressions
equation
A mathematical statement that says two things are EQUAL, using an "=" sign. Example: x + 5 = 12.
solve
To find the value of the unknown variable that makes the equation true.
balance / balancing method
Doing the SAME operation to BOTH sides of an equation, so it stays equal — like keeping two sides of a scale level.
inverse operation
The operation that "undoes" another. The inverse of + is −. The inverse of × is ÷.
one-step equation
An equation that can be solved using just ONE inverse operation. Example: x + 3 = 10 (one step: subtract 3).
check / verify
Substituting your answer back into the original equation to make sure both sides are equal.
⚖️ The Balancing Method — The Big Idea
🔑
Golden rule: An equation is like a balanced scale. Whatever you do to ONE side, you must do EXACTLY the same to the OTHER side — otherwise the scale (the equation) tips over and is no longer true.
💡
To solve an equation, you want to get the variable ALONE on one side. You do this by using the INVERSE operation to "undo" whatever is being done to the variable.
If the equation has...Use this inverse operationExample
x + 5 = 12 (addition)Subtract 5 from both sidesx = 12 − 5 = 7
x − 4 = 9 (subtraction)Add 4 to both sidesx = 9 + 4 = 13
3x = 21 (multiplication)Divide both sides by 3x = 21 ÷ 3 = 7
x ÷ 5 = 6 (division)Multiply both sides by 5x = 6 × 5 = 30
➕➖ Solving Addition & Subtraction Equations
Example 1: Solve x + 8 = 15
x + 8 − 8 = 15 − 8 ← subtract 8 from BOTH sides
x = 7

Example 2: Solve y − 6 = 11
y − 6 + 6 = 11 + 6 ← add 6 to BOTH sides
y = 17
✅
Always check your answer: Substitute back in. For x + 8 = 15: 7 + 8 = 15 ✓. This confirms your answer is correct.
✖️➗ Solving Multiplication & Division Equations
Example 3: Solve 4x = 28
4x ÷ 4 = 28 ÷ 4 ← divide BOTH sides by 4
x = 7

Example 4: Solve n ÷ 3 = 9
n ÷ 3 × 3 = 9 × 3 ← multiply BOTH sides by 3
n = 27
⚠️
Common mistake: For "4x = 28," students sometimes try to subtract 4. But 4x means 4 MULTIPLIED by x — the inverse of multiplication is division, not subtraction. Always identify the operation correctly first.
🌍 Equations in Real Life — Word Problems
1
Read the problem and identify the unknown
"A number plus 9 equals 23. What is the number?" → Let the number be n. Equation: n + 9 = 23.
2
Write the equation
n + 9 = 23
3
Solve using the balancing method
n + 9 − 9 = 23 − 9 → n = 14
4
Check your answer makes sense
14 + 9 = 23 ✓. The answer fits the original word problem.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL Science Guide
Builds on Week 8 — mixtures and pure substances
element
A pure substance made of only ONE type of atom. It cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical means. There are 118 known elements.
atom
The smallest particle of an element that still has the properties of that element. Atoms are the building blocks of all matter.
compound
A substance made of TWO OR MORE different elements chemically joined together. Example: water (H₂O) = hydrogen + oxygen.
chemical symbol
A one- or two-letter code representing an element. The first letter is always capital. Example: O = oxygen, Na = sodium, Fe = iron.
periodic table
A chart that organises all known elements by their properties — arranged in rows (periods) and columns (groups).
group
A vertical COLUMN in the periodic table. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties.
period
A horizontal ROW in the periodic table. Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells.
metal / non-metal
Metals: shiny, conduct electricity/heat, malleable (bendable). Non-metals: dull, poor conductors, brittle. The periodic table is divided into both types.

📚 Key Vocabulary — ESL History Guide
Learn these words before exploring medieval Europe
feudalism / feudal system
A system of medieval Europe in which land was exchanged for loyalty and service. Kings gave land to nobles, who gave protection and land to knights, who were served by peasants/serfs.
monarch
A king or queen — the supreme ruler of a kingdom, believed in medieval times to rule with authority from God.
lord / noble
A person of high social rank who owned and controlled land granted by the monarch, in exchange for military service and loyalty.
vassal
A person who received land from a lord in exchange for a pledge of loyalty and military service. A vassal could also be a lord to others below them.
knight
A warrior who served a lord, fighting on horseback in armour, in exchange for land. Knights followed a code of honour called chivalry.
serf / peasant
A person at the bottom of the feudal system who worked the land. Serfs were legally bound to the lord's land and could not leave without permission.
manor
A large estate of land controlled by a lord, including the lord's house, farmland, and the village where serfs lived and worked.
clergy
Officials of the Church — priests, bishops, monks. The medieval Church was extremely powerful and wealthy, often rivalling kings in influence.
🏛️ The Feudal Pyramid — How Medieval Society Was Organised
💡
After the fall of Rome (Week 4) and during centuries of instability, feudalism developed as a system to organise land, loyalty, and protection in the absence of strong central government.
MONARCH (King/Queen) — owns all land
↓ grants land for loyalty
LORDS / NOBLES — control large estates
↓ grants land for military service
KNIGHTS — fight for their lord
↓ protects and rules over
SERFS / PEASANTS — farm the land
⚠️
KS3 key idea: This was NOT a system of equal exchange. The king technically owned all land; everyone below received the RIGHT to use land in exchange for service — but serfs at the bottom had almost no rights or freedom, and could not leave the lord's land.
⚔️ Knights and Chivalry
Aspect of KnighthoodDetail
TrainingBoys often began training as a "page" around age 7, became a "squire" around 14, and could be knighted around 21 after years of training in combat and horsemanship.
EquipmentA knight needed armour, a warhorse, and weapons — extremely expensive, which is why knighthood was usually limited to noble families.
Obligation to lordIn exchange for land, a knight owed his lord a set number of days of military service each year (often 40 days).
Code of chivalryAn ideal code of conduct: bravery in battle, loyalty to one's lord, courtesy, and protection of the weak — though in reality, behaviour varied widely.
💡
ESL tip: "Chivalry" comes from the French word "cheval" (horse) — a knight (chevalier) was originally simply "a horseman." The idea of chivalry as a moral code of honour developed alongside the practical role of mounted warrior.
🌾 Life as a Serf
⚠️
The majority of medieval Europeans — perhaps 90% — were peasants, and most of those were serfs. Their daily life was extremely hard, structured entirely around farming and obligation to their lord.
1
Working the land
Serfs farmed strips of land assigned to them, but had to give a portion of their crops to the lord and also work several days a week on the lord's own fields for free.
2
Legal bondage to the land
Unlike free peasants, serfs could not leave the manor or marry without the lord's permission — they were tied to the land they worked.
3
Protection in exchange
In return for their labour, serfs received protection from the lord (defence against raids and bandits) and the right to farm a small plot for their own family's food.
⛪ The Power of the Medieval Church
⛪ Religious Role
Conducted baptisms, weddings, funerals — central to every life stage
Ran monasteries — centres of prayer, learning, and copying books
Taught that obedience to God and the Church secured salvation
🏛️ Political & Economic Role
Owned vast amounts of land — often more than any single king
Collected "tithes" (a tenth of income) from the population
Could crown or excommunicate (reject) kings, giving it huge power
✅
KS3 key idea: In medieval Europe, religious and political power were deeply intertwined. The Church was not simply a place of worship — it was one of the most powerful landowning institutions in Europe, sometimes in direct competition with kings for authority.
