🟢 EasySection A — Recall and comprehension. What happened and when?
📚 Recall: Joseph's Two Dreams
💡 ESL Tip: Interpret means to explain the hidden meaning of something — like a dream, a symbol, or a difficult text. Joseph's gift was not just predicting the future; it was correctly understanding the meaning that God had placed inside the dream. This is why the word interpretation appears so many times in Genesis 40–41.
1
Describe BOTH of Joseph's dreams from Genesis 37:5–11 in your own words. For each dream, explain what the symbols were and what they represented. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Dream One (Gen 37:6–7): Joseph and his brothers were binding sheaves of grain in a field. Joseph's sheaf suddenly stood upright, and his brothers' sheaves gathered around it and bowed down to it. The sheaves represent Joseph and his brothers — agricultural workers, as the family was. His sheaf standing upright while theirs bowed represents Joseph rising to authority and his brothers becoming subordinate to him. Dream Two (Gen 37:9–10): The sun, moon, and eleven stars all bowed down to Joseph. The sun and moon represent his father Jacob and his mother; the eleven stars represent his eleven brothers. Even his father was troubled by this dream — it suggested Joseph would have authority not just over siblings but over his parents. Both dreams pointed to the same outcome: Joseph in a position of great authority over his whole family. This came true, but only about twenty-two years later.

What does the word "steward" mean in this context? In what TWO roles does Joseph serve as a steward during the story? 3 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A steward is a person trusted to manage someone else's affairs or property on their behalf — the owner retains ultimate authority, but the steward has day-to-day responsibility. In the story: Role 1 — Steward to Potiphar (Gen 39): Joseph is trusted with everything Potiphar owns; Potiphar has no concern about anything except the food he eats. Role 2 — Steward of Egypt (Gen 41): Joseph manages the entire food supply of Egypt for Pharaoh — collecting grain during seven years of plenty and distributing it during seven years of famine. Both roles show Joseph as someone whose integrity makes him trustworthy with enormous responsibility that ultimately belongs to someone above him.
Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Analysis and explanation. Think carefully about WHY.
🔍 Integrity, Injustice & God's Purpose
4
Genesis 39 says: "The LORD was with Joseph." This phrase appears TWICE — once when Joseph is a slave in Potiphar's house, and once when he is in prison. What does this repeated phrase teach us about HOW God's presence works in Joseph's story? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The phrase "the LORD was with Joseph" does NOT mean Joseph's circumstances were comfortable — he was enslaved and then imprisoned, which are among the worst situations a person can be in. Instead, it means God's presence expressed itself through Joseph prospering within his circumstances: Potiphar noticed it, the prison warden noticed it, and both gave Joseph more and more responsibility. God's presence is revealed not by removing the hard situation but by enabling Joseph to function with integrity and effectiveness inside it. The repetition — once in Potiphar's house, once in prison — teaches something vital: God's presence is not withdrawn when injustice is done to a faithful person. The false accusation did not mean God had abandoned Joseph; it meant God's presence was now operating in a different setting. This directly challenges any assumption that suffering = God's absence. For Joseph, the hardest places were also the places where God's presence was explicitly noted by the narrator.
5
Joseph shows integrity when Potiphar's wife tries to tempt him (Gen 39:9). His reason for refusing is NOT "I'll get in trouble" — it's something deeper. What reason does he actually give? What does this tell us about where his moral code comes from? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Joseph says: "How then could I do such a wicked thing and sin against God?" (Gen 39:9). His reason is not "I'll lose my job" or "Potiphar will punish me" — it is that doing so would be a sin against God. This reveals that Joseph's moral code is theologically rooted, not merely consequentialist (based on what happens to him as a result). He is not acting rightly because virtue is rewarded (indeed, his virtue is immediately punished by false accusation) — he is acting rightly because he understands himself as accountable to God regardless of circumstances. This is exactly what the lesson's definition of integrity captures: doing what is right even when no one is watching and even when it costs you something. For Joseph, doing right cost him prison. But his refusal is not shaken — it is grounded in something more solid than personal outcome.
6
The lesson says Joseph was "forgotten" after correctly interpreting the cupbearer's dream in prison (Gen 40:23). Two years passed. Why does this detail matter to the story's message about providence and timing? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Joseph correctly interpreted the cupbearer's dream and asked him: "But when all goes well with you, remember me... mention me to Pharaoh and get me out of this prison" (Gen 40:14). The cupbearer was restored exactly as Joseph said — and then forgot Joseph for two full years (Gen 40:23). Joseph did everything right: he correctly used his God-given gift, made a reasonable request, and waited. And nothing happened. This two-year delay matters enormously because it removes any sense that providence works on a human schedule. Joseph had a perfectly reasonable expectation of being released and it did not happen. The delay teaches that God's timing is not adjustable to human convenience — and it also ensures that when Joseph is finally brought before Pharaoh, it is specifically because Pharaoh needs a dream interpreted, not because of any human initiative. The moment is perfectly set: Pharaoh's dream, Joseph's gift, the famine on its way. The cupbearer's forgetfulness was not a failure of providence; it was part of its timing.

🏆 Extended Analysis
7
The lesson defines providence as "God's wise, unseen guidance over events — often only recognisable looking backward." Using at least FOUR specific events from Joseph's story, show how each individual event seemed negative or random at the time but was later revealed to be part of a larger purpose. Write at least 6 sentences. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Each stage of Joseph's story that appeared to be a dead-end was actually a doorway to the next stage of God's purpose: Event 1 — Being sold by his brothers (Gen 37): Appeared to be total abandonment and betrayal. It was actually the means of transport to Egypt — the very country where his destiny would unfold. Without being sold, he would never have reached Egypt. Event 2 — Serving Potiphar (Gen 39): Appeared to be permanent slavery. It was actually a training ground in administration and management at a senior level — skills he would need to govern Egypt's entire food supply. Event 3 — False accusation and imprisonment (Gen 39): Appeared to be the worst possible outcome after doing the right thing. It was actually placement in the prison where royal officials were kept — the only prison where he would meet the cupbearer who would eventually remember him to Pharaoh. Event 4 — Two years of being forgotten (Gen 40–41): Appeared to be God ignoring a faithful servant's reasonable request. It was actually precise timing — Joseph was brought to Pharaoh exactly when Pharaoh had a dream that no one could interpret and Egypt needed a plan for the coming famine. Looking back, no event was wasted. Providence does not mean God makes everything comfortable; it means God makes everything purposeful — but this is only visible in retrospect.
8
Write a short reflective paragraph (at least 5 sentences) from Joseph's imagined perspective at his lowest point — in prison, forgotten by the cupbearer. What might he have been thinking? How might he have held on to faith? Then write a second paragraph explaining what this teaches a reader today. 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Joseph's imagined perspective: "Two years now. I did what was right — I refused her, I served faithfully, I interpreted correctly and asked only to be remembered. And still I am here. The same walls, the same chains, the same silence. Perhaps the dreams were wrong. Perhaps I misunderstood them. Perhaps I am not who I thought I was. Joseph's lowest point was also the point closest to his breakthrough. Anyone who has worked faithfully, done the right thing, and been overlooked for what seems like too long will find in Joseph's story something more than comfort — a framework for understanding that faithfulness in obscurity is not wasted, even when it feels like it might be.
9
Evaluate this statement: "Forgiveness in Joseph's story is only possible because he had power over his brothers. Without that power, he could not have forgiven them." Do you agree or disagree? Use evidence from the lesson to support your view. Write at least 4 sentences. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
This statement has some surface logic — Joseph does have enormous power when he forgives — but the lesson's content suggests it misunderstands the nature of Joseph's forgiveness. The lesson notes that forgiveness does NOT mean pretending the wrong didn't happen; it means "choosing not to let that wrong control how you treat the person now." Joseph could have used his power to take revenge (it was entirely within his authority), but he explicitly told his brothers not to be angry with themselves, wept, and invited them to come live safely in Goshen. The forgiveness came from inside him — not from the power itself. If power were the source of forgiveness, powerful people would forgive more easily than powerless people, which is clearly not the case in the real world. What enabled Joseph's forgiveness was his theological framework — his understanding that "God intended it for good" — which reframed the story. That framework was available to him whether or not he was Pharaoh's second-in-command. Power made the forgiveness visible and consequential; it did not generate it.

Day 2Harder ProblemsKS3 Level 6
🟢 EasySection A — Consolidating conversion and percentage-of-amount skills.
📚 Consolidation: Conversions & Percentage of Amount
💡 ESL Tip: Percentage change asks you to describe HOW MUCH something has changed relative to the original — expressed as a percentage. The formula is always: change ÷ original × 100. The original is always the starting value, never the new value.
1
Without a calculator, work out each percentage of an amount. Show the mental shortcut method (build up from 10%): 6 marks

a) 15% of 240    b) 45% of 80    c) 22% of 150    d) 75% of 360    e) 5% of 460    f) 110% of 90
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) 10% = 24; 5% = 12; 15% = 24 + 12 = 36. b) 10% = 8; 40% = 32; 5% = 4; 45% = 32 + 4 = 36. c) 10% = 15; 20% = 30; 2% = 3; 22% = 30 + 3 = 33. d) 50% = 180; 25% = 90; 75% = 180 + 90 = 270. e) 10% = 46; 5% = 23. Answer: £23. f) 100% = 90; 10% = 9; 110% = 90 + 9 = 99.

🟡 MediumSection B — Percentage change and comparing percentages.
🔍 Percentage Change & Comparison
2
Calculate the percentage change in each case. State whether it is an increase or decrease. Show working: change ÷ original × 100. 6 marks

a) Price rises from £40 to £52
b) Population falls from 8,000 to 6,800
c) Temperature drops from 20°C to 15°C
d) Score improves from 48 to 60
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
a) Change = £12. 12 ÷ 40 × 100 = 30% increase. b) Change = 1,200. 1200 ÷ 8000 × 100 = 15% decrease. c) Change = 5°C. 5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25% decrease. d) Change = 12. 12 ÷ 48 × 100 = 25% increase. Remember: always divide by the ORIGINAL (starting) value, not the new value.
3
Two students take the same test twice. Aisha scored 48 in Test 1 and 60 in Test 2. Ben scored 55 in Test 1 and 66 in Test 2. (a) Calculate each student's percentage improvement. (b) Who improved more in percentage terms? (c) Who scored higher overall in Test 2? (d) Explain why percentage change and raw score change can give different answers about "who improved more." 6 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Aisha: Change = 12. 12 ÷ 48 × 100 = 25%. Ben: Change = 11. 11 ÷ 55 × 100 = 20%. (b) Aisha improved more in percentage terms (25% vs 20%). (c) Ben scored higher in Test 2 (66 vs 60). (d) Percentage change measures improvement relative to the starting point. Ben started higher, so adding 11 points represented a smaller proportion of what he already had. Aisha started lower, so adding 12 points represented a larger proportion of her starting score. This is why raw score improvement and percentage improvement can tell different stories — and why mathematicians are careful to specify which measure they are using when discussing growth or change.

🔴 HardSection C — Reverse percentages and multi-step reasoning.
🏆 Reverse Percentages & Extended Reasoning
4
A jacket is on sale for £63 after a 30% reduction. What was the original price? Show full working using the multiplier method. Explain in one sentence why you CANNOT just add 30% onto £63 to get the answer. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
After a 30% reduction, the sale price is 70% of the original. So £63 = 70% of the original price. As a decimal: £63 = 0.70 × original. Original price = 63 ÷ 0.70 = £90. Check: 30% of £90 = £27. £90 − £27 = £63. ✓ Why you cannot add 30% to £63: The 30% reduction was taken from the ORIGINAL price (£90), not from the sale price (£63). Adding 30% of £63 gives you 30% of the wrong number — you must work back from the sale price by dividing, not forward by adding.
5
A shop adds 20% VAT to all prices. After VAT, a TV costs £480. What was the pre-VAT price? Show full method. 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
After 20% VAT, the customer pays 120% of the pre-VAT price. £480 = 1.20 × pre-VAT price. Pre-VAT price = 480 ÷ 1.20 = £400. Check: 20% of £400 = £80. £400 + £80 = £480. ✓
6
A town's population was 12,000 in 2010. It increased by 15% between 2010 and 2015, then decreased by 10% between 2015 and 2020. (a) What was the population in 2015? (b) What was the population in 2020? (c) What was the overall percentage change from 2010 to 2020? Is this the same as 15% − 10% = 5%? Explain. 7 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) 2015: 12,000 × 1.15 = 13,800. (b) 2020: 13,800 × 0.90 = 12,420. (c) Overall change = 12,420 − 12,000 = 420. Percentage change = 420 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 3.5% increase. This is NOT the same as 5%. The 10% decrease was applied to 13,800 (a larger number), so it removed more people than the 15% increase added (in absolute terms, the 10% of 13,800 = 1,380 removed, vs 15% of 12,000 = 1,800 added). The percentages can never simply be added or subtracted because they are always calculated on different base values. This is the same principle as the "20% up then 20% down" exam trap from Day 1.

💡 ESL Tip: Biodiversity = bio (life) + diversity (variety). It means the variety of living organisms in a habitat. High biodiversity means many different species — which creates more connections in a food web and makes it more resilient (able to recover from a disturbance).

Section B
🟡 MediumSection B — Ecosystem stability and predicting effects of change.
🔍 Ecosystem Stability & Predicting Change
3
Consider this simplified food web:

Grass → Rabbits → Foxes → Eagles
Grass → Mice → Owls → Eagles
Mice → Foxes

A disease wipes out ALL the foxes. Predict what would happen to: (a) rabbit population, (b) mouse population, (c) eagle population, (d) grass population. Explain your reasoning for each. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Rabbit population — increases dramatically. Foxes are rabbits' main predator; without them, nothing controls rabbit numbers. (b) Mouse population — also increases. Foxes also eat mice; without this predation pressure, mouse numbers rise too. (c) Eagle population — initially increases, then may crash. More rabbits and mice mean more food for eagles short-term; however, the explosion of rabbits and mice will eventually overgraze the grass, reducing the food base for all herbivores, which then reduces eagle food supply. Long-term, eagle numbers could fall significantly. (d) Grass population — initially decreases sharply. More rabbits and mice = more grazing. If overgrazing occurs, grass cover reduces severely, potentially triggering collapse of the whole web. Overall: Removing foxes makes the web less stable — it demonstrates the "keystone species" concept: some species regulate populations throughout the whole web, and their removal has cascade effects far beyond their immediate prey.
4
The lesson says "biodiversity makes a food web more stable." Explain this using the idea of food web connections. What happens in a high-biodiversity web when one species goes extinct, compared to what happens in a low-biodiversity web? 4 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
In a high-biodiversity food web, each consumer typically has multiple food sources. If one species goes extinct, the consumer can switch to another prey species and survive — the web continues to function. The loss is absorbed because there are alternative connections. In a low-biodiversity food web, each consumer may depend on only one or two species. If one of those disappears, the consumer loses its entire food source and may also go extinct, triggering a cascade of further extinctions throughout the web. Stability comes from redundancy — having multiple "backup" connections at each level. A web with many species and many connections is like a net with many threads: losing a few threads leaves the net intact. A simple web with few connections is like a chain: break one link and the whole chain fails.
5
The Amazon rainforest is the most biodiverse habitat on Earth. Using what you know about food webs AND what the geography lesson (Day 1) says about the Amazon's physical conditions, explain WHY such high biodiversity is possible there. 4 marks
🔗 Cross-subject link: History/Geography Day 1 — Amazon region: rainfall, temperature, location.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The Amazon receives extremely high rainfall (over 2,000 mm/year in many areas) and enjoys stable warm temperatures (around 25–30°C) year-round, near the equator where sunlight is intense and consistent. These conditions are almost ideal for photosynthesis — plants can grow continuously rather than having dormant winter seasons. The result is an extraordinarily dense, multi-layered producer base: from ground-level mosses and ferns, through shrubs and mid-level trees, to a tall forest canopy. Each layer provides different food sources and niches. The richer and more varied the producer base, the greater the number and variety of primary consumers it can support; more varied primary consumers support more varied secondary and tertiary consumers. The Amazon's physical conditions essentially maximise the energy available at the base of the food web — and this multiplies upward through every trophic level to produce the greatest biodiversity on the planet.

🏆 Extended Science Analysis
6
"The energy pyramid explains everything about why ecosystems look the way they do." Evaluate this statement. Use the 10% energy rule, at least TWO habitats from the lesson, and the concepts of biodiversity and food chain length. Write at least 7 sentences. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The energy pyramid is an extremely powerful explanatory tool because the 10% rule generates observable predictions that match what we see in every ecosystem on Earth. Starting with the rule: only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next (the rest is lost as heat through respiration, movement, and waste). This immediately explains why ecosystems always have far more producers than primary consumers, far more primary consumers than secondary consumers, and why top predators are the rarest organisms in any food web. In the polar/tundra habitat, the food chain algae → krill → fish → seals → polar bears is very short (5 levels) and each level has relatively few individuals — there simply is not enough energy flowing from the scarce algae base to support longer, more complex chains or larger populations at the top. In the Amazon rainforest, the enormous productive base (continuous warm, wet conditions driving maximum photosynthesis) means the energy entering the base of the pyramid is so large that even after 90% losses at each level, enough energy remains to support extraordinary numbers and diversity of species at multiple levels. The energy pyramid also explains food chain length — most food chains are 4–5 links because by the 5th or 6th level, so little energy remains that it cannot support a viable population of top predators. The statement has strong validity: the pyramid explains population sizes, biodiversity, food chain length, and habitat differences. Its limitation is that it does not explain the specific species that fill each level — for that, climate, geography, and evolutionary history are also needed. But as a structural explanation of why ecosystems have the shape they do, the energy pyramid is foundational.

The lesson explains how rivers shape the land differently at each stage of their journey. Explain what a meander is, why meanders form, and where in a river's journey they are found. Then explain what a delta is and why it forms at the river's mouth. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
A meander is a large, sweeping bend or curve in a river's course. Meanders form in the middle and lower course of a river where the gradient (slope) becomes less steep, slowing the water. Slower water loses the energy to flow in a straight line and begins to follow the easiest available path, swinging from side to side. On the outside of each bend, the water moves slightly faster and erodes the bank; on the inside, it slows further and deposits sediment. These two forces — erosion outside, deposition inside — progressively exaggerate the curve into the large S-bends we call meanders. A delta forms at the river's mouth where it meets the sea or a lake. The water slows suddenly as it enters the still or tidal water body, losing virtually all remaining energy. The sediment it has been carrying — mud, sand, and silt — is deposited in a fan or triangular shape at the mouth. Over time this builds up into a delta of new land, sometimes splitting the river into many smaller channels (distributaries) as it navigates its own deposited material.

The lesson draws a cross-subject link: the world's earliest civilisations arose along major rivers. Using the comparison table, explain specifically how the physical geography of the Nile Valley made Egypt MORE stable than Mesopotamia (between the Tigris and Euphrates). 4 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 2 History — "Egypt's predictable Nile floods created stability; Mesopotamia's floods were chaotic, reflecting a more anxious religion."
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The Nile floods predictably every year in July, depositing a consistent layer of fertile black silt ("Black Land") on either side before receding — farmers could plan their entire agricultural calendar around this reliable rhythm. Beyond the flood zone, harsh desert ("Red Land") acted as a natural barrier, protecting Egypt from invasion on both sides. This combination of reliable food production and natural defence created the political stability that allowed the 3,000-year pharaonic civilisation to develop. The Tigris and Euphrates, by contrast, flood unpredictably — sometimes catastrophically and violently, destroying crops and settlements without warning. Mesopotamia also had no natural defensive barriers (open plains in all directions), making it vulnerable to repeated invasion from surrounding peoples. The lesson notes this is reflected in the two cultures' religions: Mesopotamian gods were anxious and hostile, reflecting an uncertain environment; Egyptian religion emphasised order (Ma'at) and the afterlife, reflecting a more predictable, stable world. Physical geography shaped not just farming but politics and even theology.

"Geography is destiny." This famous phrase suggests that where you live determines how your society develops. Using evidence from at least THREE subjects studied this week (Bible, History, Science, Maths), argue either FOR or AGAINST this statement. Write at least 7 sentences. 8 marks
🔗 You might connect: Egypt/Mesopotamia (History), ecosystems and habitats (Science), Joseph's story (Bible — did geography shape his destiny?)
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
FOR the statement: The evidence across multiple subjects this week strongly supports the idea that geography shapes destiny at the level of civilisations and ecosystems. In History, the contrast between Egypt and Mesopotamia is almost entirely explained by physical geography: the Nile's predictable floods created agricultural stability, desert borders created natural defence, and the result was 3,000 years of stable pharaonic civilisation. Mesopotamia's unpredictable floods and open plains led to repeated invasions and political instability — not because its people were less intelligent or capable, but because their physical environment created different conditions. In Science, the energy pyramid shows this even more clearly at the ecosystem level: the polar/tundra habitat has short food chains and sparse populations because the physical conditions (cold, dark, low rainfall) limit photosynthesis severely; the Amazon rainforest has the world's greatest biodiversity because physical conditions (warm, wet, sunny) maximise photosynthesis. The organisms have no choice in their geography — their food web is shaped by where they live. Even in Bible, Joseph's destiny is partly geographical: it was specifically because Egypt was a grain-producing empire (made possible by Nile geography) that it could store food for seven years and feed surrounding regions — without that geographical advantage, Joseph's administrative genius would have had no stage. Counter-argument: Joseph's story also demonstrates human agency within geography — his integrity, wisdom, and forgiveness transcended his physical circumstances. The statement "geography is destiny" is too deterministic if taken to mean geography is everything. It is perhaps better to say geography is the stage upon which human choices play out — powerfully shaping the options available but not eliminating human freedom, creativity, or character.
