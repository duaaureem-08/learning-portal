💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Independent clause: a group of words with subject + verb that makes complete sense alone. Example: "The river flooded." Dependent clause: has subject + verb but does NOT make sense alone — needs a main clause. Example: "Although it rained." FANBOYS: the 7 coordinating conjunctions: For And Nor But Or Yet So — used to join two independent clauses. Comma splice: joining two independent clauses with ONLY a comma — always an error. Example (wrong): "It rained, she got wet." Semicolon: joins two related independent clauses without a conjunction; stronger than a comma, weaker than a full stop. Parenthetical phrase: an interrupting phrase inserted between commas to add information. Example: "The teacher, exhausted from marking, sat down." Subordinating conjunction: introduces a dependent clause (because, although, when, unless, until, since, while, after, before, if). Complex sentence: one independent clause + one or more dependent clauses joined by a subordinating conjunction.

2
Read this passage carefully. Then answer all four questions below: 8 marks

"She had lived in the city for thirty years. She knew its streets the way a cartographer knows a map — not just the routes, but the hesitations, the wrong turns, the places where the pavement ends without warning. The city, she sometimes thought, was less a place than a habit. And habits, as everyone eventually discovers, are harder to break than walls."
a) Identify the sentence type of sentence 1 and explain what effect this creates.
b) The writer uses a dash in sentence 2. What is the dash doing, and how is this different from a comma or bracket at that point?
c) "The city, she sometimes thought, was less a place than a habit." Identify the parenthetical phrase and explain what it contributes to the sentence.
d) The final sentence is a complex sentence. Identify the independent and dependent clauses. What effect does placing "as everyone eventually discovers" in the middle create?
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Simple sentence — single IC, plain statement. Creates a matter-of-fact opening — no embellishment, just a plain fact that sets up the deeper exploration that follows. (b) The dash introduces an elaboration — it signals "let me expand on what I just said." A comma would feel too light for the extended list that follows; brackets would feel parenthetical and separate. The dash creates a sense of the narrator pausing to dig deeper, which matches the content (she knows the city's hidden details, not just the obvious). (c) Parenthetical phrase: "she sometimes thought" — placed between commas it softens the statement, making it a personal reflection rather than objective fact. It makes the narrator seem thoughtful and self-aware. (d) IC: "And habits...are harder to break than walls." DC (embedded): "as everyone eventually discovers" — placed in the middle between commas, this creates the effect of a parenthetical insight — as if the narrator pauses to remind the reader of a universal truth mid-sentence. It also slows the sentence down, making the final comparison ("harder to break than walls") land with more weight.

8
Explain in full sentences why the LCM method works for adding fractions. Your explanation must include: (a) what a denominator REPRESENTS, (b) why you cannot add fractions with different denominators directly, (c) why the LCM (rather than just any common multiple) is the most efficient choice. Write at least 5 sentences. 6 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 3 Maths — LCM using prime factorisation. You learned HOW to find the LCM; now explain WHY it matters for fractions.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) The denominator tells you how many equal parts the whole is divided into — it defines the SIZE of each piece. 1/4 means the whole is split into 4 equal pieces and you have one; 1/6 means split into 6 equal pieces. (b) You cannot add fractions with different denominators directly because the pieces are different sizes — adding 1/4 + 1/6 directly would be like adding one large slice of pizza and one small slice and calling them "2 slices" — they are not the same size, so the count is meaningless. You need to convert both to the same-sized pieces first. (c) The LCM is the smallest number that both denominators divide into evenly. Using the LCM keeps the numbers as small as possible, making simplification easier at the end. You COULD use a larger common multiple (e.g. the product of the two denominators) but this creates larger numbers to work with and more simplification required at the end. The LCM is the most efficient choice because it is the smallest valid common denominator.

🔗 Synthesis & Narration
3
Write a "journey narrative" — trace a piece of bread from the moment it enters your mouth to when the glucose from it reaches a muscle cell in your leg. Name every organ and process involved. Write in full sentences. Aim for at least 8 steps. 8 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Week 3 Science — circulatory system (heart + blood vessels), organ systems working together. Week 4 — digestion and absorption.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
1. Mouth: Teeth chew bread (mechanical); amylase in saliva begins breaking starch into simple sugars (chemical). 2. Oesophagus: Peristalsis pushes bolus of food to the stomach. 3. Stomach: HCl acid kills bacteria; churning continues mechanical digestion; pepsin (protease) begins protein breakdown. Bread's starch already partly converted; amylase is denatured in the acid. 4. Small intestine (duodenum): Pancreatic juice (amylase, protease, lipase) continues digestion. Bile from the liver (stored in gallbladder) is released to emulsify any fats. 5. Small intestine (ileum): Amylase completes starch → maltose → glucose. Glucose molecules are now small enough to pass through villi. 6. Villi: Glucose absorbed through the thin walls of the villi by active transport into blood capillaries inside the villi. 7. Bloodstream → portal vein → liver: Glucose travels to the liver, which regulates blood sugar levels, before entering general circulation. 8. Circulatory system → muscle cell: Heart pumps blood (now carrying glucose) through arteries → arterioles → capillaries in leg muscle. Glucose diffuses from blood into the muscle cell, where mitochondria use it in cellular respiration to produce ATP — energy for muscle contraction.
4
Thinking across Weeks 3 and 4: a person's cells need BOTH oxygen (from the respiratory system, Week 3) AND glucose (from the digestive system, Week 4) to produce energy via cellular respiration. Explain precisely how these TWO systems must coordinate to deliver both substances to every cell in the body, and what role the circulatory system plays in this coordination. 5 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
The respiratory system absorbs oxygen from inhaled air into the bloodstream through the alveoli in the lungs (large surface area, thin walls, rich blood supply). The digestive system absorbs glucose from digested food through the villi in the small intestine (same structural principles — large surface area, thin walls, rich blood supply). The circulatory system acts as the delivery network — the heart pumps blood carrying both oxygen (in red blood cells as oxyhaemoglobin) and glucose (dissolved in plasma) through arteries to capillaries in every tissue of the body. At the capillaries, oxygen and glucose diffuse into cells. Inside each cell, the mitochondria use both in cellular respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + ATP (energy). The carbon dioxide is then carried back by blood to the lungs for exhalation — completing the cycle. Without any one of these three systems, the other two cannot function.

🔬 Experimental Design & Scientific Essay
5
Design an experiment to investigate how TEMPERATURE affects the rate at which amylase breaks down starch. Include: (a) a clear hypothesis, (b) independent, dependent, and control variables, (c) method (step by step), (d) expected results, (e) how you would make it a fair test. 8 marks
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
(a) Hypothesis: Amylase will break down starch fastest at 37°C (body temperature). Below this it will be slower (less kinetic energy); above it the enzyme will denature and stop working. (b) IV: temperature (e.g. 10, 20, 37, 50, 70°C). DV: time for iodine to stop turning blue-black (starch fully broken down). CV: concentration of amylase, concentration of starch solution, volume of each, pH buffer, same person timing. (c) Method: Prepare starch solution and amylase solution. Set water baths at each target temperature. Place both solutions in the water bath for 5 min to equilibrate. Mix equal volumes (e.g. 2ml each) in a test tube. Every 30 seconds, remove a drop and test with iodine on a spotting tile. Record when iodine stays orange-brown (no starch left). Repeat 3 times at each temperature. (d) Expected results: slowest at 10°C; faster at 20°C; fastest at 37°C; slower again at 50°C as enzyme begins to denature; no reaction at 70°C (fully denatured). (e) Fair test: same concentrations, same volumes, same timing method, repeated 3 times for reliability.

Complete this cause-and-effect chain for the fall of the Western Roman Empire. For each cause, explain its effect on Rome's stability: 6 marks
CauseEffect on RomeSignificance (long-term result)
Military overextension
Political instability / 50+ emperors in 3rd century
Economic weakness / inflation
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Military overextension: Effect — the army was spread too thinly across thousands of miles of border; no region could be properly defended. Significance — Germanic tribes (Visigoths, Vandals) were able to penetrate and eventually conquer Roman territory. Political instability: Effect — constant civil wars as military commanders competed for the throne; resources diverted from defence to internal conflict. Significance — no long-term planning or stable leadership possible; empire fragmented into competing factions. Economic weakness: Effect — governments debased the currency (reduced silver content in coins) causing inflation; high taxes squeezed farmers and merchants; trade declined. Significance — the tax base collapsed, making it impossible to fund the army or maintain infrastructure.

Three civilisations, three legacies. For each one, identify their MOST IMPORTANT single contribution to the modern world and explain specifically how we still experience it today: 6 marks
🔗 Cross-week connection: Weeks 2, 3, 4 History — Mesopotamia, Ancient Greece, Roman Empire. The biggest cross-topic question of the course so far.
💡 Show Model Answer ▼
Mesopotamia — most important: Writing (cuneiform, c.3200 BC). Still experienced: every time we write, read, record data, or use a legal document, we are using a skill that originated in Mesopotamia — invented for trade records, now foundational to all human knowledge transfer. Ancient Greece — most important: Democracy and philosophical reasoning. Still experienced: most governments in the world claim democratic principles originating in Athens; courts use logic and evidence (Aristotle); universities teach systematic questioning (Socrates). Roman Empire — most important: Legal system and the concept of written public law. Still experienced: "innocent until proven guilty," right to representation, proportional punishment, codified civil and criminal law — all Roman legal innovations embedded in modern legal systems from the UK to the USA to Europe.
